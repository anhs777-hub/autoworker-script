Did you eat lunch at a company cafeteria today? That meal might have been from Ourhome. However, in the year this company posted its first loss since its founding, 77.6 billion won went out the door. A million people eat this company's food every day. At schools, companies, airports, and rest areas. Four people shared that money in the year the company suffered a loss. They are the founder's children. Usually, a company changes owners when business is bad. But this company changed owners while generating record-high revenue. Let's follow the sequence of where the money went. In the morning, school cafeteria doors open. Lights turn on in company cafeterias, and soup starts boiling in airport lounges and highway rest area kitchens. The total number of people served daily at all these locations is one million. It means the population of a decent-sized metropolitan city eats food prepared by this company every day . This company is Ourhome. They operate 800 locations nationwide, and nearly 9,900 employees work within them. From schools and businesses to airports, highway rest areas, hospitals, and nursing homes—everyone knows the name , but many don't realize the scale of the company. It didn't start out big. It was formed in 2000 when the food service division of LG Distribution split off. The person who founded it was Koo Ja-hak, the third son of Koo In-hwoi, who built the LG Group. He was a figure who had served as CEO of Lucky , president of GoldStar, and chairman of LG Construction; after working across key group affiliates, the last business he chose was catering. At the time, it was an industry that didn't stand out much. The company's first revenue after the spin-off was 212.5 billion won. In 20 years, that company grew into an entirely different entity. By 2019, revenue had climbed to nearly 1.9 trillion won. Operating profit, the money actually left in hand from business operations, reached 71.5 billion won. It was the best year in the 20 years since the company was founded. This company was private. It had never been listed on the stock market. It was literally a family-owned company where the founder and his children held all the shares. For a company responsible for the lunches of a million people, they were exceptionally quiet owners. But right after its best year, something that had never happened since its founding occurred. In the spring of 2020, schools closed their doors. Lights went out in school cafeterias across the country, and companies switched to remote work. School cafeterias that were crowded every morning and company cafeterias that had long lines at lunchtime locked their doors. The people who were supposed to come and eat had simply disappeared. Ourhome's revenue that year slumped to 1.6 trillion won. That is a drop of over 13 %from nearly 1.9 trillion won the previous year. And for the first time that year, their operating profit turned negative. An operating loss of 9.3 billion won. It was the first deficit in the 20 years since the company was founded. Yet, in that same year, the company distributed 77.6 billion won to its shareholders. The previous year. So, since the 2019 dividend was 45.6 billion won, it increased by 32 billion won. In a year when they posted a deficit, dividends actually grew by over 70%. You might not grasp how large this 77.6 billion won is, but if you divide it by the number of employees, it comes out to over 7.8 million won per person. A company that posted a deficit sent out enough money to give every single employee 7.8 million won. Looking at the numbers alone, it sounds strange, right? How can dividends increase when a company has posted a deficit? The answer is simple. Revenue and profit are different types of money, and dividends are also different from that profit. If a company takes money from the reserves it has built up, money can go out regardless of how the business is doing. Just as total revenue is not the same as total profit, the money earned that year is not necessarily all the money that goes out that year. Who did this 77.6 billion won go to? The majority of the shares were held by four people, the children of the founder, Koo Ja-hak. The eldest son, Koo Bon-sung, who was the CEO at the time, received 29.9 billion won. The youngest, Koo Ji-eun, received 16 billion won, the third, Koo Myung-jin, received 15.2 billion won, and the second daughter, Koo Mi-won, received 15 billion won. Yes. Just adding up their shares exceeds 76 billion won. Think of a business started by a family . A business started by their father was divided among his four children. If the business goes well, the four of them split the store's earnings, and even if it doesn't, they can still split the money accumulated in the reserves. That decision is a separate matter from whether to close the store or not. What actually happened at Ourhome was exactly this situation. Even in the year of the deficit, the money going out of the reserves did not decrease. It actually increased. A year earlier, in 2019, when the company was at its best, the net profit was 48 billion won. But the dividends paid out that year were 45.6 billion won. It was like paying out 95%of what they earned right back out. It was the company's habit not to hoard surplus cash, but to take out and spend almost everything they earned. If you look at who decided on these dividends, you will also understand why that became the company's last major dividend. The CEO of Ourhome who decided on the 77.6 billion won dividend was the eldest son , Koo Bon-sung. And in the autumn of that year when the dividends were paid out, an incident occurred in Gangnam, Seoul. It was September 5, 2020, at 12:35 PM, near Hakdong Intersection in Gangnam-gu. It was lunchtime. The CEO of a company that prepares lunch for a million people a day caused an accident on the road right in the middle of that lunch hour. Claiming he had been cut off, he overtook the vehicle in front, forced it to brake suddenly, and left the scene without addressing the accident. The victim had to chase him for about 10 minutes to avoid losing the car before the report was made. The charges were special property damage, special injury, and fleeing the scene. The first trial verdict for this case was delivered on June 3 of the following year. The court sentenced him to six months in prison, suspended for two years. Neither Koo Bon-sung nor the prosecution appealed. The sentence was finalized as it was. And two days later , on June 5, the Ourhome shareholders ' meeting was held. Two decisions were made simultaneously at this meeting. Koo Bon-sung was dismissed from his position as CEO, and the youngest daughter, Koo Ji-eun, took his place. Just two days after the verdict, the dismissal and the return took place in the same meeting room. For Koo Ji-eun, it was a return after five years. She joined Ourhome in 2004 and served as executive director and vice president, but left the company in 2016. During the time her older brother was CEO, she was excluded from the management of Ourhome. And she returned to the very seat her brother had vacated. She was the first female CEO to emerge from a company spun off from LG. That year, the company turned a profit. It recovered the very next year after recording a loss of 25.7 billion won in operating profit on sales of 1.7 trillion won. However, the dividend for that year was zero. A company that had shared 77.6 billion won the previous year didn't share a single cent in the year it turned a profit. Whether to share the money or not was ultimately a matter of who was sitting in that chair . But the stake held by the person pushed out of the company remained intact. The largest stake in this company, that is. For the largest shareholder ousted from management, there was only one card left. It was the dividends from that stake. At the shareholders' meeting on April 4, 2023, Koo Bon-sung raised a dividend proposal as a shareholder proposal. The amount was nearly 300 billion won. It was 11 times the net profit of the previous year and over 70 billion won more than the 224 billion won in cash assets the company held. It was essentially a proposal to take out more money than what was in the company safe. At the same meeting, the second daughter, Koo Mi-hyun, also separately proposed 45.6 billion won. The labor union stepped up to oppose it. Their position was that the real owners of the company are the workers. At the time, the labor union was demanding his performance-based bonus. While those who call themselves the owners of the company were out to divide 3 billion won, the share for those who actually did the work was left out. And at the shareholders ' meeting, both Koo Bon-sung and Koo Mi-won withdrew their own proposals. It wasn't rejected by a vote; they handled it themselves. In the end, the company's 3 billion won proposal was confirmed as it was. Let's look at how this company's dividends have shifted in order. 45.6 billion won in 2019, 77.6 billion won in 2020, 0 won in 2021 , and 3 billion won in both 2022 and 2023. In terms of dividend payout ratio , the final year fell to the 4%range. These are figures from the same company . The 77.6 billion won payout was not the company's original practice. It was an amount that changed depending on who sat in that seat and what decision they made. The 3 billion won requested in 2023 was more than six times the dividend of 2019, the year the company earned the most. Around the same time, the company sued Koo Bon-sung for embezzlement and breach of trust. It was a case handed over to the police in November 2021 after an internal audit. In September 2024, the first trial sentenced him to two years in prison, suspended for three years, and in August 2025, the appellate court actually increased the sentence to three years in prison, suspended for four years. Koo Bon-sung appealed, and the case is now being argued at the Supreme Court. Looking into the charges found guilty by the appellate court, scenes start to emerge one by one. First, it was the golf membership. He used the membership bought with company money 117 times in total, 97 of which were rounds played alone with his spouse. That means out of over 100 uses , more than 80%were for playing golf as a couple. And then there were the gift certificates. Circumstances were revealed showing that from July 2017 to 2021, he bought gift certificates with company money and used them personally, amounting to hundreds of millions of won. Just by looking at these two charges, one can guess how blurred the line between company money and personal funds was at this company. There is one more thing. In the very year the company saw a decline in revenue and incurred losses. It’s the fact that in 2020, a management performance bonus was paid selectively only to Koo Bon-sung himself. According to reports, the amount reached 5.4 billion won. The appellate court judged that not only did they divide 77.6 billion won in a year of deficit, but one person even took extra for himself through golf, gift certificates, and his own performance bonus. So, who finally won this fight? To start with the conclusion, no one won. On May 12, 2022 , the founder Koo Ja-hak passed away. It was his son. He was the man who married the third daughter of Lee Byung-chul, the founder of Samsung. With the two chaebol families becoming in-laws, Koo Ja-hak's children were born into such positions from the start . In the space left behind by the person who had led this company for 20 years, the siblings' fight continued. On April 17, 2024, it was the youngest sister, Koo Ji-eun's, turn. At the annual general meeting, the motion to reappoint her as an internal director was rejected. This was because the eldest sister, Koo Mi-hyun, sided with the eldest son, Koo Bon-sung. The person who turned the company profitable and achieved the best performance in its history with an operating profit of 94.3 billion won in 2023 was ousted by a single shareholders 'vote. It looked like the alliance that pushed out Koo Ji-eun had won. But exactly one year later, that alliance sold their entire stake. On May 15, 2025, Hanwha Hotels&Resorts purchased nearly 59%of the stake in Ourhome. The price was nearly 870 billion won. They bought over 13 million shares at 65,000 won per share. Converting this price to the entire company, the corporate value comes to nearly 1.5 trillion won. The stakes held by Koo Bon-sung, Koo Mi-hyun, and their related parties were all handed over. The shares held by the founder's eldest son and eldest daughter were all transferred to Hanwha. What remains are only the stakes of the two sisters who were already pushed out of the company. The owner of the company is now Hanwha, not the founding family. Although the stakes of Koo Ji-eun and Koo Myung-jin remained, they had already been forced out of management. They are left in a position where they can neither sell their shares nor influence the company. Hanwha is not leaving the company as it is. They have set a goal to achieve 5 trillion won in revenue and 300 billion won in operating profit by 2030. That figure is more than double the current revenue. To reach that goal, they are growing the size by even acquiring Shinsegae Food’s catering division, and Ourhome has been incorporated under a newly created holding company. The numbers are already moving. The quarterly revenue for 2026 was 669.4 billion won, with an operating profit of 18.2 billion won. Compared to the same period the previous year, the operating profit has nearly doubled. It wasn’t that the company went bankrupt and the owner changed. On the contrary, the company is still doing great business, but only the family that built it has disappeared from the scene . Let’s go back to that analogy. I said the shop their father started was divided into four parts. That shop is still doing great business today. It’s just that the faces behind the counter have completely changed. They didn't just share the profits from the four parts, but they handed over the entire shop itself to someone else. The fight between the siblings is also not over yet. They have been to court three times this year alone. The eldest sister, Koo Mi-eun, received court approval this March to place an attachment on a building in Gangnam, Seoul, owned by her two sisters. Conversely, the attachment the two sisters had placed on the eldest sister's home in Hannam-dong two years ago was canceled this April, and their subsequent appeal in July was also dismissed. The embezzlement and breach of trust case appealed by Koo Bon-sung is still pending at the Supreme Court. However, while this dispute was unfolding, what was happening to the actual industry this company occupied —the cafeteria business? During that time, the presence of cafeterias was quietly shrinking. The number of businesses registered as cafeterias this year dropped by over 6%compared to the previous year. When ranked by the speed of closures, the industry that saw the largest decline was study rooms . They decreased by nearly 10%. Next were small bars, pubs, and PC cafes, with cafeterias coming in right after them. Places where you once studied alone, stopped for a drink after work, and ate daily meals have all ended up on the same list of decline. This isn't just a one or two-year trend. From 2018 to 2022, over 20 out of every 100 cafeteria businesses shut their doors. The reason wasn't that the group catering industry was doing a particularly poor job. As companies increased remote and flexible work, the number of employees sitting at their desks every day decreased. The number of students also fell. Compared to the year 2000, the school-age population has shrunk by more than 4 million. Public institutions also reduced their cafeteria operations, causing those spaces to disappear as well. It is impossible for the number of kitchens to remain the same when there are fewer people to feed. This becomes even clearer when looking at the market as a whole. The group catering market grew from 3.3 trillion won in 2019 to 6 trillion won in 2023, but it has recently stalled at that level. After nearly doubling in size over a few years, it has come to a sudden halt. This market is also one where the top five companies account for 80%of the share. Within this stagnant 6 trillion won market, large companies have begun buying out each other's territory. Looking at revenue from catering alone, Samsung Welstory earned nearly 1.9 trillion won, and Ourhome earned about 1.2 trillion won. However, as Ourhome acquired Shinsegae Food's catering division, that figure jumped to the 1.7 trillion won range. It wasn't that new customers increased, but that they bought the spots where others were already cooking. In the end, a structure is solidifying where only two companies, Samsung Welstory and Ourhome , are growing in size. It’s not just Ourhome that created this trend. But the order of events is clear. While this company was fighting over dividends, the very ground occupied by the industry it belonged to was gradually narrowing. And even in that diminished space, at this very moment somewhere, this company’s meals are being prepared. This company did not change hands because it failed at making meals. They still cook just as well. Revenue has continued to rise, and there are still lines at the serving stations. Only one thing has changed. It is simply that the four siblings could never agree on where to put the money earned from those meals. Even at this very moment, the Ourhome serving lines are still running. It’s just that the name in front of them has changed from the founder's children to Hanwha. And behind the scenes, the siblings' attachment disputes and the rulings pending at the Supreme Court continue on. No matter how that fight ends, this company they built together will no longer return to either side. All that remains is the process of confirming each person's share.

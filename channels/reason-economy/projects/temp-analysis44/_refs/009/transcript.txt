Take a look at the rice cooker in your kitchen. If it’s not a Cuckoo, it’s likely this brand. Cuchen, the rice cooker powerhouse that once vied for the top spot. But do you know the current state of this company that cooks rice every day? They boasted of 500 billion won in sales, but the reality is they’ve been slashed in half to 150 billion won and turned to a deficit. Even in a year of losses, the owner family held a 5.4 billion won dividend party, and they were fined 1 billion won by the court for stealing technology from a partner company. The shocking downfall of Cuchen, which once aimed for number one. We reveal the full story. If you had to name the most important appliance on a Korean dining table, it would undoubtedly be the electric pressure rice cooker. The rice cooker, steaming hot meals three times a day, was more than just a kitchen tool—it was a symbol of warmth and abundance in the home. For a long time, one company reigned as the absolute leader in this market. It was the world of Cuckoo Electronics, famous for its red rice cookers. However, in the early 2010s, a rival emerged with fierce momentum, shaking the solid rice cooker kingdom. That was Cuchen. The roots of Cuchen date back to the 1970s with Samsin Industrial and Bubang Techron. Having honed its electric rice cooker and heating plate technology for decades, the company began to make a name for itself in 2006 by launching its premium home appliance brand, Lihom . Then, they made a decisive move. In February 2009, they suddenly acquired the home appliance division of Woongjin Cuchen, which was famous for its rice cooker business, from the Woongjin Group. With technology combined with brand power, the speed of growth was frightening. In 2013, they changed the company name entirely to Lihom Cuchen and started aggressive marketing. They hired top star Jang Dong-gun as an ad model, and later selected actor Song Joong-ki, who was causing a syndrome with the drama*Descendants of the Sun*, as their exclusive model. The moment a top star with a gentle smile appeared on screen and opened a rice cooker lid, the hearts of housewives were completely swayed. On top of this, technical innovation was added. Addressing consumer anxiety about heavy metals leaking from peeling inner pots, they released the premium "Iron Pot" lineup, the first in Korea to apply 100 %stainless steel inner pots. As word of mouth spread that it replicated the taste of traditional cast-iron pot rice , Cuchen's premium rice cooker emerged as a wedding essential and a must-have item for housewives. Performance soared explosively. Annual sales surpassed 270 billion won and quickly neared the 300 billion won mark. Their market share in the rice cooker industry easily surpassed the 30% "wall" and climbed up to the 40%range. It shook the foundations of the supposedly impregnable top-ranked Cuckoo, effectively reshaping the South Korean rice cooker market into a true two-way race. As their confidence soared, management began pouring out unabashedly rosy blueprints for the future. At a press conference for their premium new products in August 2016, the then-CEO took the microphone before a crowd of reporters and made a bold declaration. He vowed that through overwhelming technological prowess and a premium strategy, they would certainly achieve 500 billion won in annual revenue by 2020. To anyone watching, Cuchen looked like the hero who would go beyond first place and build a new empire in the Korean kitchen market. But just as everyone was intoxicated by the glamorous figure of 500 billion won and raising their glasses, an unstoppable crack was quietly spreading from the very depths of this rice cooker master's foundation . There is a trap that successful companies most commonly fall into. It is the arrogance of believing that because our products are selling well now, the world will forever go our way. The tragedy that befell Cuchen began at that very point. In August 2015, Lihom Cuchen carried out a spin-off, splitting the company into two. It reorganized its governance structure into a holding company, Bubang, which would rule over the operating company, Cuchen, that manufactured and sold rice cookers. It was a move to solidify the owning family's control. However, while they were rearranging the company's signboards, a massive, unimaginable tectonic shift was occurring on the dining tables of the Korean people. First and foremost, single-person households increased explosively. Young people and office workers living alone no longer washed rice and started the cooker every weekend. Food delivery apps became a daily routine, and shelves at large marts and convenience stores began to fill with instant rice that tasted freshly cooked after just two minutes in the microwave. The act of cooking rice with a rice cooker itself was gradually disappearing from our daily lives. If the number of people looking to buy rice cookers was shrinking, how should the company have responded? Naturally, they should have released small, single-person rice cookers at reasonable prices or expanded their territory into new home appliances like water purifiers or induction cooktops. But the answer the Cuchen management provided pointed in the completely opposite direction. It was an obsession with ultra-high-end premiums. They raised the price tags on rice cookers to 500,000 and 600,000 won , and eventually began churning out models that approached 800,000 won. No matter how good the technology was, there weren't many consumers willing to dump a significant portion of their monthly salary into a single rice cooker just to cook a meal. While consumers 'wallets were frozen shut, Cuchen was playing a luxury brand game all by itself in some distant universe. To make matters worse, the overseas markets they had relied on vanished like a mirage. Cuchen had high hopes for demand from Chinese tourists visiting Korea and for their expansion into the Chinese market. However, when the THAAD dispute erupted, a ban on Korean culture was imposed, and the flow of Chinese tourists that once filled duty-free shops suddenly came to a halt. As local Chinese appliance makers flooded the market with affordable and decent rice cookers, their strategy to penetrate the local market ended in complete failure. But the truly fatal blow did not come from the outside; it erupted from within Cuchen itself. In February 2022, alarming news began to spread among consumers using Cuchen's premium rice cookers. Reports emerged one after another of dangerous safety incidents, such as high-pressure steam leaking from the seals while cooking, and in some cases, the lid even popping open during operation. For a pressure cooker , having the lid open on its own is an extremely dangerous defect that can lead to burns or explosions. Ultimately , following discussions with the Korean Agency for Technology and Standards, a humiliating order was issued for a full , free-of-charge recall of all six models of their ambitious "121" electric pressure cooker line. It wasn't just the rice cooker lids that blew open. The trust of consumers who had paid a premium, believing in a brand known for its rice cookers, flew out the window as well. And the price they paid was truly devastating. The market’s judgment on a brand that has lost its credibility was cold and harsh . Cuchen’s market share, which once neared 40%and threatened the top spot, began to drain away like an ebbing tide . As consumers turned back to Cuckoo instead of Cuchen, the market quickly solidified into an 80%monopoly for Cuckoo. Cuchen’s market share was slashed in half to around 20%, falling to a miserable second place that made the term "two-way race" meaningless. As their market share crumbled, the company’s physical scale shrank miserably as well. Cuchen’s annual revenue, which had surpassed 270 billion won in its prime and was on the verge of breaking the 300 billion won mark, slid endlessly downward. By 2024 and 2025, their annual revenue had sunk to the 150 billion won range. The company’s overall size had been cut exactly in half. What was even more serious was their profitability. With raw material and parts costs skyrocketing while sales volumes dwindled, the more they ran their factories, the more losses accumulated. Entering the 2020s, Cuchen fell into a quagmire of chronic deficits, posting net losses ranging from hundreds of millions to billions of won. But what was truly bizarre was the company’s reaction at this moment of crisis. Instead of facing reality soberly and focusing on painful restructuring and R &D, Cuchen once again tried to deceive the public with absurd numerical manipulation. At the completion ceremony of the new plant in Cheonan, Chungnam, in August 2022, then-CEO Park Jae-soon gathered the press to announce another grand vision. It was called the "Challenge 2025: 55 Vision." He boldly promised that by 2025, they would achieve 500 billion won in revenue and a 5%operating profit margin to become one of the top five kitchen appliance brands. The promise made back in 2016 to reach 500 billion won by 2020 had already ended in total failure, yet they threw out the same 500 billion won empty promise again while the company was in ruins. And when the results were finally revealed in 2025, the report card was truly miserable. Cuchen’s actual revenue amounted to only around 150 billion won. It reached barely 30% of the 500 billion won promised by management, and the company was still struggling in a deep quagmire of deficits. As its subsidiary Cuchen collapsed, the situation for its holding company, Bubang, also became dire. The stock price of Bubang, once a respectable listed company, plummeted to the 1,000-won range, falling to the status of a penny stock. As rumors of potential delisting spread due to the falling stock price, Bubang had to resort to desperate measures, buying back and canceling its own shares to artificially prop up the price. To anyone looking, Cuchen was a patient suffering from a severe illness. Yet, even as the company screamed in pain from halved revenue and chronic deficits, there was a suspicious path of money that never stopped flowing out . How would a rational company act when it falls into a deficit? Naturally, it should tighten its belt, cut executive salaries, and save every penny of remaining cash to invest in new product development and quality improvement. That is the only way to overcome the crisis and recover. However, the clock at Cuchen was ticking according to an entirely different set of rules. To understand this, we must look into the ownership structure behind Cuchen. 100% of the shares in Cuchen, the company that sells rice cookers, are held by its holding company, Bubang. And Bubang’s shares are overwhelmingly controlled by founder Chairman Lee Dong-gun’s family and the special related companies they dominate. It is a perfect vertical governance structure for the owner family. And the flow of funds that occurred within this structure was shocking. In the very same year that Cuchen recorded an operating deficit and a net loss due to poor rice cooker sales, an unbelievable dividend record appeared in the audit report. Cuchen paid a massive cash dividend to its parent company, Bubang, amounting to approximately 5.4 billion won. The company wasn't making money and was suffering losses, so where did this huge sum of 5.4 billion won come from to end up in the parent company’s bank account? It was a payout of dividends by emptying the retained earnings the company had stored away during its prime. The parent company swept up the seed money needed to sustain the business and the lifeline funds meant for future R&D, even while the company was in the red. Then why did the parent company, Bubang , drain 5.4 billion won from its subsidiary, Cuchen, even emptying its coffers? Bubang itself was in a dire situation, with its profitability plummeting and stock price hitting rock bottom due to the poor performance of its subsidiaries. To cover its own deficits and forcibly defend its financial statements, it cut open the belly of its cash cow, Cuchen, to get its hands on the cash. The money that flowed in went toward stock buybacks and dividends, which defended the stock price and maintained the owner family’s control. While the subsidiary was struggling with recalls due to defective parts and lacked the funds to develop new products, the parent company emptied its pockets for a 5.4 billion won cash party. This was the very essence of the controversy over the owner family’s private gains , which the market and the media have pointed out. With corporate growth pushed to the sidelines, the national brand Cuchen was being used like a disposable item to fuel the owner family’s governance structure and fill the parent company’s pockets. But the true tragedy did not end there. The actions the management took against suppliers to cut costs from the outside , while they were obsessed with extracting money from the inside, were truly in the realm of criminal activity that went too far. Let’s turn the clock back to 2018. At the time, Cuchen was suffering from a double whammy of stagnant sales and worsening profitability, and was desperate to cut manufacturing costs by any means necessary. The most essential component that controls rice taste and heat in an electric pressure rice cooker is the printed circuit board, or PCB assembly. It is the most precise core component that acts as the brain of the rice cooker. There was one small-to-medium-sized supplier that had faithfully supplied this key component to Cuchen for many years. Having silently endured soaring labor costs and part prices, this supplier hit its limit and earnestly requested that Cuchen raise the supply unit price through proper procedures. For any normal large corporation, it is common sense to audit the supplier’s cost details and put heads together to find a mutually beneficial solution. However , the response from Cuchen’s management was beyond imagination. With no intention of raising the unit price, Cuchen decided to kick out this supplier entirely and replace them with another company. But the problem was that the new competitor did not have the technical expertise to immediately manufacture the precise boards perfectly suited for Cuchen’s cookers . This is where Cuchen crossed a line that should never have been crossed. They secretly stole the core technical data—the circuit diagrams and blueprints that their supplier had painstakingly developed and submitted to get their parts approved. Without any consent or even notice to the original developer, they handed that valuable technical data over to a competing firm four separate times. They stole the technology entirely and gave it to another company, instructing them to copy the blueprints and manufacture the parts at a lower cost. It was a shameless act of technical theft, committed without even a single written request for technical data, which is strictly mandated by the Subcontracting Act. The small supplier, facing the theft of its technology and the loss of its business by the conglomerate it once trusted, tearfully reported this ugly crime to the Fair Trade Commission. The Fair Trade Commission launched an investigation, and their judgment was firm. They defined it as a major illegal act that fundamentally destroyed the order of subcontracting and threatened the survival of small businesses, imposing a heavy fine of 922 million won on the corporation and referring the firm and its leaders to the prosecution. Cuchen refused to accept this and hired a large law firm to file an administrative lawsuit, but even the Supreme Court dismissed the case, siding with the Fair Trade Commission and handing them a crushing defeat. And finally, the judiciary delivered a cold , criminal penalty. In December 2025, the Seoul Central District Court’s criminal division sentenced the Cuchen corporation to a record-high fine of 1 billion won for violating the Subcontracting Act. The two employees who spearheaded the crime were also each fined 20 million won. The court’s reasoning for the verdict was clear. Against the victim's wishes, they systematically and intentionally misappropriated the technical data and provided it to a third party. Acts where a large corporation abuses its superior position to steal the hard-earned technology of a small business are crimes that shake the foundations of a market economy, making severe punishment inevitable. The final destination for a company that boasted fantasies of 500 billion won to beat the rice cooker industry leader was the pathetic seat of an offender with a 1 billion won criminal fine for technical theft. Ten years of Cuchen stealing technology and holding a 5.4 billion won dividend feast. Then, where exactly was their rival, Cuckoo, whom they so desperately wanted to overtake? Ten years later, the report cards of the two companies were so sharply divided that it left observers speechless. While Cuchen was trapped in the rice cooker business, pointlessly issuing empty promises of 500 billion won, the industry leader Cuckoo was preparing for a completely different future. They poured the money earned from rice cookers into research, development, and business diversification. Cuckoo Homesys was independently grown into a rental business covering water purifiers, air purifiers, and bidets, further expanding its territory to food waste disposers and large kitchen appliances. As a result, Cuckoo Homesys surpassed 1.0572 trillion won in annual revenue, proudly joining the 1 trillion won club. Combining the 900 billion won in revenue from Cuckoo Holdings, the appliance holding company that makes rice cookers, the Cuckoo Group has risen to become a massive appliance empire with annual sales approaching 2 trillion won. On the other hand, where did Cuchen stand? The loud claims of reaching 500 billion won in sales are nowhere to be found, and annual revenue has shriveled to the 150 billion won range. The gap in scale between the two companies, once called the two major pillars of the industry, has now widened to more than tenfold. No one calls Cuchen a rival to Cuckoo anymore. Let us return to the kitchen table we saw at the beginning. Cuchen, the familiar rice cooker in our home that would steam and prepare warm rice every morning. On the lid of that rice cooker , the pride of a prestigious brand that once dominated Korean kitchens was shining brightly. However, the truth hidden beneath that flashy silver and gold exterior was incredibly shabby and miserable. While ignoring changes in consumer food culture, they were obsessed with 800,000 won "luxury" marketing and threatened consumer safety with defects that caused lids to pop open. While the company suffered from deficits, the parent company pocketed a massive 5.4 billion won in dividends to fill the owner family's private coffers, and they stole core blueprints from small and medium-sized suppliers who had developed technologies through blood and sweat, all under the pretext of cutting parts costs. A company that ignored the essence of its core business— technological innovation and quality— and clung only to the owner family's private gain and empty promises. In the place where their greed-filled feast has ended, the heaviest and coldest bills have arrived: a 1 billion won criminal fine and the icy cold shoulder of consumers.

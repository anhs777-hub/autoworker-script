In November 2017, the last ship left a shipyard in Tongyeong, Gyeongnam. There was no next ship. For the nine years that followed, not a single completed ship came out of this shipyard. It was a place that once ranked eighth in the world. It was put up for sale three times but never sold, and it nearly faced liquidation. Samsung Heavy Industries has now entrusted four oil tankers to that very shipyard. This is the first time a domestic shipbuilder has outsourced the entire construction of ships to a non-affiliate domestic shipyard. By the end of this year, the shipyard will cut its first steel plate in nine years. Why did Samsung entrust the construction of these ships to a Tongyeong shipyard that hadn't built a single vessel in nine years? Although the news of this contract passed by quietly in a single line, that line holds the history of a shipyard that built ships on land without a dock, and the nine years of those who stayed there until the end. When you think of Tongyeong, you usually think of Gangguan Port or the Jungang Market first. This shipyard is not located there. It is located in the Anjeong National Industrial Complex in Gwangdo-myeon, north of the city, where a vast flat area stretches along the Anjeong Bay. The former name of this shipyard was Sungdong Shipbuilding& Marine Engineering. Its current name is HSG Sungdong Shipbuilding. This company did not start out by building ships from the beginning. It began as a company that supplied ship segments to Samsung Heavy Industries in Geoje in the early 2000s. Large ships are not built all at once. They are made by creating house-sized steel pieces separately and joining them together. In the industry, these pieces are called "blocks." A company that used to make blocks for Samsung ships. Please make sure to remember this starting point. It will come up again later. In 2005, the company decided to build entire ships instead of just pieces. The first order came from a Greek shipping company. However, this shipyard lacked one thing that every other major shipyard had. It was a dock . Usually, large ships are built inside a massive pit where the ground along the coast is excavated and drained of water. Once finished, the pit is filled with seawater to float the ship. This pit is called a dock. From the beginning, Sungdong built ships on bare ground without such a pit. Then how did they send the completed ships out to sea? They grabbed the tail end of the ship and dragged it directly toward the ocean. It's like making a toy boat in the yard and dragging it to a pond instead of building it inside a bathtub . According to the company, this was the world's first large-scale shipyard to use the dock-less, land-based construction method as its primary process from the start. In 2006, the shipyard's first ship was moved from the land to the sea. According to company records, all ships launched after that were also built on land. To give you an idea of how vast this land is, it’s 1.2 million square meters. That’s an area large enough to fit about 168 soccer fields. Instead of a water-filled dock, this vast expanse of bare ground was the shipyard’s workshop. And just a few years after opening, this land-based shipyard made it onto the global shipyard rankings. In 2007, Sungdong Shipbuilding became the world’s 8 th-largest shipyard. It was the very place where the first ship was pulled from the land into the sea. This ranking was based on the order backlog. The order backlog refers to the amount of work—ships that have been ordered but not yet built—that is piled up. In restaurant terms, it’s like a stack of pending orders in front of the kitchen. It means their stack was the eighth thickest in the entire world. A young shipyard in Tongyeong that once supplied ship parts to Samsung made it into the global top ten just years after opening. They also set records with their dockless construction method. According to company records, the largest oil tanker ever built on land at the time, a 170,000-ton vessel, was launched here in 2008. The company states that in that same year, they launched the first container ship built on land. Container ships had to be built faster and sleeker than oil tankers, so they were considered difficult to build on land. They flipped the conventional wisdom that you couldn't build large ships without a dry dock. When this shipyard was in its prime, Sungdong directly employed over 2,000 workers. If you include the subcontracted workers, nearly 10,000 people were commuting to this seaside. The number of partner firms receiving work for blocks and parts reached 700. The livelihoods of countless small companies were tied to this single shipyard. It wasn't just Sungdong that was located on the Tongyeong coast. Shipyards like 21st Century Shipbuilding and Samho Shipbuilding were lined up along the sea. Tongyeong was originally a city that lived by fishing and welcoming tourists. Alongside that long-standing lifestyle, noisy shipyards had sprung up side by side. Those who lived in Tongyeong or Geoje back then will likely remember. The sight of streets filled with people in work uniforms every morning and lines of commuter buses heading into the industrial complex. It was a time when the whole neighborhood would be stirred with excitement every time a ship set out to sea. However, the financial crisis that began across the ocean in the fall of 2008 washed up on this shore. Once the financial crisis hit, new ship orders from all over the world were cut off. Shipowners who had promised to buy ships closed their wallets all at once. But for Sungdong, something even more painful than the loss of orders was waiting. Why on earth does the exchange rate shake a shipyard? Ship prices are paid in dollars. And it's not paid all at once, but spread out over the years it takes to build the ship. If the exchange rate drops during that time, you get less won for the same amount of dollars, resulting in a loss. So, Sungdong took out financial products to prepare for when the exchange rate dropped. It was a kind of shield they carried to protect themselves. But in 2008, the exchange rate didn't fall. On the contrary, it skyrocketed. The product meant to hedge against a falling exchange rate began pouring out losses as it jumped. The shield they were holding turned into a sword that cut the company down. In 2009, the losses from these financial products reached 1.4 trillion won. This money wasn't lost because they built the ships wrong. It was money lost by being cut by the shield meant to hedge the exchange rate. The books collapsed rapidly. The company turned to a deficit, and then its capital began to erode. Eventually, on April 8, 2010, Sungdong Shipbuilding&Marine Engineering signed a voluntary agreement with creditors. The banks that lent the money gathered and promised to manage the company together to keep it alive. That same year, the founder stepped away from management. After that, the company's largest shareholder became the state-run Export-Import Bank of Korea. The money the creditors put into the shipyard, including new loans and debt-to-equity swaps, exceeds 4 trillion won. They saw it as worth saving that much. But if you only remember this period as a story of collapse, you're missing something. Even during the eight years under creditor control, the shipyard never stopped building ships. During that time, they built and delivered 206 ships to shipowners. In terms of value, that's 11.6 trillion won worth. Even on top of a mountain of debt. Even on a pile of debt, the people of Tongyeong went out to the yard every morning to build ships on land and launch them into the sea. But with each ship they finished and delivered, the work for the next one was gradually shrinking. In September 2015, as work was dwindling, a hand reached out from Geoje. It was Samsung Heavy Industries. The main creditor bank, the Export-Import Bank, asked Samsung to take over management, but Samsung refused. Instead, they agreed to help with sales, procurement, and production , and share some of their own workload. For two years, about 20 Samsung engineers were dispatched to the Tongyeong yard. The connection from back when they supplied blocks was renewed like this. But that didn't fill the increasingly empty production schedule. In the first half of 2017, they only secured five new ships. That was far short of their annual goal of 15 ships. The creditors 'calculation was like this. Once the ships currently under construction leave by November, there won't be any work left to touch. The calculations proved to be exactly right. In November 2017, the last ship was handed over to the owner and left the yard. Usually, the steel plates for the next ship would have been laid down in that spot immediately. But this time . Nothing was laid down. Only the sea breeze passed over the ground where welding sparks used to fly. No one at the yard knew then how many years that vacancy would last. In March 2018, the government and creditors decided on court receivership. The application was filed at the Changwon District Court that month, and rehabilitation began in April. The judgment handed down by the Export-Import Bank was cold. It concluded that shutting down and liquidating the assets was worth more than keeping it running. They believed that even with massive layoffs and new funding, it could not survive alone. The backlog of orders that once made it the world's 8th largest was now zero. The government designated Tongyeong, Geoje, and Goseong as crisis zones with volatile employment. It was no longer just one shipyard’s problem, but a crisis shared by the entire coastal region. Even so, the court sought a way to save it. To save it, there had to be someone willing to buy the shipyard. In October 2018, the first sale announcement was made. The vast yard and facilities used to move ships on land were put up for sale all at once. Not a single company stepped forward to buy it. The second bidding also ended empty-handed. In June 2019, at least one company submitted a bid for the third attempt. But they failed to provide proof that they could pay the acquisition price. The sale price known in the industry was in the 300 billion won range. Some bidders couldn't even prove they had the 10 percent deposit for that price. Even at that price, the shipyard failed to find a new owner. When the third sale fell through, the rehabilitation plan was changed. A condition was added that if it didn't sell by the end of that year, it would go into liquidation. Liquidation means dissolving the company entirely and selling off the remaining assets. The clock that could erase the name that built over 260 ships began to tick. The ones who heard the ticking of that clock most clearly were the employees. The company, which once had over 2,000 workers, had only 770 left by then. Among them, 650 were taking turns on unpaid leave. It was a time of dividing up the remaining work and waiting at home for one's turn. This meant more than eight out of ten employees were waiting at home to return to work. There were times when their salaries didn't come in at all. That’s how the seasons changed several times. Still, there were those who stayed, keeping their names on the roster and holding on. Other mid-sized shipyards that once lined the coast of Tongyeong closed their doors one by one during the shipbuilding downturn. After some time, the site of one closed shipyard was transformed into a space for startups, education, and culture. In the spring of 2022, the Tongyeong International Triennale exhibition was even held in the old research building. The place where ships were once built had found an entirely new purpose. The time given to Seongdong Shipyard was until December of that year. And in the autumn of 2019, the fourth door opened. Six companies submitted their names for the fourth formal bid. The Changwon District Court determined that, this time, there was a qualified buyer. In November 2019, HSG Heavy Industries was selected as the preferred bidder. It was a company based in Changwon that made shipbuilding equipment, having started as a partner to Samsung Heavy Industries. Such a company had stepped forward to take over an entire shipyard . The final contract was signed on December 31, 2019. It was the very last day of the year the liquidation deadline had been set. The contract price was 200 billion won. Compared to the over 4 trillion won poured in by creditors, it met its new owner at about one-twentieth of that value. The new owner also announced a policy to continue employing even those who had been on unpaid leave. It was the most welcome news for those who had been waiting. On April 1, 2020, the dormant shipyard began to hum with activity again. A little over a month later, the court's rehabilitation process ended, and that spring, the company took on the new name, HSG Seongdong Shipbuilding. The Export-Import Bank of Korea evaluated it as a successful case of handing over a small-to-medium shipyard. That was how the long period of court receivership finally came to an end. However, the workers could not all return at once. About 190 people returned first that year, while the rest had to wait until the following year. Then, on the morning of January 4 , 2021, the sun rose over the Tongyeong sea in the biting cold. About 260 workers who had been waiting at home until the end put on their work clothes and entered the yard. It was their first day back in 28 months since starting their unpaid leave. Since they had started their leave in the autumn, it was a journey back in the heart of their third winter. The returning workers received retraining and were assigned to new positions. For that day , the work clothes they had pulled out after such a long time must have felt more reassuring than any other outfit. The shipyard, which once sat on land that rose to 8th in the world, faltered due to exchange rate losses, and after sending off its last ship, it failed to sell three times. It met a new owner at the threshold of liquidation, and the people returned after 28 months. But in front of the people who returned, there were no ships to build. The new owner's plan had been clear from the start. It was to build pieces of ships, or blocks , rather than finished vessels for the time being. That was the right order for a shipyard that had once stopped to get back to work. The vast yard began to be used again as a workshop for building ship blocks and offshore structures. In July 2021, a bigger job came in. It was a semi-subcontract with Samsung Heavy Industries. A " semi-vessel" is literally half of a ship. It’s a massive block roughly the size of a ship cut in half at the waist. It was for the hull of a large container ship ordered by a Taiwanese shipping company. It was a contract worth about 250 billion won for 15 ships until 2024. As work increased, so did the number of people. At that point , the number of people working in the yard grew back to about 1,800. The once quiet yard was filled with the sound of people again. In May 2023, an order arrived from across the sea. A Danish offshore wind power company commissioned the foundation structures for a wind farm off the coast of Taiwan . They are legs that support the offshore windmill underwater so it doesn't fall. How big are those legs, you ask? They are over 80 meters tall. Electricity generated by the wind at this farm is reportedly destined for Taiwan's semiconductor plants. The legs for this wind farm were all installed in the Taiwan sea by the summer of 2025 . Hands that used to build ships had successfully created legs to stand in the sea. From the year it commissioned the semi-vessels, Samsung Heavy Industries stood as a guarantor for the company. They put their own name on the line, believing they would complete the work to the end. However, that didn’t mean their finances immediately recovered. In 2024, they generated 318.8 billion won in revenue but still posted an operating loss of 22.3 billion won. They were still in the red . Nevertheless, in the spring of the following year, they secured over 210 billion won in funding again. Banks and investors decided to continue providing money to this shipyard. But no matter how well they built blocks and wind turbine legs, there was one wall this shipyard couldn't overcome alone. That wall was a single sheet of paper. It is called a refund guarantee. A shipowner who orders a vessel does not wait until it is finished to pay the full price. They send payments in installments while the ship is being built. But no shipowner sends large sums of money without a guarantee. A promise that if the shipyard fails to complete the ship , the bank will refund the money received in advance. Money only moves when that guarantee is in place. It’s easy to understand if you compare it to building a house. To receive a construction down payment, you must present a guarantee with a bank stamp, but banks rarely stamp it for a company that has once collapsed. Tongyeong's Sungdong Shipbuilding was in exactly that position. As a company that had gone through court receivership, the banks' doors were high, and it was difficult to receive ship orders directly from shipowners. They could make parts of a ship, but they couldn't take on an entire ship from start to finish. Samsung Heavy Industries in Geoje opened that door. Samsung had its own reasons, too. In the first half of 2025, the dock utilization rate at the Geoje shipyard was 116%. This means there weren't enough slots to build ships, so they were operating even more densely than capacity allowed. They also had a backlog of work spanning several years. They had to prioritize expensive ships like gas carriers, and some oil tankers had been outsourced to Chinese shipyards. In July of that year , Samsung Heavy Industries and HSG Sungdong Shipbuilding signed a business agreement. Then two ships in November, and two more in December. Contracts were signed in sequence to build a total of four oil tankers in Tongyeong. The method is as follows. Samsung Heavy Industries is the party that contracts with the Greek shipowner. HSG Sungdong Shipbuilding in Tongyeong builds the ships, and the completed vessels are delivered to the owner under Samsung's name. By standing face-to-face with the shipowner, Samsung effectively carries the burden of the guarantee. This is the first instance of a domestic shipbuilder entrusting an entire ship to a non-affiliate domestic shipyard. Dozens of Samsung employees overseeing quality and processes are already staying at the Tongyeong yard, inspecting and monitoring welding directly. Over 20 years ago, this company was a supplier making pieces for Samsung ships. Now, that same Samsung is entrusting them with an entire ship. I would like to call this period "the 20 years from pieces to the whole." So, why did Samsung choose Tongyeong in particular? The answer lies, first of all, in this ship. The ship they've taken on this time is a Suezmax-class oil tanker. This is the name for the largest oil tanker that can pass through Egypt's Suez Canal while fully loaded. This ship is a 158,000-ton class vessel and is scheduled to be delivered to a Greek shipowner in 2028. Ships of this class are usually around 270 meters in length . They are generally longer than the 63 Building in Yeouido lying on its side. As it happens, oil tankers were the specialty of the old Sungdong Shipbuilding. Among them, the Suezmax was a vessel type where this shipyard showed particular strength. It means that although it's the first ship they've built in nine years, it's not an unfamiliar one. The land and facilities were still there. I'm talking about the large land-based yards and major equipment mentioned earlier. On top of that, they have been working in sync with Samsung over the past few years by building ship halves and blocks. From Samsung's perspective, it's a shipyard whose way of working they already know. Proximity played a part as well. Geoje and Tongyeong are neighbors, so it's convenient for Samsung employees to travel back and forth to oversee things. The people were still there, too. Although the workforce shrank after the shipyard changed owners and the building of complete ships ceased, it is known that the engineers who created the method of moving land-built ships to the sea remain. Industry experts view this shipyard as a second production line that provides breathing room for larger shipyards overflowing with work. Tongyeong was the partner Samsung chose within the country. And at the end of this year, the yard is scheduled to cut the first steel plate for the 269th ship in the company's ledger. The moment that thick steel plate is cut is the very first step in shipbuilding. Following the company's established method, this ship will also be built on land rather than in a dock. If so, the scene I mentioned at the beginning could appear again in 2028. Like at the end of last year, when a finished ship was slowly pulled from the land into the sea. The people who returned after 28 months endured for several years by building half-ships and wind turbine components. And by working with Samsung , they overcame the wall of financial guarantees they couldn't clear on their own, taking on an entire ship. However, there are reasons why it is still too early to celebrate. For one, this order was not received under the Tongyeong shipyard's own name. The contract with the shipowner was signed by Samsung, and the threshold for receiving guarantees alone remains high. The last known financial records also showed a deficit. The city, too, has been left with empty spaces. Tongyeong's population has decreased from about 140,000 in 2012 to the 110,000 range recently. Even when the lights at the shipyard turned back on, the people who had left did not return immediately. Still, there were places that reacted first. In the spring of 2020, before the shipyard resumed operations, shops began reopening near the site. Stories of merchants who had newly opened their doors appeared on local broadcasts. These challenges are closer to things not yet done rather than things that cannot be done. If they successfully deliver the four ships on the promised dates, that record can become the foundation for securing the next guarantee. Tongyeong is not the only place walking this path. The Gunsan shipyard, which had been closed, also stood on the path to building ships again after nine years. There are three things to watch for in the future. Whether the first plate is cut as scheduled at the end of this year, whether the four ships are delivered on time, and someday, the first order this shipyard receives under its own name. That yard, which sent off its last ship in November 2017, is scheduled to cut its first steel plate again by the end of this year. When that day comes, it will mean they have waited for nearly 3,300 days. In 2028, the name on that ship will be Samsung, but the place where it is built is the land of Tongyeong, protected by people who waited 28 months to return. Even a shipyard that has fallen once can stand again to build ships if there are people who hold on.

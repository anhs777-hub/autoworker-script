Let’s turn back the clock to 1951, right in the middle of the Korean War. A man named Son Do-ik establishes a coal briquette company. The name is Musan Briquette. In Chinese characters, it means "lush mountain." It was a time when the mountains were becoming barren due to the war and logging for firewood . In 1967, this family established Wangpyo Briquette in Busan. Those who lived in Busan will be familiar with that name. After that, winters in Korea were spent with coal briquettes. And they were also spent with coal gas poisoning. In 1976 alone, over 1,000 people lost their lives to coal gas. Those who lived through those days will remember checking the bedroom doors every winter night. In those times, in March 1978, the briquette family set up a separate boiler company. KyungDong Machinery. The following summer, a factory was built in Pyeongtaek, Gyeonggi-do, and that autumn, the first boiler was released; it wasn't a coal briquette boiler, but an oil boiler. Its name was Corona. In 1987, they introduced the first domestic oil boiler that used a fan to push exhaust gases outside the house. However, by that time, a Japanese name had already entered the Korean boiler market. It was a company called Rinnai. Founded in 1920 in Nagoya, Japan, by two men, the name was formed by taking one character each from the founders 'surnames, Hayashi and Naito. It is said they started by making oil stoves after seeing the blue flame of an imported one. It is a company over 100 years old , having been established 58 years before Kyungdong Machinery. In 1974, Rinnai partnered with a Korean firm to establish Rinnai Korea. Starting the following year, they sold gas stoves, initially importing parts from Japan for assembly. By the late 1980s, they even began introducing gas boilers. Today, Rinnai Korea is a company almost entirely owned by its Japanese headquarters. It means that from the very beginning, a Korean home heating company had to face a Japanese rival. The trump card Kyungdong played came unexpectedly not from Japan, but from Europe. In February 1988, they signed a technical partnership with the Dutch boiler company Nefit, and four months later in June, they released the condensing gas boiler. Even today, the company promotes this as the first of its kind in Asia. What is condensing? When you open a rice cooker lid, you see water droplets beaded on the inside . When water vapor turns into droplets, it releases the heat it was holding. Ordinary boilers just send the hot steam from burning gas out through the chimney. Condensing boilers pass that steam through a heat exchanger once more to turn it into droplets, using the released heat to warm the water again. It is a machine that captures the heat that was being wasted through the chimney. What is truly amazing comes next. In December 1990, Kyungdong exported that very condensing heat exchanger back to Nefit. Just two years and ten months after learning the technology, they sold the core component back to their mentor. I couldn't help but laugh at this part. It was as if the student had built the heart of the mentor’s own boiler. In 2014, an economic newspaper wrote that most competitors did not acquire this technology until the mid-2000s. That is more than 15 years behind Kyungdong. In the same article, a securities analyst evaluated it like this: Although they invested in condensing early and didn't make as much profit as others, that steadfast investment in technology was finally beginning to shine. In the meantime, the company became a name known to the entire nation. In March 1991, Kyungdong Machinery changed its name to Kyungdong Boiler and began airing "filial piety" commercials. It was the work of director Yoon Seok-tae, who also created the "Choco Pie: Affection" campaign. Many of you probably felt a lump in your throat thinking of your parents back home while watching that ad, right? Around 30 years later, in 2020, even a clothing brand called SPAO collaborated with KyungDong Navien to parody the line as "We should get Warmtech for your father," proving it remains a national catchphrase. In August of that same year, 1991, KyungDong released "Symphony," a wall-mounted instant gas boiler that heats water on demand instead of storing it in a tank. This "on-demand" heating technology would later become their secret weapon overseas. In 1992, they became the first in the industry to export gas boilers to China, winning the first "1 Million Dollar Export Tower," and the following year, they became the first boiler company to list on the stock market. In 1995, they even established a corporation in Beijing, China. In 1998, even as the country was reeling from the IMF crisis, they launched a new Korean-style condensing boiler. This boiler won the Presidential Award for Quality Management that year, and the company introduced the world's first integrated heat exchanger made of corrosion-resistant stainless steel. The company's leadership changed in 1998. Son Yeon-ho, the founder's son who joined in 1979 at age 28, became chairman at 49, and the founder passed away the following year. The family boiler business, which began with coal briquettes, started looking beyond the seas under the son's generation. The domestic market battle in Korea continued for a long time after that. In 2012, KyungDong began using the slogan "The National Representative Boiler." This even led to their competitor, Kiturami, reporting them to the Fair Trade Commission for false advertising. In January of the following year, the Fair Trade Commission concluded there was no case. In 2013, KyungDong’s annual sales surpassed both Kiturami and Rinnai Korea. Though, that did include their sales from U.S. exports. At the end of 2020, Lotte Aluminum folded its home boiler business after 38 years. Today, the domestic boiler market is often called a duopoly between KyungDong and Kiturami. The one that captures the wasted heat is the one that wins. This is the story of the home front in Korea . But the place where this company truly bet its future was not Korea. It was a country where Japanese companies from across the ocean had already opened the doors. In January 2002, KyungDong became the first in the Korean boiler industry to export condensing gas boilers to the U.S. In June of that year, they also became the first in the industry to obtain U.S. product safety certification. In December 2024, the U.S. Department of Energy finalized a rule requiring that only high-efficiency gas tankless water heaters be sold. Starting in December 2029, gas tankless water heaters made or imported in the U.S. essentially had to be high-efficiency products that capture even the heat from the exhaust. Throughout the rule-making process, Rinnai was opposed to it. They claimed they would be directly harmed by this regulation. The Department of Energy's answer was this. All 12 companies that make gas tankless water heaters are already producing high-efficiency products. The extra cost of switching to high-efficiency is recovered in fuel savings within about nine years. The reason was recorded exactly as is in government documents. Rinnai was the No. 1 leader in old-fashioned water heaters, with six out of ten units, but in the high-efficiency segment, they held one out of five. They also argued that since the U.S. water heater market is dominated by three big companies, the change in regulations would put them at a disadvantage as a smaller competitor. It was essentially the king of old-style water heaters claiming to be a small company when looking at the entire U.S. market. There is an even more surprising part. Rinnai began U.S. production in 2018 in a rented building in Griffin, Georgia, and in 2021, they even built a new factory costing nearly 70 million dollars. That facility was a factory dedicated solely to producing conventional tankless water heaters. High-efficiency products were still being shipped in from Japan. They had built a massive factory on U.S. soil, yet tailored it to old-fashioned technology. I got goosebumps reading that part. In the same market, one side made heat-recovery technology their hallmark, while the other geared their U.S. plant toward outdated methods. Rinnai did not back down. They joined a lawsuit filed by several U.S. states and gas industry groups in a federal appeals court, arguing that the regulation effectively banned conventional tankless water heaters. The president of Rinnai's U.S. subsidiary claimed the rule threatened hundreds of manufacturing jobs, even suggesting that their Georgia plant might have to close. However, the market had already been tilting in one direction even before the government drafted the regulation. The Department of Energy estimated that two out of three gas tankless water heaters sold in the U.S. in 2024 were already high-efficiency, and projected that would reach seven-eighths by 2030, even without the mandate. Customers were already shifting toward the side that captures even the waste heat. The regulation was eventually overturned by the U.S. Congress in 2025 and rendered null.

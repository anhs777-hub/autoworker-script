There is another Jeju beneath the ground of Jeju Island. This is not just a figure of speech. It is an actual research finding announced by the Jeju Special Self-Governing Province World Heritage Headquarters on July 20, 2026. In Sang-dong, Cheogi-foot, there is a volcanic cone called Deodae Oreum. It is a small hill that rises only 48 meters from the surrounding ground. Most tourists pass by it without even knowing its name. A hole was drilled at a spot about 200 meters northeast of that oreum. It was a hole dug to develop and monitor groundwater. From the surface down to 109 meters, the typical Jeju soil was found. There were several layers of lava with soil sandwiched in between. However, once it went past 109 meters, the rock changed. The very trachyte that forms Deodae Oreum started to appear. And that rock did not end there. At 150 meters, 200 meters, and 300 meters, the same rock kept coming up. We drilled down to 330 meters, but the bottom is still not in sight. If we calculate it, it looks like this. From the top of the oreum to the confirmed point underground, there is a single rock mass of at least 360 meters. At 360 meters, a size familiar to the people of Jeju will come to mind . It is Sanbangsan Mountain. The height of Sanbangsan Mountain rising from the surrounding ground is about 345 meters. So, what we thought was just a small hill actually had an entire volcano the size of Sanbangsan hidden underneath it . The research team stated the following. They confirmed for the first time that a massive volcanic body is hidden beneath Deodae Oreum, which appears as a small oreum on the surface . The Jeju we know is the Jeju above the ground. It is an island with Hallasan Mountain, oreums, stone walls, and the sea. But there is another Jeju beneath it. Though invisible to the eye , Jeju is still breathing even now. Water flows, underground paths stretch out, and deep down, something hot is still cooling. Today, we will descend into that hidden Jeju. It is an island much older, much larger, and much more amazing than the Jeju on the surface. First, let me ask you a question. How could a volcano the size of Sanbangsan be buried underground? The answer is simple. Lava flowing from the surroundings gradually covered the volcano bit by bit. For hundreds of thousands of years, lava flowed and flowed, covering the mountain's waist, then its shoulders, and finally leaving only the top of its head. The small hill we call Deodae Oreum was, in fact, the crown of a giant. There is one more surprising fact. The point where this massive rock mass begins is 132 meters below sea level. Since the base of Sanbangsan Mountain is at about 50 meters above sea level, it means it grew from a place more than 180 meters deeper than that. The research team is even considering the possibility that this volcano formed around the same time as Sanbangsan Mountain, or perhaps even earlier. The exact age will be known once the dating results are out at the end of this year. Depending on the results, the history of Jeju's volcanic eruptions may be rewritten. Then, did this happen only at Daeo-eum? No, it didn't. Jeju's underground is filled with such secrets. And it wasn't scholars, but people searching for water who first brought these secrets to light. Until the 1960s, water was the biggest concern on Jeju Island. It rained much more than on the mainland, but there was no water to use. Streams would flow briefly when it rained, then dry up quickly, so people drew water from coastal springs and carried it in jars. Then, in 1961, they succeeded in drilling deep into the ground to obtain groundwater in Susan-ri, Seop-eureup. It was a project that used well-drilling techniques brought from Hawaii. After that, they drilled holes in 59 locations across Jeju until 1967. Only 18 of those places were successful . It wasn't because there was no water. It was because the water was much deeper than expected. As equipment improved and they could drill deeper, Jeju's water situation changed completely. The water supply rate rose to 99.9%, and now there are thousands of groundwater extraction wells in Jeju . An important fact arises here. Every time a hole is drilled, a rock core comes up from inside. Thousands of holes mean thousands of underground records. The holes drilled to find water eventually drew a map of Jeju's underground. When you unfold that map, an island completely different from what we knew appears. This is what we learned in school. That Hallasan Mountain erupted, and the lava flowed down to create the island. That is not wrong. But it is not even half of the story. By drilling into the ground, we found that Jeju is an island stacked in three or four layers. The top layer is the basalt we know. That black stone with holes everywhere. The very stone used to build stone walls and mark field boundaries. However, this basalt layer is not as thick as one might think. Near the coast, even if you dig just a little, a completely different layer appears. It is a hard, grayish sedimentary layer. And sea shells come out from within it. Don't you find it strange that shells are found inside the ground of a volcanic island? There is a place where you can see this layer with your own eyes. It is the western coastal cliff near Cheonjiyeon Falls in Seogwipo. A stratum about 40m thick is exposed on a cliff that is about 50m high. Traces of marine life such as shells, coral, shark teeth, and whale bones are embedded inside. The name of this stratum is the Seogwipo Formation. It was designated as a Natural Monument in 1968. The nation recognized its value early on. So, is the Seogwipo Formation just hardened sea mud? That is not the case. If you look at its composition, a large part is volcanic ejecta, debris spewed out during a volcanic eruption. And it was a volcano that erupted in a very special way. What happens when hot magma meets seawater? Imagine pouring molten iron straight from a furnace into cold water . The water turns into steam in an instant, expanding explosively, and the magma shatters into pieces, scattering in all directions. Volcanoes that erupt violently upon meeting water are called hydrovolcanoes. If you're curious about what a hydrovolcano looks like, you don't need to go far. Jeju's symbol, Seongsan Ilchulbong, is exactly that— a hydrovolcano. Volcanic ash piled up as it erupted in the shallow sea, creating a massive, bowl-shaped crater. Recent measurements suggest it formed about 6,000 years ago. Suwolbong, at the western tip of Jeju, is the same type. The volcanic ash layers are so clearly exposed on the coastal cliffs of Suwolbong that world volcanologists call it a textbook of volcanology. Researchers from foreign universities even travel to Jeju specifically just to see these cliffs. Songaksan, Yongmeori Coast, and Udo’s Seomeori Oreum are all volcanoes that erupted in the sea. This is where the story of the underground connects. The hydrovolcanoes we see on land now are the lucky ones that survived. Far more hydrovolcanoes were covered by subsequent lava flows and vanished underground. Researchers who have cross-referenced drill records believe there are over 100 such buried hydrovolcanoes beneath Jeju. Dozens or hundreds of volcanoes like Seongsan Ilchulbong were stacked layer upon layer to form the Seogwipo Formation. In other words, the Seogwipo Formation is a graveyard of volcanoes and the true skeleton of Jeju Island. At this point, the sequence we first learned is reversed. We used to think Hallasan formed first and its lava filled the sea. In reality, it was the opposite. Long before Hallasan existed, the site of present-day Jeju was a shallow sea. Beneath that sea, small volcanoes began to erupt here and there. They erupted, were eroded by waves, then erupted again, and were eroded again. As volcanic ash piled up, the seafloor gradually rose. Eventually, land emerged above sea level, and from that point on, lava began to flow over land rather than into the sea. Hallasan appears in the final scene of this entire process. I will show you with numbers just how late it appeared. In March of this year, the World Heritage Office released the ages of 90 out of Jeju's 360 or so oreums. The oldest oreum identified so far is Gunsan Oreum in Andeok-myeon. Volcanic activity began there about 917,000 years ago. Sanbangsan was formed about 785,000 years ago. Byeoldobong Peak, where Jeju citizens go for walks, is about 435,000 years old, and Dodobong Peak is about 410,000 years old. Then what about Hallasan Mountain? Among the volcanic cones in Hallasan National Park with confirmed ages, Janggumok was formed about 133,000 years ago, and the Yeongsil area about 71,000 years ago. And the Baengnokdam crater at the summit was found to have erupted about 16,000 years ago. Compared to the volcanic cones of Gunsan, it is more than 50 times younger. It is the highest point on Jeju, but in terms of age, it is essentially the youngest. The story of Baengnokdam is worth looking into a bit more. In 2017, Dr. Ahn Ung-san of the World Headquarters and Dr. Hong Se-seon of the Korea Institute of Geoscience and Mineral Resources uncovered the birth process of Baengnokdam. The western wall of Baengnokdam was formed after about 37,000 years ago, when sticky lava slowly pushed up and hardened into a rounded dome shape. The eastern side was created later by a new eruption of magma. Baengnokdam was not a crater formed by a single explosion, but a work created by two overlapping volcanic activities at different times. As measurements have continued to accumulate since then, the timing of the summit eruption has been revised down to 6,000 years ago. The more we measure, the younger the peak of Hallasan Mountain becomes. For reference, there is a place that has long been introduced as the oldest volcano on Jeju. It is Yongmeori Coast. The figure of 1.2 million years ago has been written on tourist information boards for a long time. However, there is now a strong consensus among scholars that this number needs to be re-evaluated. It is pointed out that the samples and methods used for the measurement were not suitable for a young volcano like Jeju. Looking at the border with Sanbangsan, some researchers believe that because the tuff of Yongmeori sits on top of the Sanbangsan rock, it was actually formed later than Sanbangsan. We must be honest about these parts. The age of Jeju is still being rewritten even now. And the people rewriting it are none other than us researchers. Now, let's go further down beneath the Seogwipo Formation. Once you break through the layer of volcanic ash, another layer appears. This time, it is not stone. It is fine sand and mud that have not yet hardened into rock. Its existence became known after drilling in 1989, and researchers call this layer the U-formation. There are no traces of volcanic activity in this mud. This is because it is not something spewed out by a volcano, but sediment that calmly settled on the seafloor over a long period. This is evidence that before Jeju's first volcano erupted, this place was a quiet seafloor. In some thick areas, it reaches several hundred meters. Beneath the volcano lies a memory of the sea, completely unrelated to the volcano, that is just as thick. What is underneath that? The answer to this very question is what makes Jeju a special island in the world. Below the U-formation, there is granite. And this granite is a rock from a completely different era than Jeju's volcanic rocks. Fragments of granite are embedded within the volcanic ash at Byeoldobong in Jeju City. When the volcano erupted, it tore up and spewed out rocks from deep underground. When we dated these granite fragments, they turned out to be about 170 million years old. This was the Jurassic period , when dinosaurs roamed the Earth. It is the same type of rock that forms the mountains on the Korean Peninsula. Why is this so significant? Hawaii is an island often compared to Jeju. That is because both are volcanic islands. But if you look underground, they are completely different islands. Hawaii is an island that rose from the oceanic crust, thousands of meters deep in the sea. Jeju is an island where volcanoes sit atop the continental crust connected to the Korean Peninsula. The sea between Jeju and the mainland is barely over 100 meters deep at its deepest point. During the Ice Age, when sea levels were lower, Jeju was actually part of the land connected to the mainland. So, Jeju is not an island floating alone in the sea, but an island carried on the back of the massive Korean Peninsula. Deep underground, Jeju and the Korean Peninsula are connected as one. Let’s summarize. On the very top is the basalt we walk on every day. Below that are layers of volcanic debris from hundreds of underwater eruptions. Deeper still are mud layers from the sea before the volcanoes arrived. At the very bottom lies the granite from the dinosaur era, supporting it all. If the Jeju above ground is a story less than a million years old, the Jeju underground is a story over 100 million years old. But this hidden Jeju is not just sitting there stacked up. I mentioned earlier that it is breathing. The first breath comes from the paths opened underground. May 2005, Woljeong-ri, Gujwa-eup, Jeju City. While workers were digging to replace a utility pole, the ground suddenly collapsed before them. A massive cave stretched out underneath. It was Yongcheon Cave. The exploration team that entered was left speechless. This was because white limestone decorations were blooming inside the black lava cave. Stalactites hung from the ceiling , and stalagmites rose from the floor. It was a rare cave globally, where features of both lava and limestone caves existed in one space. The secret lay above the cave. The ground above was covered in sand mixed with crushed seashells, and rainwater dissolved the lime, seeping it into the cave ceiling. It is a masterpiece crafted together by the sand created by the sea and the cave created by the volcano. The surprises did not end there. At the end of the cave, there was a lake. Initially, we estimated it to be about 200 meters, but detailed surveys revealed it reached 800 meters in length. The deepest parts were over 10 meters. And inside the cave, pottery from the 8 th-century Unified Silla period was discovered. Charcoal and animal bones were also found together. This means someone had been entering this cave long before that. Afterward, the entrance was blocked, leaving the cave forgotten for over a thousand years, only to reappear due to a utility pole construction project. Yongcheon Cave is not alone. Caves created by lava flowing from Geomun Oreum form a continuous line, and this cluster became the core of the UNESCO World Natural Heritage site in 2007. Among them, the most famous, Manjanggul Cave, is over 7 kilometers long. How are these caves formed? Flowing lava cools and hardens starting from the surface that touches the air. However, the inside remains hot and continues to flow downward. When the lava drains out , only the hardened shell remains, leaving behind an empty tunnel. It is similar to thinking about scooping out the inside of frozen ice cream. The age of these caves has also changed. For a long time, Manjanggul Cave was known to have been formed hundreds of thousands of years ago. The results of measurements taken by the World Heritage headquarters over four years using three different methods showed it was 8,000 to 9,000 years old. This result was announced in 2020. The figure once cited as 200,000 or 300,000 years has been drastically reduced. It means the upper part of Jeju Island was formed much more recently than we thought. The second is hidden water. There are 143 large and small streams on Jeju Island. Yet, there are almost no streams where water flows on a regular basis. Where did all that rain go? The answer lies in the basalt beneath our feet. Those porous stones are where lava gas once escaped. Combined with cracks formed as lava cooled and underground caves, the Jeju land absorbs water like a giant sponge. Over 40%of the rainfall seeps into the ground, which is several times higher compared to the mainland. What happens to the water that enters the ground? It might seem like it would just flow out to the sea, but that is not the case. This is where the Seogwipo Formation we mentioned earlier comes back into play. The Seogwipo Formation, made of compacted volcanic ash, allows much less water to pass through than basalt. So, in many areas, it acts as a floor that supports the water coming down from above. Furthermore, freshwater is lighter than seawater. This creates a structure under the island where freshwater floats on top of seawater in a lens shape. Ancient volcanoes buried underground are now, hundreds of thousands of years later, supporting the water supply for the people of Jeju . How long does the water stay in that water basin? Jeju Samdasoo, which you buy at convenience stores, provides the answer. In 2025, Jeju Special Self-Governing Province Development Co. and Professor Yoon Sung-taek's research team at Korea University analyzed seven years of rainwater and groundwater data and published their findings in the international journal*Journal of Hydrology*. The rainwater that becomes Samdasoo falls at an altitude of over 1,450 meters on Mt. Halla. It takes an average of 31 years for that water to travel through the ground to reach the aquifer. This is much longer than the previously known 18 years. It means the bottle of water you drink today is rain that fell high on Mt. Halla over 30 years ago. It is water that has been filtered and refined as it passed through basalt cracks for that long duration. Where does the water eventually emerge? Along the coast surrounding the island. The spring water of Jeju. In other words, more than 90%of the springs that gush from the ground are concentrated in coastal areas below 200 meters above sea level. This is the reason why Jeju’s villages are situated in a circle along the coastline. Ancient Jeju people didn’t know the underground map, but they built their homes where the water welled up. It is as if they drew a map of the visible Jeju based on the invisible Jeju. And the third breath. The deepest breath remains. Everything discussed so far was the world within a few hundred meters, where drills can reach. How can we see much deeper than that? The method is seismic waves. When an earthquake occurs on the other side of the globe, the vibrations travel through the Earth to Jeju. These vibrations move fast through solid rock and slow down when passing through hot or molten areas. Just as a hospital ultrasound reveals the inside of a body , we can look beneath the island using seismic waves. Research teams from Seoul National University, Korea University, and Pukyong National University analyzed seismic observation data from across Jeju. And they reported astonishing results in an international academic journal. There was a zone about 55km deep beneath the center of Jeju where seismic waves slowed down. The research team suggested the possibility that magma exists there. That zone was estimated to split as it rises, extending to depths of 10km to 5km under the east and west of the island. 55km is the distance from Seoul past Suwon. It means that at that distance directly underground, there is something that has not yet cooled down. Evidence that Jeju is truly a living volcano also remains on the surface. In geology, a volcano is generally considered active if it has erupted within the last 20,000 years. Jeju has several such records. An eruption approximately 5,000 years ago has been confirmed at Seonheul-ri. Songaksan erupted about 3,700 years ago. That is a time when Neolithic people could have witnessed the scene firsthand. In fact, human footprint fossils were even discovered in the Hamori formation near Songaksan. And at Doro-reum, about 4km west of Mt. Halla, even younger eruption traces were found. The World Heritage Office cross-verified findings using three dating methods, initially announcing them as about 2,600 years old, and later suggesting about 2,000 years in revised data. Jeju is also listed as an active volcano in the Smithsonian Institution's global volcano catalog. There were also people who left behind records. The History of Goryeo contains a record from the 5th year of King Mokjong, or the year 1002. It describes four holes opening on Tamnal Mountain, with red water gushing out for five days before stopping, after which the water all turned into stone. Five years later, in 1007, there was a report that a miraculous mountain had risen from the middle of the Tamna Sea. The imperial court sent the royal scholar Jeon Gong-ji to investigate. Records remain stating that when the mountain first rose, the clouds and fog turned pitch black and the ground shook like thunder. Our ancestors witnessed a volcanic eruption a thousand years ago and documented it as a national record. There are not many places in the world that possess such ancient records of volcanic observations. However, the exact location of the volcano in the record has not yet been identified. It was once speculated to be Biyangdo, but research has shown that Biyangdo is a much older volcano. The record clearly exists, but its protagonist is still hidden somewhere underground. Should we be worried then? The researchers ' answer is clear. No, we should not. Dr. Ahn Woong-san of the World Heritage Office explained it this way. He said that even if a pot contains hot liquid, it will not boil over if you do not keep the fire burning. The presence of magma underground and an imminent eruption are two completely different stories. Instead, our research team has made preparations so as not to miss the island's breathing. Since 2020, five broadband seismometers have been installed in high-altitude areas of Hallasan, between 1,450 m and 1,920 m above sea level. Across 79 locations in mountainous regions, we have installed receivers to detect even the slightest tremors in the ground. Even at this very moment, Hallasan is under our observation, like a patient with a stethoscope pressed against them. Let us return to the beginning of the story . The massive volcano beneath Daejeong was not discovered by accident. The Hallasan Research Department of the World Heritage Office is working to create a new geological map of all of Jeju from 2025 to 2028. This year alone , we are photographing and recording rock columns from 13 deep boreholes across the island and extracting samples from each layer for analysis. Because we did not overlook even a single hole drilled to find groundwater , we were able to discover a volcano the size of Sanbangsan. The research team also left us with these words. They said that rock columns obtained through drilling are invaluable assets that, once lost, are difficult to recover. When you think about it, it is truly amazing. Jeju is already an island recognized by the entire world. Jeju was the first in the world to earn all three UNESCO natural science titles : Biosphere Reserve in 2002, World Natural Heritage in 2007, and Global Geopark in 2010. However, those evaluations were mostly based on what we see above ground. The Jeju beneath the surface has only just begun to be understood. Out of over 360 volcanic cones, the age of only about 90 has been identified so far. The World Heritage Office has set a goal to uncover the age of nearly 200 by 2028. The age of Daeborum will be revealed at the end of this year. That number might change the chronology of Jeju’s volcanoes once again. Next time you visit Jeju, I hope you take a look at what lies beneath your feet. Beneath those black stones sleep hundreds of volcanoes that erupted from the sea. Underneath that is the mud from the sea before the volcanoes, and at the very bottom lies the granite that has supported the Korean Peninsula since the age of dinosaurs. Through those layers, 30-year-old rainwater flows slowly, and 55km down, the island's heart has not yet cooled. If you climb Seongsan Ilchulbong, try to imagine its crater as the face of countless siblings buried underground. And when you pass by a small, nameless volcanic cone, it might be worth stopping for a moment. Because beneath that hill, there might be a giant as large as Sanbangsan Mountain. Below Jeju Island, there is another Jeju. It is an older, larger, and still-breathing Jeju. And the ones uncovering this Jeju layer by layer are not people from other countries, but our own researchers. The day the age of Daeborum is revealed.

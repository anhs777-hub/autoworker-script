The soju brand Chamisul, released by the bankrupt company Jinro, captured half of the national soju market in just two years. It was a truly bizarre scene: customers were lining up even as the company was collapsing. Samlip Hopang, which guards the steamers in convenience stores every winter, met the same fate. Hopang is still sold today, but the company that first made it collapsed because it couldn't cover a promissory note worth a mere 300 million won. You’re all thinking of the IMF crisis, right? You're right. But the ledgers of these companies already had holes in them before the IMF arrived. Let’s follow where the money earned by the number-one players leaked, and how their names survived even after the companies faltered. For buried records of success and failure, subscribe to Such Economic and hit the like button to keep watching. Now, let's look at Jinro. In April 1997, the top soju maker, Jinro, stood before the banks. A returned promissory note. In other words, they couldn't honor their promise to pay on the specified date. Instead of just letting the company fail, the banks in Seoul created a strange new mechanism. It was called the "Bankruptcy Prevention Agreement." It was an agreement among banks to briefly delay bankruptcy proceedings even for companies that couldn't pay their debts. Jinro was the very first company to which this agreement was applied. Yet, even at that moment, Jinro was the best-selling soju company in South Korea. After dethroning the then-number-one, Samhak, in the winter of 1970, the top spot on the soju sales chart was always Jinro’s. Every restaurant refrigerator was stocked with the green bottles featuring the toad logo, and people reached for Jinro at street food stalls after work and on holiday tables. Whether it was a company dinner for the father's generation or a homecoming party for the son's, the first glass was always Jinro. The company was so big—and simultaneously so precarious—that banks had to create a new system just to keep it afloat. The soju was clearly selling. The money was clearly coming in, too. Yet, the company's safe was empty. Outside the books, it was a perfectly fine top-tier company, but inside the books, it was a company barely surviving from one maturity date to the next. So, why on earth was Jinro’s safe empty? Jinro is a soju company that was born in 1924 in Yonggang, South Pyongan Province, under the toad trademark. One line is enough for the origin story. The year their fate shifted was 1988. In the year the founder's son, Jang Jin-ho, took office as chairman, Jinro had nine subsidiaries. This number ballooned to 24 within a few years. Everything from beer and food to construction and retail. They kept adding businesses that had nothing to do with soju. Among them, their biggest gamble was beer. In 1994, Jinro teamed up with a US beer company to launch Cass. A company that had sold only soju for decades jumped straight into a battlefield dominated by established beer companies. Building a brewery and breaking into the market required funds on a completely different level than what they earned from selling soju. The problem was that a significant portion of this expansion was built on other people's money—in other words, debt. Furthermore, the affiliates acted as guarantors for each other's debts. They signed documents pledging that if one affiliate couldn't pay, another would cover the debt instead. This created a chain reaction where if one place wavered, the entire group would shake together. Simply put, it’s like this. It’s like taking out bank loans to open a barbecue restaurant, a cafe, and a karaoke bar all at once, relying solely on one successful soup restaurant. The soup restaurant's earnings remain the same, but the interest to be paid increases with the number of shops. On top of that, if the shops guarantee each other's debts, the day the karaoke bar collapses, the soup restaurant's safe is emptied too. There is a date you need to look at here. The day our country applied for an IMF bailout was November 21, 1997. Of course, the IMF crisis is a factor. It is true that the foreign exchange crisis brought down countless companies. However, Jinro stood before the banks in April of that year, seven months before the country gave up. The answer becomes clear when you look at the actual numbers. The total amount that the soju company Jinro invested in, lent to, and guaranteed for its affiliates exceeded a staggering 2 trillion won. The beer and construction companies that actually received that money had already wiped out their own capital. No matter how much soju they sold, the money coming in flowed right into those bottomless pits. But within that collapsing company, a number in the exact opposite direction began to emerge. In September 1997, after the grace period ended, Jinro eventually defaulted and filed for reconciliation. What is reconciliation? Simply put, it is not court receivership where the court takes full control, but a process of rescheduling debt repayments with creditors. The following year, in 1998, the creditors accepted this plan, and on October 19 of that same year, the company launched a new soju product. That was "Chamisul." Within two years, this soju captured half of the national soju market. Customers sought out the new soju from the bankrupt company without reservation. Why was that? They either didn't know the company's situation, or they didn't care even if they did. As long as the liquor in the bottle was good, that was enough. However, the debt returned on its due date, regardless of the taste of the liquor. What was the first thing Jinro let go of? It was the dream of beer. The brewery that made Cass was handed over to their competitor, OB Beer. But even after selling the factory, the debt was not fully resolved. Ultimately , Goldman Sachs, the US financial firm that held their debt, filed for Jinro's court receivership, and Chairman Jang Jin-ho was arrested on charges including accounting fraud. Then, in 2005, Jinro appeared on the market as a candidate for acquisition. Even though it was a bankrupt company, 14 firms jumped into the bidding war. The final winner was Hite Brewery. The acquisition price was a staggering 3.4288 trillion won. A brewery had bought out, lock, stock, and barrel, the soju company that had collapsed after challenging the beer market. Thus , HiteJinro was born, bringing soju and beer under one roof. So, when you order a Chamisul at a restaurant now, that money goes to HiteJinro. The old toad has returned to the label, and Jinro has held the title of the world’s best-selling spirit brand for 25 consecutive years. The total number of Chamisul bottles sold so far is about 41.3 billion—a number hard to grasp, which averages out to 47 bottles sold every second. Most customers holding those bottles don’t know what happened to the company that first made this soju. Chairman Jang Jin-ho passed away in Beijing in 2015 while traveling between Cambodia and China, and no one from the Jang family, who grew the toad brand, remains at the company. The story does not end here. The very next month after Jinro faced the banks, a bread company collapsed. On May 13, 1997, Samlip failed to pay a 300 million won promissory note. Two days later, it officially went bankrupt. Samlip filed for court receivership at the Suwon District Court, and two of its affiliates bearing the Samlip name were also declared bankrupt on the same day. This was half a year before the country applied for an IMF bailout. For a large company, that amount is almost embarrassing to even call money. However, the company brought down by that small note was one that had truly changed South Korea’s winter. Samlip was a company started by Heo Chang-sung in a small bakery in Ongjin, Hwanghae-do. That bakery moved to Seoul , released cream bread, and established itself as a packaged bread company. Then, on the last day of winter in 1971 , the "Ho-ppang"(steamed bun) that left the factory sold over 1 million units a day. It was a record set even though they cost 20 won each, the price of four regular loaves of bread. It was a product that shattered the industry's common belief that bread doesn't sell in winter, all thanks to a steamer. So, where did the money earned from Ho-ppang go? Its destination was not bread. It went into beverages, cable broadcasting, and condominiums. These were businesses unrelated to a bakery. The situation in the bread business was also deteriorating. As bakeries opened in every neighborhood, competition for packaged bread intensified, and Samlip recorded losses for two consecutive years starting in 1995. Of course, the bakery competition is also a valid point. But the final blow that brought the company down wasn't the bread. The final blow was a stamp. Why on earth did a stamp bring the company down? The money the Samlip affiliates borrowed from financial firms reached 288.4 billion won. Of that, the payment guarantees for non-Samlip affiliates alone amounted to 75 billion won. In short, the money earned from steamed buns was leaking into other companies through blank documents. The 300 million won promissory note was just the trigger. The price of the bankruptcy remained with the people. Over 2,200 employees and 450 partner firms were shaken all at once. Guarantees on paper put the jobs of those outside the factory at risk. But when people find out who bought this company five years later, they all smile. Let me give you the answer first . The buyer was not a stranger. The story goes back to 1983. That year, Heo Young-in, the founder's second son, took over the affiliate bakery company, Shany, and branched off from his older brother. The younger brother’s family , having branched off, took a completely different path by opening the first Paris Baguette store in Gwanghwamun, Seoul, in 1988. Instead of churning out bread from a factory, it was a bakery that baked on-site. In 2002, five years after the older brother’s company, Samlip, went bankrupt and was under court receivership, the one who stepped up as the new owner was none other than the younger brother Heo Young-in’s company. The family that branched off bought back the main house that had collapsed. It’s as if two bakery companies from the same father saw one collapse due to guarantees and the other rise through neighborhood bakeries. That is why Samlip is now part of the same SPC Group as Paris Baguette. Dunkin’ and Baskin Robbins are also under the same group. The person leading that group is none other than the second son who branched off, Chairman Heo Young-in. Among those who eat steamed buns, how many truly know that Samlip and Paris Baguette are family? In January 2006, the new holding company adopted "Sangmidang," the name of the bakery first opened by the founder in 1945, and in March, the company name returned to "Samlip" after 10 years. Among the names in this video , Samlip is the only one whose founding family took the name back into their own hands. So, that was the story of things we eat and drink. This time, it’s a story about what we wear on our feet. However, this company sold more of other things than shoes. The protagonist this time is Esquire. It started in 1961 as a 10-pyeong shoe store in an alley in Myeong-dong, Seoul . It grew from there to become a company that led the shoe market alongside Kumkang and Elcanto. After the second-generation owner took over, sales grew rapidly. But when you looked inside, it was different. More than half of their sales were actually coming from gift certificates, not shoes. The discount rates were also substantial. They were sold at such deep discounts that you could buy five pairs for the price of three. What kind of business structure was this? Every time a gift certificate was sold, a pair of shoes to be delivered in the future was essentially reserved. Simply put, it was a business where the cash came in today and the shoes went out tomorrow. Of course, you might ask, isn't it good that money is coming in? But it wasn't. That money was cash generated by selling a future promise to provide shoes at a discounted price. A future borrowed today always returns as a bill. And the bills started arriving one after another. In 2009, the Myeong-dong flagship store closed, and a shoe retailer, ABC Mart, took its place. The operating company later went through a corporate workout program and was eventually placed under court receivership. They had reached a point where even doing business left them with no profit. The company was eventually put up for sale and found a new owner in 2015. An affiliate of Fashion Group Hyungji acquired it for 67 billion won. During that process, the court only recognized a portion of the gift certificate value held by customers as company debt. If you happened to have those Esquire gift certificates tucked away in a drawer back then, those papers never fully turned into shoes. Today, the brand remains under the name Hyungji Esquire and continues to sell shoes. However, the company that started in a 10-pyeong shop in Myeong-dong has left the founding family's hands, and a new surname, "Hyungji," has been added to its name. Everything up to this point was a bill written by the company's own hand. Whether it was business expansion , guarantees, or gift certificates, the company had stamped its own seal on them. But there is a name that involved the government's hand, not just the company's seal. This time, the name is Pro-Specs. The dissolution of the group that made these sneakers began not in a bank vault, but with a government decision. If you ever wore black rubber shoes, you likely remember the name " Wangja-pyo." The company that made those rubber shoes was the Kukje Group. It started from a rubber shoe factory established by Yang Jung-mo in Beomil-dong, Busan. Back when poorly made rubber shoes were common, other brands bowed to the superior quality of Wangja-pyo. This company was the first in Korea to export footwear to the US, and it was the key player in turning Busan into the world's largest shoe production hub. And the sneakers that this company introduced were none other than Pro-Specs. Just before its dissolution, the Kukje Group was a massive conglomerate ranking among the top firms, with 38,000 employees. On February 7, 1985, the Minister of Finance presented two proposals to the President. There was a plan to keep only Kukje Corporation, the core company, and liquidate the rest, and another to dismantle the entire group and hand it over to other companies. The president approved the second option. On February 20, the government summoned the president of Korea First Bank, the main creditor, to notify them of the dismantling policy and the companies to take over, and the next day, the dissolution was announced in the name of the bank. This sequence of events was later recorded exactly as such in the Constitutional Court's decision. The official reason cited was poor management, and the bank processed the returned bills as defaults. Before the year ended, some 20 affiliates were scattered and taken over by other groups, such as the Hanil Group and Dongkuk Steel. Employees found themselves working for companies that had changed owners overnight. However, even after the group was split up, the sneakers called Pro-Specs survived. It became a brand that sponsored the Seoul Asian Games and the Seoul Olympics, establishing itself as the nation’s favorite sneakers through the two events. The sneakers, having lost their group, hit their prime without one. In 1993, eight years after the dissolution , the Constitutional Court reached a conclusion on this case. By a 7-to-1 vote, the court ruled that the government's use of public authority to dismantle the Kukje Group was unconstitutional. So, did the company come back? It did not come back. Three years later, in 1996, the Supreme Court judged that the stock sales contracts signed back then were still valid. It meant that while the dissolution was ruled wrongful, there was no way to put the dismantled company back together. Founder Chairman Yang Jung-mo passed away in 2009 without ever being able to make a comeback. Kukje Corporation, which made Pro-Specs, went to the Hanil Group and then appeared on the market again in 2007 as a company under court receivership. It was bought by E1, an LS Group affiliate, and that company is now LS Networks, which runs Pro-Specs. Even while the owner changed twice and it went through court receivership, the name of the sneakers remained Pro-Specs . The path to collapse was different from the previous companies, but the outcome—the group scattered and the brand name surviving in someone else's hands—was the same. Let’s return to the story of debt. This time, the scale is different. It is the number two conglomerate in the business world. Daewoo Corporation started in 1967 as a trading company with 5 million won in capital. One generation was enough for this company to become the second-largest group with 41 affiliates . There was a time when the slogan " Global Management," put forth by founder Chairman Kim Woo-choong, covered the newspaper pages. It grew through exports and bulked up by buying up failing companies one after another, such as Korea Machinery, Saehan Motor, and Korea Shipbuilding Corporation. Saehan Motor becoming Daewoo Motor and Korea Shipbuilding Corporation becoming Daewoo Shipbuilding&Marine Engineering was that path. And a significant portion of those acquisition funds was debt. However, there is a point that sets Daewoo apart from other companies. At the very time when the foreign exchange crisis hit and everyone else was cutting debt, Daewoo went the opposite way and set out to acquire Ssangyong Motor. They chose to see the crisis as an opportunity and opted to buy even more. In the final stages, Chairman Kim Woo-choong and Chairman Lee Kun-hee attempted a "big deal" swap —giving Samsung Daewoo Electronics in exchange for Samsung Motors—but even those negotiations ultimately fell through. The result was the dissolution of the group in 1999. That year, Chairman Kim Woo-choong stepped down from his position all at once, along with the presidents of his affiliates. An investigation by the prosecution following the dissolution revealed a staggering 47 trillion won in accounting fraud. Chairman Kim stayed abroad for a long time before returning , and the 17.9 trillion won in fines levied against him as an individual remains the largest in history. So, where is the name "Daewoo" now? Daewoo Corporation, the root of the group, became POSCO International and removed the word "Daewoo" from its sign. Daewoo Motor was taken over by GM of the U.S. to become GM Korea, and Daewoo Securities was sold to Mirae Asset, becoming today's Mirae Asset Securities . Daewoo Shipbuilding&Marine Engineering, which built ships, became Hanwha Ocean. A single group was torn into several pieces, each bearing a different sign. Yet, the name "Daewoo" still makes money. Overseas, Daewoo is still considered a trusted name, so dozens of foreign companies pay licensing fees to use it on their own products, like home appliances. The company that acquired Daewoo Electronics operated under the name Winia Daewoo for a while, but when that contract ended in June 2020, POSCO International, which held the trademark , signed new contracts with BGH in Argentina and Vestel in Turkey. The following year, they even created a separate brand division dedicated solely to this trademark business. Although it has been over a quarter of a century since the group dissolved, the name has become a product that crosses borders. When you think about it, it’s a truly strange scene. Even though cars, ships, and securities firms have all taken down the Daewoo sign, the name "Daewoo" itself is still earning money. So, we’ve covered soju , bread, shoes, sneakers, and even cars . Then how long does a name last after a company disappears? The answer lies on the walls of apartment buildings. In the 1980s, the name "Woosung" was displayed like a badge of honor on apartment walls in Gangnam, Seoul. There was a time when saying you lived in a "Woosung Apartment" was proof of success. Woosung Construction, which bore that name, went bankrupt in 1996 after failing to cover 16.9 billion won in promissory notes. But even after the company vanished, the name remained on those apartment walls for decades more. It wasn't bankruptcy that finally took the name down from the walls. It was reconstruction. The Samik Apartments in Cheongdam-dong, named from the same era , have already been reborn as Leen, and Jamsil’s Woosung Apartments are also set to be renamed Jamsil Xi Riviere ahead of their reconstruction. Why do they let go of their old names? In the housing market, large-scale complexes with new, big-name construction brands attract far more applicants. Jinro, Samlip, Esquire, Kukje, Daewoo—soju, steamed buns, shoes, and sneakers were all still selling right up until the day the companies collapsed. Great products couldn't save the companies themselves. The finishing blow that brought down these companies usually came from outside the products. Expansion bought with debt, personal guarantees for others 'loans, and cash advances. In some cases, like the Kukje Group, the government even stepped in. But the names were different. Chamisul, Samlip Hoppang, Esquire shoes, Prospecs sneakers, and even Daewoo across the sea. Even though the owners have changed, those names are still selling today. Why did they survive? It’s not just because Hite Jinro, SPC, or Hyungji paid for the brand value. While factories and sales networks that these companies paid big money for were reasons, one clear reason lies in that initial scene. The scene where customers lined up for soju from a bankrupt company. In fact, that wasn't strange at all. The fate of a company is determined by the ledgers of shareholders and creditors, but the fate of a name is decided in front of the checkout counter. Chamisul survived because restaurant orders didn't stop even in the year news of its bankruptcy circulated, and the reason Woosung is coming off the walls lies in the housing market's preference for new, large construction brands. If you felt like you thought that company was still around, you were half wrong and half right. Even if the companies have left their owners to go their separate ways, their names are still here in our memories. How was it?

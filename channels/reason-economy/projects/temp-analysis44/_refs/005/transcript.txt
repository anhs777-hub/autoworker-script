Have you ever seen this bag on the street or in the subway? Back in 2014, you could always find three or four people in every subway car wearing MCM bags studded with gold decorations. Costing between 600,000 and 1 million won, this one bag alone brought in a staggering 600 billion won in a single year. But suddenly, this once-thriving company started pulling its stores out of Korean department stores one after another. Now, its revenue has plummeted to a quarter of what it was, and its operating profit has evaporated by 93%. Where did the luxury brand that brought in 600 billion won, thanks to Korean consumers, disappear to? Let’s track the astonishing trail of that money, one step at a time. This brand wasn't a Korean company from the very beginning. MCM was originally a long-standing European luxury brand born in Munich, Germany, in 1976. It made a name for itself as the premier travel luggage carried by actors, musicians, and the global wealthy on their private jets. With its unique leather pattern featuring laurel leaves and diamonds, there was a time in the mid-90s when it earned 300 million dollars annually and was called the "Louis Vuitton of Germany." But that glory didn't last long. The founder evaded massive taxes and expanded the business recklessly, leading to its downfall, and by the early 2000s, it faced imminent bankruptcy under an unmanageable mountain of debt. At this desperate moment, the savior who appeared was Chairwoman Kim Sung-joo, the youngest daughter of the Daesung Group founder. She had already established Sungjoo International in the early 90s, exclusively importing and selling MCM bags in Korea, and when the German headquarters was on the verge of collapse, she made a bold move to take over the entire company. The European fashion industry laughed it off. A small-to-medium-sized Asian enterprise —led by a woman, no less—trying to acquire a 30-year-old European luxury brand? Nobody believed it. But in 2005, Chairwoman Kim Sung-joo stunned everyone by acquiring 100%of the shares of the German MCM headquarters. It was a miraculous event, unprecedented in our fashion history, for a Korean company to buy out a prominent European luxury brand. The strategy Sungjoo Group implemented after the acquisition was sharper than anyone could have imagined. They combined Germany’s solid leather craftsmanship with Korea's distinct, vibrant pop culture. As top idols like G-Dragon and world star Rain wore them, the signature cognac-patterned studded bags took the nation by storm in an instant. Despite the hefty price tag I mentioned earlier , long lines formed in front of stores starting at dawn. It became so popular that among middle and high schoolers, it was dubbed a "uniform bag" worn over school uniforms, even earning the title of the quintessential "backbone breaker " for parents. It stood tall as an absolute powerhouse of "masstige," masterfully blending a luxury image with mass-market popularity. That explosive popularity was proven by results that defied imagination. By 2014, less than ten years after acquiring the headquarters, annual consolidated revenue for Sungjoo DND, which operates MCM, soared to a staggering 589.9 billion won. Operating profit alone reached 77.2 billion won in a single year, with an operating margin exceeding 13 percent. This wasn't a number achieved simply by selling a few extra bags. They had placed as many as fifty stores in the first-floor luxury goods sections of Korea’s three major department store chains: Lotte, Shinsegae, and Hyundai. It was a time when, upon entering the main door of a department store, you would see the brand standing shoulder to shoulder with Louis Vuitton and Gucci in the most expensive and prime locations. It was as if domestic consumers had generously opened their wallets to build a massive luxury fortress worth hundreds of billions of won. Everyone was enthusiastic about this dazzling myth. Praise poured out day after day, celebrating the German luxury brand, revived by Korean hands, standing tall as a global high-end name . However, right in the middle of this brilliant festival, a fatal crack that was invisible from the outside had begun to form quietly. It is simple. As money poured in, the company's focus shifted away from the domestic consumers who were buying their bags and toward somewhere entirely different . Yet, the company that had Korean consumers lining up to buy their products began making bizarre decisions at the very peak of its golden age, which defied common sense. They began to close the doors of Korean department stores with their own hands. Why on earth did such a strange phenomenon occur? The most expensive and flashy prime spots on the first floor of department stores are symbolic spaces where every fashion brand risks everything to get in. It is because they are the most definitive face of a brand, going beyond simply being a place to display and sell goods; they prove the brand's class and pride while instilling firm trust in consumers. But around 2013, an unbelievable reversal of numbers occurred in MCM's internal ledgers. From January to September of that year, sales at duty-free shops reached 150 billion won, easily surpassing the total sales of 100 billion won from domestic department stores. There was only one reason. It was the so-called "Youke" fever—the wave of Chinese group tourists pouring in from across the sea. Many of you will remember the scenes in Myeong-dong , Seoul, and the Lotte Duty Free main store back then. From dawn, large tour buses would line up, and countless Chinese tourists pulling suitcases would rush to the MCM corner the moment the store doors opened. It wasn't the kind of shopping where one slowly compares and chooses bag designs or colors. Every day, a spectacle unfolded where one person would sweep up five or six gold-studded backpacks from the display shelves and pay millions of won on the spot. The share of duty-free shops in total domestic sales soared in no time, skyrocketing from over 60%to 70%. With billions of won in cash pouring into the company’s accounts every single day, it must have looked like a golden era for the management, a time when they could easily ride the tide of success. So, what is the problem? One might ask, "Doesn't it matter where you sell it, as long as you're making money?" But this is precisely where a lethal management gamble began, one that would slowly erode the brand. To sell bags in a department store, you have to pay a hefty entry commission, which amounts to 20%to 30%of sales. On top of that, you had to bear the costs of demanding interior maintenance and thorough customer service. Domestic customers were discerning shoppers who would meticulously scrutinize everything, from tiny scratches on the leather to the finish of every single stitch. On the other hand, duty-free shoppers from China bought in bulk without any questions or fuss, so the headquarters calculated that it was much easier to secure massive margins with lower commission burdens. Drunk on the sweetness of short-term cash, the company eventually made an irrational and dangerous decision: they began shutting down department store outlets one after another—the very stores where they once paid high commissions to cater to demanding local customers. In 2014 alone, they closed a staggering 19 out of their 50 stores across major department stores nationwide. With eight stores closing at Lotte Department Store and four at Hyundai, the total number of locations shrank to 32 by 2017. In just three years, they had voluntarily slashed more than a third of their core department store presence. It was just like a shop owner kicking away the table of their loyal regulars—who had waited in line for years to help the business grow—the moment a group of tourist bus crowds arrived. They effectively drove away the local consumers who had cherished the brand for years and helped set the trends. It was a critical time that would determine the company's future, but the top leadership, who should have corrected this perilous trend, was distracted from the core business. As Chairwoman Kim Sung-joo took on the role of co-chair of a presidential campaign committee in 2012 and moved on to become the president of the Korean Red Cross in 2014, her core duties in fashion management and retail strategy were pushed to the sidelines. During this golden opportunity to stay a step ahead of new global trends and shifting retail environments, the top executive’s focus and capabilities were scattered toward unrelated external activities. Meanwhile, MCM’s business structure was transforming into a dangerously deformed model that pinned its entire fate on a single lifeline: Chinese tourists. But the price for blindingly chasing short-term cash and abandoning the domestic customer base soon began to destroy the very heart of the brand. Why on earth are luxury goods so expensive? It is partly because of their exceptional quality, but their most essential lifeblood lies in their scarcity. Consumers are willing to pay a premium precisely because of that sense of exclusivity—the feeling that it’s hard to come by and requires waiting in line at department stores just to make a purchase. This is exactly why world-renowned luxury houses like Chanel or Hermès never hold sales and strictly control their supply volume. They know all too well that once a brand starts selling for cheap, its mystique is shattered in an instant. However, intoxicated by the sweet allure of immediate profits, MCM began to head in the completely opposite direction. To attract Chinese tour groups and "baggage traders," they dove headfirst into a cutthroat competition that eroded their own margins the more they sold. The commissions paid to duty-free stores in exchange for bringing in these traders skyrocketed, reaching anywhere from 40%to as high as 60%. Essentially, they were running a distorted business where nearly half of every bag's price was handed over to travel agencies and traders as commission. On top of that, they began pouring out massive dumping discounts to clear out their inventory. What was the result? Bags worth 600,000 to a million won, which should have been displayed elegantly in department store glass cases, were left strewn on duty-free floors like sacks of grain, flooding the Chinese black market and domestic outlets at less than half their price. With their logo-studded bags rolling around everywhere you looked, the brand's prestige plummeted overnight. Desperate to restore their fallen status, the company came up with a bizarre strategy. While keeping the designs and fabrics exactly the same, they quietly raised the price tags to levels rivaling Chanel or Louis Vuitton . But the reaction from domestic consumers was cold, bordering on icy. Who on earth would pay full price for a bag at a department store when it's already floating around at half-price discounts in duty-free shops and outlets? It's simple. In the eyes of consumers, it was just an ambiguous, mediocre brand with a ruined reputation ; they completely turned their backs, deciding that for a bit more money, they’d rather buy a traditional European luxury item. But a far more brutal scene was unfolding behind the glamorous lights, in the subcontractor factories. In the spring of 2017, four supplier companies that were directly manufacturing and delivering these bags could no longer hold out and filed a complaint with fair trade authorities. The reality of the subcontracting agreements they tearfully exposed was nothing short of shocking. The headquarters had kept the suppliers ' margins locked at a fixed rate for a staggering 12 years, refusing to raise the unit price by even a single won. Throughout those 12 years, as inflation soared and the minimum wage more than doubled, the factory owners who stayed up all night sewing bags had to take out loans just to cover their workers' salaries. They even offloaded the costs of unsold inventory and returned goods back onto the subcontractors, charging them more than the original consumer price. So, where did all that money go, squeezed out from the blood and sweat of the suppliers? Opening the books reveals a shockingly cruel symmetry. While the subcontractors were driven to the brink, shedding tears of blood due to frozen unit prices, Chairperson Kim Sung-joo’s family was enjoying a massive cash dividend party worth 5 billion won from the company. Since the owner family held nearly 95%of Sungjoo DND’s shares, a whopping 4.74 billion won out of that 5 billion won dividend went straight into the family's private coffers. The public couldn't help but click their tongues at the owner family’s behavior, squeezing supply factories for 12 years without raising their margins by a single penny, all while pocketing billions in dividends behind their backs. Faced with a barrage of public criticism and a boycott, Chairperson Kim Sung-joo eventually had to step down from her position as CEO in a hurry. Just as domestic consumers turned their backs completely and suppliers were crying out in agony, a massive storm began to brew in the sky. The bill for their deformed business model, which abandoned local loyalists to focus on only one market, finally arrived as an unbearable, harsh demand. In the aftermath of the THAAD deployment in 2016 and 2017, the Chinese government carried out retaliatory measures by completely banning group tours to South Korea. The footsteps of the countless Chinese tourists who used to pour into duty-free shops ceased overnight. For MCM, which relied on the wallets of Chinese tourists for nearly 70%of its revenue, it was truly a bolt from the blue. However, this was merely the prelude to the massive catastrophe that was yet to come. Before the company could even catch its breath while teetering, the massive storm of the COVID-19 pandemic swept across the world in 2020. Borders were locked tight, and air routes at airports around the world were completely shut down. It was like cutting the only lifeline of a tightrope walker who had been performing a precarious aerial stunt on a single rope. The corridors of Incheon Airport and downtown duty-free shops turned into ghost towns , their lights off even in broad daylight, while the bags piled high in the stores were left to gather dust. Tens of thousands of unsold backpacks piling up in every duty-free warehouse returned as a boomerang in the form of massive maintenance costs. Having cleared out over a third of their domestic department store outlets to avoid high commissions, there was no domestic buffer left to cushion the disaster when the foreigners stopped coming. On top of this, the overseas subsidiaries in Europe, Japan, and China—established in the name of going "global luxury"—began pouring out tens of billions of won in chronic annual deficits, strangling the headquarters. The disastrous results were laid bare as a massive deficit bomb on the public disclosure page. In 2020 alone, consolidated revenue plummeted by nearly 37%year-over-year to 312.6 billion won. Operating profit plunged by 63%to 10.8 billion won, and net income nose-dived into a staggering red-ink deficit of 20.1 billion won. In just one year, over 20 billion won of the company's net profit evaporated into thin air. So, what is the problem? You might think that since it hit rock bottom, there is nowhere to go but up. But the reality was far too cold and harsh. In 2022, Sungjoo DND's annual revenue slumped to 131 billion won, and with an operating loss of 17 billion won, it fell into the worst operating deficit since its founding. Looking at the most recent 2024 report, the depth of its collapse is revealed even more painfully. In 2024, Sungjoo DND's consolidated revenue stopped at 154.2 billion won. Operating profit was a mere 5.1 billion won, barely managing to avoid a deficit and just hanging on to a thin surplus. This means a company that once earned over 200 million won in operating profit every single day now barely nets 5 billion won even after working its fingers to the bone for a whole year. When you place it side-by-side with the books from its 2014 peak ten years ago, the gap hits home painfully. Since the annual revenue of 589.9 billion won at the time has shriveled to 154.2 billion won , a massive 74%of revenue has vanished without a trace in ten years. It has been cut exactly down to a quarter of its size. Even more shocking is the collapse of its operating profit. The cash flow that used to pour in at 77.2 billion won per year has now evaporated by over 93%to just 5.1 billion won. It is as if a massive luxury fortress that boasted 600 billion won in annual revenue has crumbled like a sandcastle in just ten years. So, where have those gold-studded backpacks that once covered the streets everywhere disappeared to ten years later? Take a look at the luxury goods corner on the first floor of any major department store near you. Do you see those gold-studded bags that were greeting customers from the best, most prominent spots just ten years ago? No matter how hard you look, they are now difficult to find. With over 50 department store locations nationwide back in 2014, they began to disappear one by one, and now they have effectively been pushed out of almost every major department store in the country. In the spot by the escalator where MCM once shone brightest, imported luxury goods or emerging designer brands have taken their place. The only place that remembers its glorious past is the lonely, large flagship store standing in the luxury district of Cheongdam-dong, Gangnam. So, where did all those bags that were pushed out of the department store’s first floor end up? Ironically, the main stage where MCM sells most actively today is none other than Musinsa and 29CM, online fashion platforms beloved by teens and those in their 20s. Open a smartphone app and search for MCM, and you’ll see a truly astonishing sight. Iconic bags that once cost 600,000 or a million won in department stores are now sprawled across discount racks, marked with red "exclusive special offer" stickers at 30%to 50%off. They have been reduced to being sold off alongside street casual wear and sneakers. It means their price floor— the most vital lifeline and source of pride for any luxury brand—has completely collapsed. Fashion trends were not on the brand’s side, either. Since the 2020s, the landscape of the fashion industry has flipped 180 degrees. Subtle luxury that doesn't loudly display logos, known as "old money look" and "stealth luxury," has become the mainstream trend. The brown logo patterns covering the entire leather and the flashy gold hardware have returned as a painful stigma, labeled by the younger generation as tacky and gaudy. Trophies that should have been kept precious inside glass showcases are now treated as dusty, outdated relics. Of course, the company didn’t just sit idly by. They made tearful struggles for survival to win back the hearts of the younger generation. They recruited a new creative director, held fashion shows in Milan, and attempted to expand from bags into clothing lines. They even teamed up with trendy street brands that Gen Z and younger consumers are crazy about, like The Clubs or BAPE, to release limited-edition collaborations. They were desperate to somehow draw the attention of young consumers again and breathe new life into the brand. However, the market’s evaluation was cold, if not cruel. No matter how flashy the collaborations were or how new the clothes they produced, the wallets of domestic consumers who had lost trust and turned away did not open again. Once imprinted in the public mind as a "duty-free dumping brand" or "outlet clearance bag," there were no consumers left to pay the full price of hundreds of thousands of won. In the end, the only option left was not luxury department store shelves, but clearing out remaining inventory by slashing prices by nearly half in Musinsa’s discount corner. This is exactly why annual revenue has been trapped in the 150 billion won range for years, unable to move a single step forward. It is a stark and ruthless scorecard for a brand that trampled on the pride of its domestic customers. The bizarre path of self-destruction that MCM has walked. But this tragedy was not just the story of one company, MCM. If you broaden your perspective, you can see that this was not a tragedy limited to just one brand. First-generation homegrown handbag brands like Metrocity and Louis Quatorze, which once commanded the prime spots on the first floors of Lotte, Shinsegae, and Hyundai department stores, were similarly pushed out and vanished. This is a perfect mirror image of the cosmetics market, where It's Skin, having swept the wallets of Chinese tourists with just its snail cream and raced to a KOSPI listing, collapsed with its revenue halved by a single blow from THAAD. Just as K-beauty roads, intoxicated by the Chinese tourist boom and neglecting domestic 20s and 30s customers, lost their entire domestic market and collapsed, it was a brutal chain reaction that companies abandoning their home base had to face. What, after all, is luxury? Chanel and Hermes, with their centuries of history , have a golden rule they guard like their own lives. It is the principle that no matter how desperate they are for money, they strictly control supply and never sell products at a discount. That is because they knew too well that the essence of luxury is not a flashy logo, but the trust and scarcity that consumers are willing to pay for with pride. On the other hand, MCM fell for the temptation of short-term cash, selling off its brand's soul through mass distribution in duty-free shops and dumping discounts. Recall, if you will, the gold-studded backpacks that 15 people in every Seoul subway car were proudly wearing in 2014. There was a time when the whole nation proudly wore them—whether as a gift to oneself from a first paycheck or a graduation present from parents. The bags we wore were not just simple leather pouches. They were the excitement of youth starting new challenges and precious symbols containing the warm support of our families. Consumers opened their wallets generously, fueled by the pride that a Korean company had acquired a European luxury brand and succeeded on the global stage, along with the special value that adorned their shoulders. But the company traded that precious loyalty for short-term cash and threw it all away into the air. What remains in return is only a fragmented, shabby stature and a cold reality of being kicked out of department stores and driven into Musinsa discount sales. The trajectory of MCM chillingly proves the fact that the market never gives a second chance to a brand that has lost the trust of its home base and sold its soul while relying only on external winds. The empty space on the first floor of the department store, which remains vacant to this day, stands as the most expensive bill that its greed must pay.

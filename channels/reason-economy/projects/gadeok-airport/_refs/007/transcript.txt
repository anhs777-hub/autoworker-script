In May 2025, a rare event occurred in the South Korean construction industry. Hyundai Engineering&Construction, a top-tier firm, walked away from a 10.5 trillion won national project. It was a project to build the site for a new airport off the coast of Gadeokdo, Busan. It wasn't because they lacked the funds. And it wasn't because the company was struggling. The reason Hyundai E&C provided was time. They argued that the government’s 84-month timeline was unsafe and that 108 months were required. To reach this conclusion , Hyundai E&C assigned some 250 experts to work on the design for six months. They spent about 60 billion won on design costs alone. The government did not accept this, and Hyundai E&C did not participate in the re-bidding. The industry's eyes turned across the sea to Osaka Bay in Japan. It is Kansai Airport. The land for this airport, built by reclaiming the sea, has subsided by 13.7 meters since construction began. That is the height of a five-story apartment building. Gadeokdo is considered to have even more difficult conditions than that site. Yet, Korea set up a new plan to cut down the island's mountains and reclaim the sea with that stone, and the design passed deliberation in September 2026. Who intends to enter the sea that Hyundai E&C backed away from, and how? The story begins with an accident 24 years ago. On April 15, 2002, a China International Airlines flight from Beijing was approaching Gimhae Airport. The weather was bad. The clouds were low, and a south wind was blowing. Gimhae Airport is blocked by mountains to the north. When there is a south wind, planes must enter from the south, pass the airport, and make a wide turn from the north to land. This is called a circling approach. This plane crashed into Mt. Dotdae north of the runway while making that turn. It is a mountain 380 meters above sea level. Of the 166 people on board, 129 died. 101 of them were South Korean. The survivors numbered thirty-seven, including the captain. The accident investigation report was released three years later, in May 2005. The direct cause was concluded to be pilot error. It stated that the turn was late, the speed was too high, and they failed to pull up after missing the runway. However, this accident left behind other questions. Why must this airport require planes to turn around mountains when the weather is bad? That was not the only problem with Gimhae Airport. Because many people live nearby, flights cannot take off or land between 11:00 PM and 6:00 AM. It is an airport that shuts its doors for seven hours a day. The runway is also short. The longer side is 3,200 meters. The government's master plan judges that 3,500 meters are needed for a fully loaded large cargo plane to travel long distances. The result can be seen in the cargo figures. Gimhae Airport handles between 20,000 and 40,000 tons of international cargo per year. The processing capacity is 110,000 tons, so it’s not even filling half of that. What gets loaded is mostly just auto parts, shoe components, and agricultural products. During the pandemic, the international cargo terminal closed for over three years and only reopened in July 2023. Let's take strawberries as an example. Over 90%of the strawberries Korea exports are produced in the Jinju area of Gyeongnam. That’s 3,600 tons a year, worth 54 million dollars. To put these strawberries on a plane, they had to be trucked for five hours up to Incheon Airport. If they were loaded directly from Gimhae, distribution time would be cut by one to two days. If that's the case for strawberries, it goes without saying for semiconductors or precision parts. Passenger numbers, on the other hand, are overflowing. In December 2025 , annual international passenger traffic at Gimhae Airport exceeded 10 million. This is a first since it opened in 1976 and a first for any regional airport. Before that, the highest year was 2018, with 9.87 million passengers. There are 1,546 flights per week to 42 cities. That’s an increase of 240 flights from the 1,306 at the end of 2018. Passengers have increased like this, but the airport has to close for seven hours at night and there’s no room for a long runway. Discussions for a new airport became official in 2006 when President Roh Moo-hyun ordered a review. There were two candidate sites. Gadeokdo, pushed by Busan, and Miryang, pushed by Daegu, Gyeongbuk, and Gyeongnam. On March 30, 2011, the government announced that neither was suitable. Out of 100 points, Miryang scored 39.9 and Gadeokdo scored 38.3. It was 38.3 points. Both failed to meet the standard of 50 points. In the 2012 presidential election, candidates promised a new airport again. You might be thinking, why not just cut down the mountains at the existing airport and extend the runway? The government went down that road first, too. In June 2016 , following an evaluation by a French agency, the government chose to add another runway to Gimhae Airport instead of building a new one. This conclusion came after Gadeokdo and Miryang had clashed for nearly ten years. However, four years later in November 2020, the Prime Minister’s verification committee announced that this expansion plan needed to be reconsidered from scratch. The verification took 11 months. To build a new runway, surrounding mountains would need to be cut, and the issue was that this had not been discussed with the city of Busan. The geographical constraint of mountains in Gimhae ended up being the dealbreaker. There was also significant backlash. Daegu, which had pushed for Miryang, said they could not accept it, and there was criticism that this decision was made ahead of the Busan mayoral by-election. In February 2021, the National Assembly passed the Gadeok New Airport Special Act. In April 2022, it was confirmed that the preliminary feasibility study would be waived. The project cost is 13.7 trillion won. It was the largest scale ever for an exempted project. An airport that couldn't find an answer on land moved out to sea. Gadeokdo is an island attached to the western edge of Busan. Busan New Port is located right next to it. The problem is that there is almost no flat land on this island. To the south of the island stands a rocky mountain called Guksubong, and beyond that lies deep sea. The average water depth at the airport site is between 20m and 25m. It's not calm water inside a breakwater, either. It faces the open sea directly, meaning waves can reach up to 12m. The bigger problem is beneath the water. The seabed is not solid. It is mud. There is a thick layer of soft, water-saturated clay. The thickness in the section where the runway will pass was surveyed at 22m to 42m. Some point out that it reaches up to 60m in thicker areas. This type of ground is called soft ground. Think of it like placing bricks on top of silken tofu. It starts to sink the moment you put them on, and the heavier it is, the more it sinks. Can’t you just build it higher in advance to account for the sinking? You might ask that. If it sinks evenly, that would be correct. The problem is that it sinks differently in every spot. And mud doesn't sink all at once. As the water inside slowly drains out, it settles over several decades. A place where that actually happened is Kansai Airport. Japan began building an artificial island in the middle of Osaka Bay in 1987. They intended to create Japan's first 24-hour airport. The idea of moving to the sea to avoid noise was the same as Gadeokdo. The budget for the first island alone was 1.45 trillion yen. The water depth was 18m. On the seabed, there was a 20 m-thick layer of clay that was 70% saturated with water. Japanese engineers were aware of this. So, they drove 1 million sand columns into this clay layer. They inserted 20 m-long columns at 2.5 m intervals. The idea was to let the water inside the mud drain out quickly through the sand columns. They piled stones around the island and surrounded it with 48,000 concrete blocks to block waves. This work was successful. The upper clay layer hardened as planned. The problem was beneath that. There was another older clay layer beneath the soft clay layer. It is a layer reaching depths of 140m to 200m. It was too deep to drive in sand columns. Japanese engineers assumed this layer would be solid and wouldn't settle much. That judgment was off the mark. The maximum settlement predicted during the design phase was 5.7 meters. However, by 1999, five years after the airport opened, it had already sunk 8.2 meters. In the first year of operation, it sank by 50 centimeters. As of December 2025, the cumulative settlement of Kansai Airport's first island is 13.72 meters. Of this, 9.82 meters sank before opening, and 3.9 meters sank after it began receiving passengers. It is still sinking by about 5 centimeters every year. What is even more troublesome is that it does not sink evenly. The rate of sinking varies from spot to spot. That is why hydraulic jacks are installed under the 900 columns of the passenger terminal. When a column sinks , they lift it and insert steel plates to adjust the height. This is a job they have been doing for 30 years. Over 150 million dollars were spent just to raise the island's level by 2000. Japan did not stop there. In 1999, they began building a second island and opened a 4,000-meter runway in August 2007. This time, they drove 1.2 million sand columns into the ground. They applied everything they learned from the first island. However, the second island was in deeper water, and the layer of old clay beneath it was thicker. The cumulative settlement of this island is 17.47 meters. It is still sinking by 21 centimeters every year. 400 million cubic meters of soil and rock were used to build the two islands. The total cost incurred by 2008 is around 20 billion dollars. They built it relying on their calculations, but the geological strata moved beyond those calculations. But the bigger price came on September 4, 2018. Coincidentally, it was exactly 24 years to the day the airport opened. Typhoon Jebi passed through Osaka Bay. Seawater flowed over the lowered island. Both runways were damaged, and the terminal basement was flooded. A 2,591-ton oil tanker, pushed by the wind, crashed into the bridge connecting the mainland to the airport. 3,000 passengers and 2,000 staff members were trapped on the island. The next day, three ships capable of carrying 110 people ferried people out every 15 to 20 minutes, and buses crossed the bridge using the undamaged lane. Two days later, some domestic flights resumed, but it was October 1st before the airport regained full functionality. The damaged bridge was fully repaired the following April. However, not all airports built on reclaimed land end up like this. Incheon Airport was also built on reclaimed tidal flats, but the measured settlement over 30 years is at most 2.9 centimeters. It does not even reach the 7.5 centimeters or 5 centimeters predicted during design. Chubu Centrair International Airport in Nagoya, Japan, opened in 2005, reduced landfill soil by 4 million cubic meters by mixing cement with seabed soil to solidify it. The difference comes from the seabed strata. It means that where you place an airport determines the subsequent construction methods, costs, and maintenance. Then, which one is the seabed of Gadeokdo closer to? Professor Emeritus Lim Jong-chul of the Department of Civil Engineering at Pusan National University says Gadeokdo is different from Kansai. At Kansai, the soft soil layer is hundreds of meters deep, so only the top 20 to 30 meters could be treated, but the Gadeokdo runway section is 22 to 42 meters, which is a range that can be fully improved. However, there are opposing voices as well. They point out that 59%of the site is reclaimed land and there are areas where mud reaches 60 meters deep, making differential settlement difficult to avoid. The framework of the plan is as follows. Cut the mountains on the island and use the rock to fill the sea. It sounds like a new method, but there is a precedent. It's Hong Kong. The current Hong Kong International Airport was created by cutting down the entire island of Chek Lap Kok. They leveled the 3-square-kilometer island and filled 9.38 square kilometers of the surrounding sea. The airport site created in that way is nearly 1%of Hong Kong's total area. It started in 1991 and opened in July 1998. When Hong Kong built its third runway, it reclaimed an additional 650 hectares of the sea, and the method used was deep cement mixing to solidify the mud. That runway opened in 2022. It is the same technology that Gadeokdo intends to use. However, there is a difference: Hong Kong's sea is inland, surrounded by islands, while Gadeokdo faces the open sea directly. They will excavate Guksubong Peak and dump the resulting rock and soil directly into the sea in front of it. The amount of material required to fill the sea is 96 million cubic meters. That is enough to build ten Soyanggang Dams. The entire construction area is approximately 6.67 million square meters. The order is set. First, they extract stone from the mountain, treat the seabed mud, build a breakwater, fill the sea inside, compact the land, and then lay the runway. Since these six processes must interlock, the contract was awarded by bundling design and construction to a single company. One runway will be built, measuring 3,500 meters long and 45 meters wide. The runway height was also raised to prepare for typhoons. It was 15 meters above sea level in the initial study, but it became 31.5 meters in the basic plan. This design follows seeing Kansai submerged by waves. The outer breakwater is designed based on waves that occur once every 100 years. It also includes the highest grade of precision approach facilities that allow landing solely by instruments even in fog. If you recall the accident in 2002, you can understand why this part was included. However, the terminal itself is not built on the sea . The passenger terminal, which is the heaviest and most crowded area, is placed on the bedrock of land carved out of mountains. Bedrock does not sink . So there is no need to place jacks under pillars like in Kansai. The terminal will have a total floor area of 200,000 square meters, with an apron capable of accommodating 58 aircraft and passenger boarding bridges. But this layout comes with a price. The runway ends up spanning both land and reclaimed land. One end sits on solid bedrock, while the other rests on land filled with mud. Since the bedrock doesn't move but the reclaimed land sinks, the runway could crack at the boundary. This is called differential settlement. Actually, this was not the original plan. The pre-feasibility study released in April 2022 chose a plan to float the entire airport on the sea. The reason was exactly this differential settlement. The logic was that if it were entirely on the sea, it would all sink at a similar rate, resulting in less distortion. There were other reasons for choosing the sea . It was to avoid noise for nearby residential areas, prevent overlap with military flight procedures in Gimhae and Jinhae, and not block the paths of large ships entering and leaving Busan New Port. However, that plan took too long to build. The opening date was set for June 2035. In 2023, the government changed the layout to span both land and sea. The calculation was that the construction period would shorten as the area to be reclaimed from the sea decreased. By gaining the advantage of placing the terminal on bedrock, they are taking on the problem of differential settlement they initially tried to avoid. So the key to success lies in how much the land on the sea side can be kept from sinking. The standards are already set. The International Civil Aviation Organization states that the height difference must not exceed 3cm for every 30m of runway length. In terms of slope, that is 1/1000. It might not be visible to the eye, but it is different for airplane wheels moving at 250 km/h. Maintaining this line for 30 years is the goal of the design. Two methods are combined to achieve this. One is to drain water from the mud in advance. Just like wrapping tofu in a cloth and pressing it with a heavy object to drain water and make it firm. It is the same principle. However, since mud particles are so fine, it takes decades for the water to drain on its own. So, we create paths for it. From a ship, we insert countless flat drainage materials deep into the mud layer. It is like inserting straws closely together. When rocks and soil are piled on top to press down with weight, the water comes up through the straws. This is called the vertical drain method. It's a technology in the same vein as the sand pillars used at Kansai. Another method is to solidify the mud entirely. The target is the area beneath the breakwaters that take the direct impact of the waves. Equipment with rotating blades is lowered into the mud to spray cement while mixing it in. This creates solid pillars within the mud layer. This is done all the way down until it reaches the seabed bedrock. It is known as the Deep Mixing Method. While Kansai couldn't reach the deeper layers, the fact that Gadeokdo is at a depth that allows for reaching the bedrock is the basis for this plan. The problem is that all of these tasks consume a significant amount of time. Draining water from the mud and waiting for it to harden is a process that cannot be rushed by humans. However, the schedule for Gadeokdo was set backwards from the start. The opening date suggested by the preliminary feasibility study was June 2035. But Busan was in the midst of a bid to host the 2030 World Expo. Demands emerged that the airport must open before the Expo, so the government moved the opening up to December 2029. They cut the timeline by 5 years and 6 months. On November 28, the host city vote was held in Paris. Riyadh, Saudi Arabia received 119 votes, Busan 29, and Rome, Italy 17. The Expo did not come. Yet, the date of 2029 remained unchanged. The tender conditions stipulated an 84- month construction period and a budget of 10.5 trillion won. The project went to tender four times in 2024, but all four bids failed. No real competition was established. Ultimately, the government pursued a private contract with the Hyundai E&C consortium, which was the sole applicant. Hyundai E&C, along with Daewoo E&C and POSCO, deployed 250 experts to review the design for six months. However, the conclusion reached in the spring of 2025 was 108 months. The logic was as follows. In a sea where waves reach up to 12 meters, you cannot build the breakwater and perform reclamation at the same time. A portion of the breakwater must be built first over seven months. And 18 months are needed to wait for the mud under the runway to stabilize. The government halted the private contract procedure on May 8, 2025. The reason given was that it differed from the bid conditions. Criticism was also directed at Hyundai E&C, calling it a ploy to increase the construction period for profit. On May 30, Hyundai E&C announced it would not participate in the re-tendering. Their official statement read as follows. We cannot accept unreasonable demands to shorten the construction period that compromise airport safety due to local and political interests. We also cannot accept the false accusation that we are delaying a national project for private gain. At the same time, they stated they would relinquish the rights to the basic design, which cost 60 billion won , and cooperate in selecting the next contractor. Honestly, it is hard to blame Hyundai E&C on this point. It wasn’t the construction companies that set the deadline on land that needed time to settle. In the end, it was the deadline that had to give way. On November 21, 2025, the government announced it would extend the construction period from 84 months to 106 months. That is just a two-month difference from the 108 months Hyundai E&C had requested. The reasoning for the 22-month extension was the same. They said it would take 66 months just to stabilize the soft ground. They also factored in the time needed to build and source large-scale equipment for offshore work and to construct access roads first. Construction costs rose to 10.7 trillion won, and the target completion year became 2035. It returned to the very same 2035 mentioned in the preliminary feasibility study. The five and a half years they had pulled forward toward 2029 were effectively put back. In my view, that deadline was the most expensive decision made in this project . A construction firm left, the design was redone from scratch, and a year and a half passed from May 2025, when private contract negotiations stalled, to November 2026, when the first shovel hit the ground. The party that stepped forward under these new conditions is the Daewoo E&C consortium. It’s the same Daewoo E&C that was previously in the Hyundai E&C consortium. It is a participating company. Daewoo E&C holds 38%, while newcomers Hanwha holds 11% and Lotte E&C takes 10%. They filled the gap left by the 25.5%stake from Hyundai E&C and the 13.5%from Posco E&C . There were no competitors this time either. The re-bidding failed, and after five consecutive failed bids, it went to a private contract. The fact remains that there was only one team willing to take on a 10-trillion-won project. What Daewoo E&C emphasizes is that they know the sea. This company is the one that built the Geoga undersea tunnel connecting Gadeokdo and Geoje. They submerged and connected 18 concrete structures, each weighing 50,000 tons, on soft ground 48 meters underwater. The company explains that they kept the joint error within 5cm, and there has been no subsidence or leaks in the 15 years since it opened. In Al-Faw, Iraq, they are building a 5- trillion-won port facility on ultra-soft ground. It is also a company that has been reclaiming land from the sea, from the 1984 Gwangyang Steel Works site development to the Sihwa Tidal Power Plant and Busan New Port. Ahead of the Gadeokdo design, Daewoo E& C announced it would review two methods . One is to turn the sea into land first before working, and the other is to remove the soft mud and replace it with good soil. They began the basic design on March 9, 2026, and submitted the blueprints six months later on September 7. Over two days, September 21st and 22nd, 20 members of the review committee evaluated seven sectors, including airport, port and coastal, soil and foundation, civil engineering structures and construction, electrical communications, and smart construction technology. The score was 80.41, which is eligible. The schedule is set as follows. After the Chuseok holiday, they will sign a private contract and begin detailed design. They will break ground on preliminary works this November, with the main construction beginning in the first half of 2027. However, a variable has emerged. Construction companies from Busan and Gyeongnam in the consortium are demanding an increase in construction costs of 460 to 620 billion won, citing rising fuel and material prices. There is even talk that they will pull out if it is not accepted. After hearing this, you might have this question. Why insist on building an airport in such a difficult sea? The logic for those in favor is cargo. Semiconductors, batteries, and precision parts made in Busan, Ulsan, Changwon, and Gumi currently have to go to Incheon Airport to be shipped by air. They have to travel 400km by truck. The nearby Gimhae Airport shuts down at night and its runway is too short to properly handle large cargo planes. Gadeokdo is on the sea, so it can operate 24 hours, and its 3,500 m runway allows large cargo planes to take off fully loaded. Right next to it is the Busan New Port. The plan is to transfer cargo arriving by ship directly onto planes and connect it via railway. The master plan includes cargo terminals and related facilities, along with a 1.26 million square meter site for support facilities, equivalent to 180 soccer fields. It also includes a road from Gadeok Bridge and an airport railway connected to the Busan New Port railway . Since the declaration of heavy chemical industrialization in 1973, factories and ports have been built in Pohang, Ulsan, and Changwon, but there was no 24-hour airport in this belt. However, what the opposing side brings up is numbers. In the 2022 pre-feasibility study, the benefit-cost ratio of this airport was 0.51. It must exceed 1 to mean that the gains outweigh the costs. This project was pushed forward with an exemption from a preliminary feasibility study. The value recalculated by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport in August 2026 is 0.36. It is 0.36. Total project costs swelled to 14.4168 trillion won, while benefits came out to 5.72 trillion won. That is an increase of nearly 1 trillion won from the 13.4913 trillion won estimated in the 2023 master plan. The net present value is -10.104 trillion won. Construction costs are 13.1089 trillion won. Incidental costs such as design and supervision are 910 billion won. Land compensation costs are 397.9 billion won. The initial figures put forward by Busan City were a project cost of 7.5 trillion won and opening in 2029. Costs have nearly doubled, and the date has been pushed back by six years. Demand forecasts are also conflicting. The government projects 23.36 million passengers and 286,000 tons of cargo by 2065. For cargo alone, that is over seven times the amount currently handled by Gimhae. It is half of the 46 million passengers cited by the city of Busan. The government's stance is clear. Even if profitability is low, they argue it is right to proceed for balanced regional development. There is one figure here that is easy to confuse. The 10.7 trillion won is the cost to reclaim the sea, while the 14.4168 trillion won is the total project cost, including terminals and access infrastructure. It is an airport where land costs make up about three-quarters of the total. Looking only at the numbers, the case for building it is weak. However, it is also a fact that there is no 24-hour gateway outside the capital region. Whichever side you support, once it is decided to build, only one question remains. The question is whether it will be built properly. And there are people who have always lived by this sea. They are the three villages of Daehang, Sebaji, and Yangpo, which fall within the airport site. 380 households and 650 residents must relocate. The total population of Gadeokdo is 4,329, and 36%of them are over sixty-five years old. It is not an easy age to find a new home and start over. Among the 679 landowners receiving compensation, 73%have filed objections to the compensation amounts. The relocation compensation is, at most, 24 million won. The residents 'committee, which was once unified, has split into over a dozen groups. While construction was stalled, these people could neither leave nor stay. As of December 2025, specific measures for fishing rights compensation and livelihood support had not been finalized. There are still many unresolved tasks. Whether the runway will hold up for 30 years at the boundary between land and landfill remains to be seen. After all, the engineers at Kansai also believed in their own calculations. Bird-related issues have also emerged. Gadeokdo is a gateway for migratory birds. In one survey, over 1,000 red-billed gulls, an endangered species, were identified over 16 days. There are also concerns that the runway width of 45 meters is narrower than the 60 meters at Incheon Airport. The government stated that it has included bird strike prevention measures and ground monitoring as construction requirements. They plan to measure the ground throughout construction and reflect the results in subsequent phases. And an airport's life is even longer after it opens. What Kansai showed is that subsidence does not end with the opening. The process of measuring, lifting, and raising the ground continues for decades. The story that began in 2002 at Mt. Daedae has reached the edge of the sea after 24 years. In the meantime , the Gimhae expansion plan was overturned, the date pushed forward to 2030 caused one construction firm to withdraw, and the date eventually returned to its original place. The first test is near. It is whether the first construction equipment will truly enter the waters off Gadeokdo this November.

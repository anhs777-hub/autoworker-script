They built a factory worth 3.5 trillion won and didn't get paid. When they asked for the money, a bill was sent to them instead. The client claimed the factory wasn't built properly and demanded compensation. This is what Daewoo E&C went through in Algeria. It was a factory built with Japan's Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, but Daewoo's share never arrived. The amount they were supposed to receive was about 82 billion won. The amount they were told to pay back was 810.7 billion won. That’s ten times the amount. Does this sound reasonable to you? But the factory was running perfectly fine at the time. The client was producing and selling goods from that very factory. Even the papers signing off on the factory's operation and promising the payment were signed by the client themselves. They overturned the documents they had signed and stamped. So what happened? They fought for seven years. During those seven years, all Daewoo E&C held in their hands were two pieces of paper . Surprisingly, many people don't know about this. It was just a single line in the news. But there is something even stranger in this story. Let me tell you what kind of factory it is. It’s a fertilizer factory that produces urea. Those white granules that go into urea solution. You've seen those blue containers next to gas pumps at highway rest areas, right? The ones diesel trucks fill up every time they refuel. The liquid in those containers is made in domestic factories. But the white urea granules dissolved in them are not domestic at all. There isn't a single factory in our country that produces urea. So, South Korea's urea production is zero. However, the factory Daewoo E&C built in Algeria produces 7,000 tons a day. A country with no urea production built one of the world's top-tier urea factories. What was written on those two pieces of paper? And why was the money cut off only after everything was signed and sealed? I will dig into the real reasons one by one. Before we get into the story, hitting subscribe and like would be a huge help. The story begins in Algeria in 2008. Algeria is a country rich in natural gas. They lay pipelines across the Mediterranean floor to send gas to Spain and Italy. It's a structure where money flows in as long as the valves are open. But the Algerian government hadn't liked this method for a long time. Countries that sell raw gas cannot set their own prices. When international market prices drop, revenue decreases even if the same amount is sent. So, Algeria decided not to just ship the gas, but to make and sell products using it. That product is fertilizer. To farm, you have to add nitrogen to the soil. The most common way to add nitrogen is through small white pellets called urea . Here is how urea is made. Hydrogen extracted from natural gas is used to create ammonia, which is then hardened into pellets. The raw material is natural gas. Countries with cheap natural gas can produce urea at the lowest cost. In March 2008, a company was established in Algiers, the capital of Algeria. It was a joint venture between the Algerian state-owned company Sonatrach and the Suhail Bahwan Group from Oman. Sonatrach is essentially a combination of Korea Gas Corporation and an oil refinery. The Suhail Bahwan Group is one of the leading conglomerates in Oman. Oman was to provide the capital, and Algeria was to provide the gas. The stake was 51% for Oman and 49%for Sonatrach. The company name is very long, but it means a fertilizer company created by Algeria and Oman, so we will call it the Algerian-Omani Fertilizer Company. The following month, the company selected a contractor to build the plant. Japan's Mitsubishi Heavy Industries and Korea's Daewoo E&C teamed up and won the bid. The work was split with Mitsubishi Heavy Industries taking 74%and Daewoo E &C 26%. Both companies managed everything from design to trial operations together. However, the physical work of excavating, erecting steel structures, and laying pipes fell mostly to Daewoo E&C. The total construction cost was $ 2.543 billion. That is over 3.5 trillion Korean won. Let me tell you where the plant was located. It was in the Arzew industrial zone, next to the port city of Oran in western Algeria. It is not in a desert; it faces the Mediterranean Sea. Looking at the sea from the pier, you see oil tankers, and turning back, you see an industrial complex with pipelines. It was land where they could load the produced pellets directly onto ships. At the time, it was one of the largest in the world. It was equipped with two ammonia units and two urea units. Combined, that is 4,000 tons of ammonia and 7,000 tons of urea per day. 7,000 tons of urea means 280 truckloads of 25 tons are produced every single day. It was a top-tier fertilizer plant built as a single project. The construction period was set at 43 months. That is 3 years and 7 months. The plan was to hand over the plant within 11 years. However, things did not go according to plan. Construction stopped in 2011. And it remained at a standstill for three years. But there is something strange here. The side that halted construction was not the builder. The two owners funding the plant had a falling out among themselves. In 2011, the site was about halfway finished. That was when Sonatrach, the Algerian state-owned oil company, raised an issue. They claimed that the fundamental terms of the contract they had signed three years prior were wrong. According to reports, there were two specific points they took issue with. One was the price of the gas. The contract stated that Sonatrach would supply natural gas to the plant at a price lower than the market rate. Since natural gas is the raw material for a urea plant, the price of the gas determines almost all of the plant’s potential earnings. The structure was such that the cheaper the gas, the more the plant earned, while the gas supplier received that much less. The other issue was the equity stake. In the company that owned this plant, the Omani side held a larger stake than the Algerian side. When you combine those two factors, it looks like this: It is a plant built on Algerian soil using Algerian gas. Yet, the one supplying the cheap gas was Algeria, while the one taking the larger share of the profits was Oman. But there is more to the story regarding this equity. The year after the contract was signed, in 2009, the Algerian government created a new regulation. It was a rule stating that incoming foreign capital could not hold more than half of the stake. This contract had been signed about a year before that regulation came into effect . Terms that would have been overturned had they been just a little later remained in the contract as they were. Sonatrach demanded a renegotiation of the contract, while the Omani side insisted that it had already been signed. Then, construction came to a halt. It happened at the most unfortunate moment. The heavy work of leveling the ground and erecting the steel frame was already finished, and it was just time to install the machinery and connect the piping. However, Daewoo E&C and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries had no place in this dispute. They were the builders, not the owners of the plant, so if the clients couldn't reach a conclusion, they had to sit on their hands and wait . That state of affairs went on for over three years. The half-finished plant stood there for three years, exposed to the Mediterranean winds. Rust began to form on the steel structures that had been erected. The machinery that arrived by ship remained wrapped and stored in crates. Only the sea breeze passed through the structures where no one climbed anymore . Still, someone had to remain to guard the site, and even though construction had stopped, the money kept flowing out . Three years is enough time for a new recruit to get promoted to assistant manager. They spent that time beside a half-built plant. During those three years, the company Daewoo E&C itself was being shaken in Korea. The Kumho Asiana Group, which had acquired the company in 2006, saw that acquisition shake the entire group, and Daewoo E&C was transferred to the Korea Development Bank. A company whose ownership was changing was left holding a half-built plant on the other side of the globe. The stalemate was not resolved until three years later. In September 2014, the two parties sat down in Arzew again to revise the agreement. They revised the clause for selling gas at a low price and changed the profit-sharing method to increase Algeria's share. After that, the site began to operate again. They cleared away rusted iron structures and repainted them. They took the machinery out of storage and unwrapped it. The cranes that had been stalled began to rise again. And in that same month of September 2014, Daewoo E&C received a document from the client. It was a commissioning approval certificate. It was a document in which the client approved the operation of the plant. Domestic reports state that with this document, Daewoo E&C's construction was practically finished, and subsequent processes were handled by Mitsubishi Heavy Industries. The plant was completely handed over in January 2017. The company that had entered the empty land in Arzew handed over a plant standing on that land nine years later. Three of those years were spent doing nothing and standing idle. Then, from December of that year, the plant began to operate in earnest. Gas from the Mediterranean began to flow in. White granules poured out. A ship carrying those granules left the port of Arzew. The picture that Algeria had envisioned in 2008 finally became a reality. However, even as that ship was leaving, there was still unpaid money remaining on Daewoo E&C's books. The calculation to determine that amount had already been finished in 2017. Large construction projects are just like interior renovations; once finished, you sit down to settle the final balance and agree not to dispute it further. Daewoo E&C, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, and the Algeria-Oman Fertilizer Company finished that calculation in 2017 and signed a document together. It is a document called the Comprehensive Settlement Agreement. It was a document that cleared all the delays and additional costs of the past nine years and finalized the amount. The share Daewoo E&C was to receive from that was 53.82 million dollars. Now there was only one thing left to do. For the client to pay the money they had written down. But they did not pay it. By that time, the site was already empty. The people had all left, the cranes and equipment were removed, and the office was closed down . The plant was in the client's hands and running, and all that remained on Daewoo E&C's books was a single number written as "money to be received." This is the situation that construction companies working abroad fear the most. If it were a local apartment, the built house would remain even if payment wasn't received, but a plant built on foreign soil cannot be dismantled and loaded onto a ship. Once you hand over the completed facility, all you have left in your hands is the right to claim payment. Even so, Daewoo E&C did not immediately file for arbitration. They waited for two more years. Algeria is a country where our construction firms have worked for many years. Once an international dispute begins, no one knows when it will end. After two years passed, in July 2019, the two companies finally filed for arbitration. It had been 11 years since the contract was signed. If a Korean company and an Algerian company clash, which country's court should they go to? That is why such contracts state in advance that they will go to the International Chamber of Commerce in Paris. The ruling issued there can be enforced across borders. However, it is not a light decision for a construction firm to take a client to arbitration. This is because they need to win future projects in that country as well. The fact that they did it anyway means there was no other way. What Daewoo E&C brought to that tribunal were two pieces of paper. One was the commissioning approval certificate signed by the client in 2014. The other was the comprehensive settlement agreement they both stamped in 2017. The client was the one who approved the plant's operation and specified the payment amount. So, Daewoo E&C's argument could have been summed up in a single line. "Just do what you wrote down." But two years later, an unexpected document was brought to the tribunal. It was March 12, 2021. The Algerian fertilizer company declared that the 2017 agreement they had both stamped was invalid. Refusing to follow an agreement is different from claiming the agreement never existed. The former is breaking a promise, while the latter claims the promise was never established to begin with. It means they weren't going to re-examine why the construction took nine years or what the original cost should have been . And this is the part that is truly infuriating. The client filed a counterclaim. The first demand was to return the money they had already paid. The 103.1 million dollars that Daewoo E &C had received. That is 117.2 billion won in our currency. For projects taking years, payments are made in installments as progress is made, and they demanded that all that money be returned. The second was a claim for damages. The reported scale of the claim was 810.7 billion won. Daewoo E& C's due share was about 82 billion won at the current exchange rate. The client’s claim for damages was 810.7 billion won. If they had to return the progress payments on top of that, Daewoo E&C would have to pay back everything it received and even more. It was a calculation that left them in debt after spending nine years building the plant. The plant that took nine years to build became a plant that was never built overnight. Only those who have been in that position would understand what the people who received that bill were thinking at the time. But the more you look into this claim, the less it adds up. First of all, the plant that they claimed suffered losses was running perfectly fine at the time. Since December 2017, it had been producing urea and shipping it out by boat. By March 2021, when the claim was filed, it had already been making money for the company for over three years. And the party that gave the written approval to run that plant was the client itself. They were the ones who signed off on it in 2014 and signed again in 2017 promising payment. They were effectively denying the very document they had written themselves. Furthermore, it was someone within the client's side who halted construction for over three years. The site stopped because the two major shareholders had a falling out over gas prices and equity stakes. Yet, they were still making some money from that plant during that time. Of course, this cannot be taken as a statement about the country of Algeria as a whole. This is a matter of the strategy one company chose to take in court. It took the arbitrators seven years to look into that situation. International arbitration is not about arguing verbally; it’s mostly about documents , and in this case, those were nine years 'worth of construction records. Why the construction stopped must be shown through official correspondence from back then, and what test results were produced during trial runs must be shown through the records measured at the time. So, the strength that Daewoo E&C held onto for seven years was the paperwork left behind by the people on-site back then. If the client tells them to halt construction today, they get that order in writing. Every time a piece of machinery is installed, they take photos and conduct inspections, and both sides sign on the spot. At the time, it’s just a procedure, and at the end of the day, it’s just a tedious task. Those papers protected the company 18 years later. The side that is aggrieved doesn't win; the side that wrote things down back then wins. And it is June 19, 2024. Daewoo E&C won . Two pieces of paper won. The arbitrators saw the 2017 agreement as a valid document and ruled that the client must pay the money written in it . They acknowledged the full $ 53.82 million owed to Daewoo E&C without cutting a single cent. The demands to return the 117.2 billion won in progress payments and the claims for damages were all dismissed. The only thing acknowledged was a single point that part of the consortium’s facilities was not executed according to the contract. That amount was $ 5,782,472. In our currency, that is approximately 8.8 billion won. However, this 8.8 billion won was the amount charged to the entire Mitsubishi Heavy Industries and Daewoo E&C consortium. The company announced that the amount Daewoo E&C would have to bear is zero won. They were trying to avoid paying 82 billion won by invalidating the 2017 agreement. But that agreement became a valid document, and the 82 billion won was not reduced by a single penny. Their attempt to hold out and not pay came back as a ruling that they must pay everything. So, what has become of that factory now? That Arzew plant is running quite well. According to Algerian government data, it produces 1.3 million tons of ammonia and 2.4 million tons of urea annually. And more than 90%of those products are shipped to Europe, the Americas, and Asia. In August 2026, they agreed with the Algerian energy ministry to expand the plant by 50%. They plan to build an additional production line for ammonia and urea. The Algerian energy minister, the CEO of Sonatrach, and the chairman of the Omani group sat together there. It wasn't a Korean who made that announcement. The Algerian government itself stated that the factory stands at the heart of what they export besides oil and gas. But not getting paid after completing construction was nothing new for our builders. The same thing happened 40 years ago. In the 1980s, Daewoo E&C carried out 130 projects in Libya worth over 7 billion dollars. But as oil prices fell, the Libyan government's ability to pay deteriorated. The construction was finished, but the money didn't come in. The outstanding balance at the time is known to have been 230 million dollars. It remained on the books for a long time as a long-term bad debt. Just looking at the result of not getting paid after building it, it mirrors the Algerian case. Of course, the reasons were different. In Libya's case, the client didn't have the money, whereas in Algeria, they were fighting over payment. And there is one more difference between then and now. Back then, there was no proper way to collect the debt. The counterparty was a nation, the contract was on their terms, and there was nothing to do but wait. Forty years later, when the same company faced a similar situation, it was different. They took the case to arbitration in Paris, compiled nine years of records in English, and persisted for seven years to win. Meanwhile, Korea's overseas construction industry surpassed 1 trillion dollars in cumulative orders by the end of 2024. It became the third sector to hit 1 trillion dollars after semiconductors and automobiles. Of course, there is still a long way to go . A ruling doesn't mean the money instantly lands in the bank account. The client could appeal, and the actual collection process still remains. Daewoo E&C has also stated it will actively respond if the client files a lawsuit. There are still mountains to climb. It took nine years to build the plant on that empty lot in Arzew. And it took another nine years to collect the payment for it. That’s 18 years in total.

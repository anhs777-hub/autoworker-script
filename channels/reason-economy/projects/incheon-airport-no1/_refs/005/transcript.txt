On August 13th, Incheon International Airport Corporation quietly released a report card. The aviation industry was stunned to see the name at the top of the leaderboard. In the first half of this year, 38.39 million international passengers passed through Incheon Airport. It was number one among 1,234 airports worldwide, according to preliminary tallies by the Airports Council International. Second place was the famous London Heathrow, and third was Singapore Changi. New York, Frankfurt, and even Dubai, which held the crown for 20 years, were all ranked below Incheon. For the first time since the airport opened, a South Korean airport has stood above all others on Earth. However, for those who remember how this airport started, this ranking might feel a bit bewildering. Twenty-five years ago, this place was a West Sea tidal flat that turned into mudflats at low tide, and 65%of the public opposed the construction in polls at the time. The nation wondered if an airport could even function on a mudflat. Furthermore, just five years ago during COVID-19, 96%of this airport's passenger traffic evaporated. It was an airport with nothing but lights left on in empty departure halls . Today, that is exactly the story we are telling. How did a tidal flat airport that everyone opposed survive two harsh winters to become the world's busiest gateway, and what do those 25 years have to do with your travels and your paycheck? Your support helps us create better videos. Let's head to the waters off the coast of Incheon in November 1992. Amidst the winter sea breeze, trucks begin pouring rocks into the ocean. It was construction to build a seawall in the water between Yeongjongdo and Yongyudo islands. It was the first shovelful of earth for what would eventually become Incheon Airport. The plan was to connect the two islands with a seawall, fill the tidal flat in between to create land, and build an airport on top. It was easier said than done; the idea was to reclaim the sea to build the nation's gateway. The roots of the story go back to 1989. That year, overseas travel was completely liberalized. Until then, it was a country where it was difficult for an ordinary person to even get a passport. Once the barriers were lifted, travelers increased explosively, and the airways of a nation that had just hosted the Olympics quickly became cramped. Why did it have to be the sea? It was because Gimpo Airport, the gateway at the time, had hit its limits . There was no room for more runways, and being attached to the city, noise complaints meant flights couldn't freely take off at night. It was difficult for a growing nation's gateway to be an airport that had to close its doors every night. So the answer found was land in the sea, away from homes, where planes could land and take off 24/7. The logic sounded plausible, but public opinion was cold. Records show that 65%of people opposed it in surveys at the time. Only 18%were in favor. The reasons for opposition were varied. What if the reclaimed tidal land sinks? Isn't it too far from Seoul? Why not use that money for something else? An airport on a foggy coast? What are you thinking? It sounds funny now, but those were serious concerns back then. There were reasons for that. Back then, the standard for air travel was Japan. Tokyo's Narita, Osaka's Kansai—the hub airports of Northeast Asia. In other words, Japanese airports had already secured the spot as transit hubs for international passengers. It was a time when Koreans naturally transferred in Japan to reach the US or Europe. Gimpo Airport was already saturated, Hong Kong was building Chek Lap Kok, and China was raising Pudong Airport in Shanghai. Everyone was reclaiming land and laying runways, vying for the gateway to Asia. As a latecomer, Korea planned a massive airport on mudflats, so even domestically, people were skeptical, let alone globally. Moreover , there were scary precedents worldwide . In 1995, Denver Airport delayed its opening by 16 months and earned the nickname "Baggage Hell" after its advanced system chewed up luggage. In 1998, Hong Kong’s ambitious Chek Lap Kok Airport suffered a massive disaster on its first day as computer glitches piled up baggage and stranded passengers. It was a time when newly built sea-reclaimed airports were suffering opening day fiascos and international embarrassment. Naturally, the world’s eyes were fixed on us. They thought Incheon would be next in line. Construction continued for eight years and four months amidst that doubt . Even the Asian Financial Crisis struck in the middle. Even during that winter when the nation was collecting gold, construction on the mudflats never stopped. We blocked the sea with seawalls, filled the tidal flats to create land nearly 20 times the size of Yeouido, and built runways and terminals. The cost was 7.8 trillion won. It was a massive gamble for the 1990s, described by the media as the greatest project since Dangun. What happened to the fears that the land would sink? There was almost no settlement after opening, and results show future expected settlement is less than 5cm. The runways built on the mudflats held up perfectly. The problem wasn't just the airport itself. Since it's built on an island, it’s useless without a bridge connecting it to the mainland. So, we had to build the Yeongjong Bridge, over 4km long, across the West Sea first. It was a grand undertaking that required filling in the sea, building bridges over the water, and even laying railroad tracks just to open a single airport. And on March 29, 2001, Incheon International Airport opened its doors. It was an airport born amidst 65%public opposition. That is the story of its birth. Now, it is time to see how this airport caught up to its predecessors. Let’s take a look at the scene on the night before the opening. On the night of March 28, 2001, an unusual procession stretched along the road from Gimpo Airport toward Incheon. With aircraft towing vehicles, maintenance equipment, and even the moving luggage of customs and immigration offices, it was a massive relocation operation to move the nation's gateway overnight. When the sun rose, international flights at Gimpo had to close, and Incheon had to open. There was no rehearsal. Those who knew the nightmares of Denver and Hong Kong held their breath. Then, on the morning of March 29, the first passenger plane landed gracefully on the Incheon runway . There was no baggage chaos, nor any computer system failure. It was such a quiet and ordinary first day that it even made the worried foreign media feel sheepish. In the world of airports , an "ordinary day" is the highest compliment. The newcomer had cleanly cleared the threshold of the opening day, where its predecessors had stumbled one after another. After opening, Incheon Airport made a name for itself much faster than expected. It had two weapons. One was its location. Within a three-and-a-half-hour flight from Incheon, there are 43 cities with populations exceeding one million. The runway was placed right in the heart of East Asia's population center. The other was its service. In 2005, only four years after opening, Incheon climbed to first place in the world’s top airport service evaluations. The rookie that once feared an opening-day disaster had surpassed the world in service within just four years. After that, it began writing a legend. Ranked number one in the world for airport service for 12 consecutive years. Eventually, it became a common sight for airport officials from other countries to visit Incheon for study tours. The newcomer that once went around to learn had become the textbook . An airport that is fast for immigration, convenient for transfers, and clean. It was no empty talk when those who had traveled abroad unanimously said there was nowhere like Incheon. Then, the flow of the skies began to change. The transfer business connecting the Americas, Europe, and Southeast Asia began to take off in earnest. Foreigners who used to transfer at Narita began transferring at Incheon. Incheon’s trump card for transfers was, in particular, North America. By offering the most robust selection of direct flights to the U.S. and Canada in Asia, it began ferrying passengers from all over Asia across the Pacific through Incheon. Eventually , a scene even emerged where Japanese people living in provincial cities in Japan would go through Incheon, rather than Tokyo, when traveling to the U.S. An era where Japanese passengers transfer at a Korean airport. It was a turnaround that Koreans buying tickets from Gimpo to Narita 30 years ago could never have imagined. It wasn't that there weren't any crises. In 2003, SARS , in 2008, the financial crisis, and in 2015, MERS all caused the skies to shrink. Every time, the passenger graph took a dip, but Incheon’s growth curve lifted its head again every few months. It was an airport whose growth plates never broke, even through minor illnesses. It also continued to increase its size. In early 2018, just before guests from around the world poured in for the PyeongChang Winter Olympics, Terminal 2 opened. With a two-terminal system, the airport grew once more. That year, 2018, Incheon climbed to 5th place in the world for international passengers. It was a dazzling chase for an airport built on reclaimed land. An airport built on a mudflat was growing into a gateway to the planet. For reference, cargo had already reached the top tier globally even before passengers. Thanks to light and expensive Korean exports like semiconductors and phones, Incheon vied for 2nd or 3rd place in global cargo from the start. Passenger traffic was essentially the second crown placed upon this cargo king. But in 2020, the skies closed down completely. It was COVID-19. That year, Incheon Airport’s international passengers dropped by 83%to 11.95 million. That number was even lower than the airport's first year of operation. The following year was even worse. In 2021, international passengers numbered 3.2 million. Compared to 2019, before COVID , only 3.6%remained. It was as if only three and a half people passed through a terminal that used to see 100. Transfer passengers, once Incheon’s pride, shrank to 330,000. The entire ecosystem that relied on the airport froze over. Duty-free shops couldn't pay rent and gave up, and airline staff received notices of unpaid leave. Passenger planes with nowhere to go were lined up in long rows along the runway. An airport turned into an airplane parking lot. It got so bad that they even launched "flights to nowhere," where planes just took off, circled, and landed without going anywhere. It was a funny scene from a time when people wanted a departure stamp but had nowhere to go. The duty-free zone became like a row of dark shops, and the entire aviation industry was wailing. An airport that had climbed to 5th in the world had effectively become a closed-door business. If the skepticism during the reclamation phase was the first winter, this was the second. And a much harsher winter at that. Even in the midst of that, the airport fulfilled its role as the country's quarantine gateway. Charter flights bringing citizens home landed, and vaccines and test kits moved through these runways. The airport, empty of passengers, was working in a different way. But here’s the thing. Inside this empty airport, another strange thing was happening. The sounds of construction never stopped. Incheon Airport had been undergoing a Phase 4 expansion project since 2018. It was a massive project to expand the terminal, lay a fourth runway, and enlarge the apron and parking areas. But then, COVID-19 hit. With passenger numbers plummeting by 96 %, critics rightly asked why we were spending trillions to expand the airport when there were no travelers. However, construction continued, and the Phase 4 project was completed in December 2024. With this, Incheon Airport became capable of handling 106 million passengers per year. 106 million is a capacity that could accommodate the entire population of South Korea flying twice a year. Incheon is the first airport in the world to have two mega-terminals that can each handle over 50 million passengers annually. Four runways and 6.3 million tons of cargo capacity. During the winter when there were no guests, they had prepared one of the largest vessels on Earth. The guts it took to break ground on the tidal flat despite 65%public opposition were repeated 30 years later in an empty airport. And as the skies opened up again, the vessel began to fill. International passenger numbers rose from 55.76 million in 2023 to 70.7 million in 2024, a 26.7%increase. It was the fastest recovery rate among the world's top five airports. That year, Incheon reached 3rd place in the world for the first time. And in the first half of this year, it finally hit 2nd place with 38.39 million passengers. Domestic media outlets covered the news like this. "Overtook Dubai, the king of 20 years." An airport built on a tidal flat surpassed Dubai, as well as Heathrow and Changi. To put that speed into perspective, it took Singapore Changi 35 years, Tokyo Narita 39 years, and Dubai 58 years to reach the same 1 billion mark.

Recently, the cumulative number of passengers at Incheon International Airport surpassed 1 billion. This means that one in eight people worldwide—an average of 108,000 daily users, or 4,513 per hour—used Incheon Airport, marking the fastest record among major airports globally. Furthermore, in the first quarter of this year, it even ranked first in the world for international passenger numbers. Just when we thought there was nothing but celebration left, there are reports that Incheon Airport could soon lose its title as a hub airport to China or Japan. Incheon Airport opened in 2001 with the goal of becoming a hub for the East. Incheon International Airport, constructed with the aim of serving as the central hub of Eastern Asia in the 21st century, officially opened today. Wait. The central hub airport mentioned here is a transit center on the Korea-Japan route connecting various countries; it plays a pivotal role as a place where people and cargo gather before expanding globally. Looking at the path Incheon Airport has taken to establish itself as a hub airport for the East, it has evolved from a large-scale logistics complex development in 2005 to a leap forward as a logistics hub, to a significant upgrade in 2008 by expanding airport infrastructure such as boarding areas and runways, and a massive infrastructure expansion centered around Terminal 2 in 2024. As a result, it has become the world's third-largest airport capable of accommodating 106 million passengers. Furthermore, Incheon Airport is playing a pivotal role in Northeast Asia, notably with Delta Airlines—the world's top-performing airline by revenue— relocating its Asian hub from Narita Airport in Japan to Incheon. However, there is a concern that this airport may soon cease to function properly. This is because Incheon Airport is expected to reach saturation by 2033. According to the Incheon International Airport Corporation, the projected annual passenger volume in seven years is approximately 111 million. This figure far exceeds the current capacity of 106 million, and seizing this opportunity, China and Japan are eyeing the position of the East's hub airport. First, China is pushing forward with sheer scale by establishing a strategy to expand three locations—37 N, Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou—into international hub airports, seven locations as hubs connecting neighboring regions, and N locations as cargo hubs. In particular, it is aggressively expanding Beijing Daxin Airport, already one of the world's largest single-terminal airports, with the goal of turning it into a mega-hub. Shanghai Pudong Airport is also expanding to accommodate an additional 50 million passengers per year by constructing a new Terminal 3 and baggage handling system. Japan is no pushover either. The large-scale expansion project at Narita Airport is said to be so full-scale that it is being called the "opening of the sun." Upon completion, the number of landings will increase from the current level of 340,000 per year to 500,000, catching up to 83%of Incheon Airport's landing volume. Once this capacity is absorbed, it will be impossible to handle new passengers or further expand the network; therefore, if such demand cannot be processed in Korea, it will naturally shift to Japan or China. With even Hong Kong’s Chek Naap Airport and Singapore’s Chang Yu Airport expanding their scale and the competition for the Asian hub title becoming increasingly fierce, what exactly is a hub airport that is causing such a stir everywhere? It goes beyond simply thinking of it as a terminal where planes carry passengers; it plays a significant role as massive infrastructure that generates national competitiveness and economic growth. It becomes an economic hub where people, global companies, and logistics converge. Therefore, you can view possessing a global hub airport as serving as a gateway for the exchange of people and goods between nations. Ultimately, becoming such a gateway leads to an influx of tourists. For a country like ours, which is positioned at the center of global supply, having a hub airport is crucial for boosting national competitiveness. If, hypothetically, we were to lose the hub airport status to another country, would there be any tangible changes we could experience? Our international flight networks might shrink compared to now, or the number of countries we can travel to would likely decrease. For example, if direct flights from Incheon Airport to Europe are reduced, passengers will have to transit through hub airports in China or Japan that offer direct service. Naturally, this will take longer and airfares will become more expensive, but that is not the end of it. It is said that overseas direct purchasing, which we use frequently, could also be affected. The reason we receive packages quickly in Korea is that Incheon Airport has a highly developed network. Recently, there have been plans to build "international shipping centers," which are specifically designed for direct purchases. These centers are being established to bring in goods, store them, and then ship them worldwide. These centers are attempting to enter because Incheon Airport is a hub; if it were not a hub, they wouldn't enter because there would be no connecting flights. Consequently, the delivery times for these shipments could be significantly delayed or additional costs could be incurred. Shouldn't we avoid losing this hub airport title? Can Incheon Airport just sit idly by? That is why plans are emerging to expand Incheon Airport's Phase 5 construction project, build Terminal 3, and expand facilities such as the fifth runway and parking lots. There are many airport projects that take at least 10 years from the very beginning, involving feasibility studies, design, and construction. There isn't much time left until the 33-year mark. The point is that we must push forward before missing this golden opportunity. In fact, there was a precedent in 2012 when the Incheon Airport Phase 3 construction project was delayed by two years, causing significant chaos. China and Japan are already developing these airports under the name "Mega Huff." Rather than simply saying that luxury has occurred, they are pushing to become global hubs. Since they are already aware that the airport will eventually reach saturation, the thinking is that to meet that timeline and avoid losing the initiative, we must start planning and development quickly now. However, the current situation is that not only is there no start date for the Incheon Airport expansion project, but it hasn't even been decided whether or not to expand it. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport's position is that aviation demand must be examined more cautiously, as the construction of regional airports currently underway—such as the Gadeokdo New Airport, the Daegu-Gyeongbu Integrated New Airport, and the Seemangeum Airport—could disperse passengers from Incheon Airport. I view airport development as being driven by the government's recent push for balanced regional development. Making a large-scale investment in Incheon Airport would likely place a financial burden on the government, and it could also spark various forms of local opposition. With attention now focused on whether the Phase 5 expansion project will be included in the upcoming 7th Comprehensive Airport Development Plan, will Incheon Airport be able to maintain its status as the hub airport for the East Coast?

Japan, once the world's hottest electronics powerhouse, now finds itself in a very sorry state. There was certainly a time when the world bowed down to names like Sony, Panasonic, and Sharp. But that vast empire ended up handing over its home turf to South Korea, a poor nation it once looked down upon. What on earth actually happened? Surprisingly, the answer, everyone, was hidden in that tiny outlet hole on your wall. It was around 1973. Back then, South Korea was a truly poor country with a per capita income of only 400 dollars. Yet, this poor nation suddenly began a seemingly reckless gamble. It was the decision to completely overhaul every outlet in the country. It took 32 years and cost 1.4 trillion won. A grand journey involving 7.57 million people in total. A national project that sounded completely insane—tearing down walls across the nation to switch from 110V to 220 V—was born. Meanwhile, Japan, the world’s second-largest economy at the time, chose a very comfortable path . They decided to stick with the 100V system that was working just fine. 50 years later, the fates of the two nations have been completely reversed. Samsung and LG have ascended to the throne of the global home appliance market, while Japan's appliance sector has collapsed one by one. But there is one thing we must remember here. The real twist of this story is just beginning. I will now reveal, piece by piece, the surprising hidden story of how a single outlet determined the fate of the semiconductor, EV, and AI eras. Now, to tell this story properly, we must first take a brief time travel trip to the early 1970s. The world’s power map back then was completely different from what it is today. The U.S. used 110V, Japan used 100V, and our South Korea was also using 110V. Only Europe was uniquely using 220V. In short, most countries in the world were living quite harmoniously with low voltage. Let's briefly touch upon what voltage is before we move on. There is absolutely no need to think of it as difficult. Just imagine a water pipe. When sending water through the same pipe, higher pressure allows you to send more water much more efficiently in the same amount of time. Electricity works on exactly the same principle. With higher voltage, you can send much more electricity through the same wire with much less loss. What happens if the voltage is low, then? The amount of electricity lost as heat while traveling through the wires becomes immense. No matter how much the power plant generates, it’s like it’s leaking away before it ever reaches your home. It is at this point that the real protagonist of our story appears. Dr. Han Man-choon. He was South Korea’s first Ph.D. in electrical engineering. He is a person with a truly impressive background. In 1943, he was a member of the very first graduating class of the Electrical Engineering department at Keijo Imperial University, and right after liberation, he served as the power generation manager at Chosun Electric, the predecessor to today’s KEPCO. In 1961, he became the nation’s first government-sponsored overseas student in nuclear energy, going to the University of Nottingham in the UK to earn his doctorate. He was, quite literally, a living encyclopedia of South Korean electrical engineering at the time. But then, one day in 1968, this man sat down with a piece of paper and began to calculate. It was for a paper to be published in a magazine called Science and Technology. The title was "The Economic Feasibility of Increasing Distribution Voltage." Just hearing the title, it sounds like a paper that would make you yawn, right? But that single paper would later go on to shake the entire nation. The question Dr. Han posed was this: We are in such a frenzy because of a power shortage, is there no other way besides building more power plants? That was when he discovered a very interesting fact. Surprisingly, a massive amount of electricity was just disappearing as heat from our country’s power lines. A significant portion of the electricity produced with such effort at the power plants was being lost before it even reached people's homes. So, when Dr. Han ran the numbers, a truly startling figure emerged. If you simply double the voltage, the current flowing through the wires drops by half , and the electricity lost to leakage is reduced by a factor of four. Just how significant was the result? It was equivalent to building an entirely new power plant. Without pouring a single bag of cement or laying a single brick, simply raising the voltage had the same effect as constructing a whole new power plant. It was a truly brilliant idea. Dr. Han quietly presented these findings to the world. But I want you to remember this specific point. Because this one-page calculation would become the spark for a massive event that would turn South Korea upside down for 32 years starting five years later. And now, the real story of how that spark grew into a wildfire begins. So, let’s head back together to South Korea in 1973. You need to understand the situation our country was in back then to grasp how absurd the decision that followed really was. In 1973, South Korea’s per capita national income was a mere $ 400. At that time, Japan had already surpassed $ 3,900 and the US was well over $ 6,700. We were at almost one-tenth of that level. We were truly among the poorest of the developing nations. The situation with electricity was even more miserable. Electricity hadn't even reached every corner of the country yet. There were still many homes in rural areas using oil lamps, and even in cities, blackouts happened all the time. It was a time when the whole country was celebrating because power rationing had been lifted just a few years prior. In a country like this, the government suddenly makes a bombshell announcement . I’m going to replace every single outlet in the country. How did people react? It caused quite a stir. A country that can’t afford a power plant, why change voltage? We’re just getting electricity, why rip out perfectly good systems? If we toss all our 110V appliances, what happens to the common folk? Honestly, you can see why they opposed it. Back then, how precious was that one fan or rice cooker in a kitchen? The news that they’d be rendered useless must have been a bolt from the blue. Yet, the government pushed ahead. The government began the historic first conversion project for 3,000 households in Gangwon-do. It was small in scale, but the first move in a massive 32-year journey. Let me show you the sheer scale of this project in numbers. Total investment: 1.4 trillion won. Total personnel involved: a staggering 750,000. That’s 24,000 people a year for 32 years straight. Target households: 17.53 million nationwide. Simply put, they tore into walls, rewired, and replaced outlets in almost every home in Korea. Doesn’t quite sink in, right? Let me give you a comparison. Building the KTX line from Seoul to Busan took about 20 years. This voltage conversion took even longer—32 years—visiting every single home to overhaul them. This wasn’t just a construction project. It was a national mission. A decision to completely overhaul an entire generation’s infrastructure. But there is a very interesting fact here. The government didn’t just recklessly force it through. They devised a very clever strategy. New areas were wired for 220V, while existing ones temporarily supplied both voltages. So, it wasn't telling everyone overnight to throw their appliances away. Instead, they gave people time to transition slowly and smoothly. Without this flexible approach, the project would have collapsed long ago. Now, here is the thing. During this massive undertaking, what was Dr. Han Man-chun doing? He didn’t just write papers and step aside. Before the conversion, he developed "Yonsei-1," Korea’s first analog computer, in 1961. In an era when computers were foreign, he built one with our own hands. He was truly the father of electrical engineering in Korea. He passed away in 1984. Regrettably, he closed his eyes before seeing the project he seeded reach completion. But the seeds he planted were quietly taking root in the world. And finally, in November 2005, exactly 32 years after breaking ground in Gangwon-do, South Korea completed a project unparalleled anywhere in the world. The results were truly staggering. Without building a single new facility, power capacity doubled, and transmission and distribution losses dropped to 4.5%, reaching the world's lowest level. Four billion kilowatt-hours of electricity began to be saved every single year. To put that into perspective for you. That is a massive amount of electricity, enough to power the entire island of Jeju for a year and a half with plenty to spare. But the true power of this project wasn't just saving on electricity bills . The real twist was happening somewhere completely different. On a stage we never even expected. It was the home appliance market. Now, this is where it gets really interesting. Let me explain simply why 220V is advantageous for home appliances. Remember the water pipe analogy from before? Higher voltage means you can send much more electricity in the same amount of time. That means it is much more advantageous for creating high-power, large home appliances. Air conditioners, large refrigerators, front-loading washing machines, induction cooktops, kimchi refrigerators, and large microwave ovens. These heavy-duty appliances we use every day actually run much better in a 220V environment. To get the same performance with 100V, you’d need an insane amount of current, which makes wires hot, requires thicker wiring, and often trips the circuit breaker. So, for Samsung Electronics and LG Electronics, what did a South Korea standardized to 220V mean? It was, quite simply, the ultimate training ground. They honed their skills by freely creating, testing, and selling large, high-performance appliances in the domestic market. There is one absolutely crucial fact here. Do you know what voltage most of the world's population uses? Surprisingly, Europe, China, India, Southeast Asia, most of the Middle East, and Africa all use the 220V to 230V range. Only countries like the U.S. and Japan are trapped in the 100V range. What that means is that once Samsung and LG finished their training at home, they could enter markets where the majority of the world's population lives. On the other hand, Japan's Sony and Panasonic? To make large appliances, they had to design and verify 100V models for domestic use and 220V models for export separately. The additional burden on development, production lines, and inventory management kept piling up. What would happen if this accumulated over 30 or 40 years? The gap was bound to become insurmountable. Now, the real highlight of this story begins here. Up until the 1990s, Japanese home appliances were truly in a league of their own. Sony Walkman, Panasonic TVs, Sharp calculators. Before these names, the whole world simply knelt. There was a time when Korean travelers to Japan felt like they’d won a badge of honor by bringing back Zojirushi rice cookers or Sony camcorders. But as we entered the 2000s, this landscape began to shift. Samsung and LG started to sweep the markets for large TVs, large refrigerators, and front-loading washing machines one by one. The real knockout blow was the large flat-screen TV. While Sony was obsessed with CRT technology, Samsung seized the global market with LCDs and OLEDs. Looking at the figures, your jaw would hit the floor. As of the 2011 fiscal year-end, the combined losses of Sony, Panasonic, and Sharp reached a staggering 1.6 trillion yen. In Korean currency, that’s over 20 trillion won. Back then, the Financial Times used a specific phrase. "The fuse has blown on the Japanese electronics industry." That’s a truly chilling expression. Then, in 2016, a truly historic event occurred. Sharp, the pride of Japan with over 100 years of history, handed over its management rights to Taiwan’s Foxconn. Foxconn invested about 390 billion yen to acquire two-thirds of Sharp and made it a subsidiary. Japan had just handed over its national brand to a Taiwanese company it once considered inferior. This news of a 100-year-old national firm falling into foreign hands was a massive shock to the Japanese industry. Especially considering Japan once treated us as mere subcontractors in the Asian electronics market. A complete role reversal had taken place. Samsung and LG, who used to be the underdogs, became the bosses, while global leaders like Sony and Sharp had to fight for survival. But here, there’s one point we need to be honest about. Did a single electrical outlet really change everything? The truth is actually much more complex than that. Let me be honest with you. The real reason Japanese electronics collapsed wasn’t just about the 220V issue. In fact, there were much larger and more terrifying factors piled up behind it. The first was the famous Plaza Accord of 1985. The U.S. sat Japan down and essentially threatened them. "You need to significantly raise the value of the yen." Do you know what the result was? The exchange rate, which was 240 yen to the dollar, plummeted to the 120 yen range in just two to three years. The value of the yen had essentially doubled. Overnight, the price of Japanese products abroad effectively doubled. No matter how good a product is, it won't sell if the price doubles. This single blow caused the entire Japanese export industry to begin to falter. The second was the famous "Galapagos Syndrome." Japan had been building products that were tailored exclusively to its own domestic market. A prime example was the Japanese feature phone. With mobile payments, high-res cameras, and terrestrial TV, they packed in the world's best features, but none of them met international standards. So, they just wouldn't sell overseas. Like life evolving on the Galapagos Islands, they evolved in a strange way, isolated within the Japanese market. The third point is truly the most painful. It was a failure in digital transformation. Sony’s Walkman defined an entire era, didn't it? But the world had already moved on to MP3s and the iPod. To protect their cassette tape business, Sony handed the entire digital music market over to Apple. Trapped by their own obsession with hardware perfection, they missed out on the new world of software and services. Hearing this, you might be thinking: "So, 220V wasn't the decisive cause, right?" You're right. It wasn't the decisive cause. But it’s clear it was one of the definitively advantageous conditions. You need to understand this difference clearly. During those precarious times when Japan reeled from the Plaza Accord , became isolated, and failed to transition digitally—What if Japan had also used 220V? It might have been slightly easier to defend their global market share, at least in large appliances. But shackled to 100V, they had fewer places to escape to when the crisis hit. Conversely, because Korea had already laid a solid 220V runway, Samsung and LG could take off into the world using that very path. To sum it up: A single outlet didn't determine the fate of two nations. But between a country that painfully overhauled its infrastructure and one that kept things comfortable, a gap emerged after 30 to 40 years that simply couldn't be bridged. And this story carries a very important message for us today. The same principle will apply to the eras of semiconductors, EVs, and AI that we face ahead. The amount of electricity a single data center consumes right now is massive. To run a fast EV charger, 220V is nowhere near enough; you need much higher industrial power. The truly powerful nations of the future will be those that can supply this high-capacity power stably and efficiently. It’s a point worth reconsidering: just how massive an asset it is that Korea has already secured some of the world's lowest power transmission losses. And where did this all begin? It started in 1968, with a single line of calculation scribbled on paper by Dr. Han Man-chun in a quiet study. That one line of calculation has led to the status of our industry today. This isn't just a story about electrical outlets. It is a living story that shows how a nation prepares for its future, and how powerful the resolve to rebuild for the next generation can be, even if it hurts right now. It was a doctor in 1968, it was South Korea in 1973, and it was the touch of 7.57 million nameless hands that spent 32 years tearing down walls and replacing outlets. The two holes of the round outlet that we plug into every day without a second thought are actually quietly soaked in the countless decisions and sweat of half a century. The kimchi refrigerator on your dinner table tonight, the large TV filling your living room, and those major appliances proudly displaying Samsung and LG logos all around the world. None of this was ever a coincidence. I could ask for nothing more if today’s story has given you a chance to look back on the roots of our industry.

Every time the gates open at Incheon Airport, there’s a noticeable increase in Westerners pulling suitcases. In the first half of this year, this airport surpassed London Heathrow to rank first in the world for international passengers. The ones who put it in that spot are people who flocked here from the other side of the globe. They are the very same people from Europe and America who used to think of other places for Asian travel. But these people don't just stop by for a quick visit. Travelers from the US visit local bathhouses and order late-night deliveries, spending an average of 8.8 days here. What on earth are they coming to see? Eight days feels like quite a long time, doesn't it? Let's follow them from the morning to see how they fill that time. One of the places they head to on the morning after they arrive is a local bathhouse. This summer, the US broadcaster CNN even came to cover a large jjimjilbang in Busan in person. Looking at the guest composition mentioned in the article, visitors from Korea and those from abroad were nearly split fifty-fifty. Is this just a passing fad ? It’s hard to see it that way. According to figures from the company operating several such locations including that jjimjilbang, the proportion of foreign guests has increased 2.3 times in three years. Looking just at that one Busan location , nearly half of last year's customers were foreigners. Even getting a professional body scrub has now become a service you book in advance. According to the inbound booking platform Creatrip, individual scrub transaction volume in the first half of this year exceeded five times that of the same period last year. People from the other side of the world are paying and securing spots to have their dead skin scrubbed off in a bathhouse. When it’s daytime, these people mainly go around to eat. When asked what they did while in Korea, 80.3%of foreign visitors cited eating. Shopping, sightseeing in nature, and visiting museums were all pushed to the background. When asked what they liked the most, the number one answer was the same. Of course, there are famous restaurants, too. Six Korean restaurants made this year’s Asia’s 50 Best Restaurants list, and Mingles ranked fourth. But the places where these people actually spend most of their days are market alleys and hole-in-the-wall eateries where you can barely see the sign. In the evening, they fold up their maps and start walking. They pass by palaces, head into alleys, spend two hours in a market, and move to the next alley without much of a plan. And at dawn, they open their delivery apps. According to Baedal Minjok, the number of orders by foreigners in the first half of this year jumped 331%, with chicken being particularly popular. Travel usually follows a schedule. What time to see something, what time to move to the next location, and what time to leave for the next city. But the eight days you just saw resemble a lifestyle more than an itinerary. I'm talking about our daily timetable: washing in the morning, eating during the day, walking in the evening, and ordering food at dawn. Then, how many people are living their days like this right now? In the first half of this year, 1.71 million foreigners entered Korea. That is a 21.3%increase from the same period last year, and it is the highest number ever recorded for a half-year period. It hasn't just recovered to pre-COVID levels. Compared to the same period in 2019, when traffic was at its peak, it is actually 26.9%higher. However, what we really need to look at in this story isn't the total number. Among them, 203,000 people came from the Americas, Europe, Australia, and New Zealand. That's about one in five. Imagine a subway car during the morning commute. If there are five foreign tourists in that car, it means one of them flew for nearly half a day to get here. Until recently, most visitors to Korea came from nearby Asian countries. They came from a distance reachable within two or three hours by plane. That composition has quietly changed over the past few years . The age of incoming visitors has also dropped, with the share of those under 30 rising noticeably. And these people do not use travel agencies. 95.8%of those from the United States came with itineraries they planned themselves. It means they looked up everything themselves—where to sleep, what to eat, and which alleys to walk through at what time. It also means they are guests who found their own way here. If this pace continues, the number of foreigners visiting Korea this year will exceed 20 million. That is a milestone we have never reached before. So, the question narrows down to one thing. Why are so many people coming to a country that isn't close by and isn't cheap? The answer is divided into three parts. Their hearts arrived before they did, paths opened for those hearts to follow, and what they experienced here made them want to return. Let's start with the first one. Since when did the name "Korea" take root in the minds of people on the other side of the globe? Listening up to this point, you probably thought it was all about K-pop . You are right. That is exactly what it is. However, let's put aside the stories you already know and look at some lesser-known numbers. The most-watched show on Netflix last year was "K-Pop Demon Hunters." According to Netflix's own data, it was played 480 million times in just the second half of the year. As a six-month record, it was the highest number in Netflix history for either a film or a series. And this isn't just a flash in the pan. Since 2023, Korean content has consistently held the number one spot in non-English viewership on Netflix. When we look at music, the rankings become even clearer. According to analysis by Luminate, an American company that tracks music sales and streaming, Korea has become the world's third-largest music exporter. It is the United States, the United Kingdom, and then Korea. However, there is a clear distinction here. Most things we see on screens quietly stop at the peripheral of our interest. That interest usually ends with just typing a name into a search bar. Only a fraction of that remains like a seed in the heart, eventually turning into a finger clicking the checkout button for a plane ticket months later. That actually happened. According to the travel booking platform Klook, bookings for travel packages to Korea rose by 80 %in the two months after the release of the K-pop game*Demon Hunters*. Official surveys also point in the same direction. When asked what sparked their interest in Korea, the number one answer was exposure to Hallyu content, with 38.3%of respondents saying so. And this sentiment wasn't just limited to K-pop. There is a small badge at the National Museum of Korea featuring a magpie and a tiger. It was an item that used to sell about two units a day. Last summer, that badge sold over 38,000 units in just one month. Online pre-orders sold out as soon as they opened, forcing the museum to stop sales entirely. The production facility was too small to suddenly increase the supply. The museum itself has changed. Last year, 6.5 million people visited, the highest number since it opened. However, only 230,000 of them were foreigners. This means the rest were all us. It means we only started lining up again after we saw them liking our things. Let me point out one more trend in the Western world. In a survey on the status of Hallyu conducted by the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism across various overseas regions, the UK saw the largest increase in favorability toward Korean culture. It rose by 8 percentage points in a single year. This is the first reason. But this reason alone doesn't make anything happen. No matter how much the desire to visit grows, if there are no seats to carry that heart to Seoul, it just ends as a desire. The second reason begins right there. At the end of March this year, the British airline Virgin Atlantic began daily flights between Incheon and London Heathrow. It is a route that operates one flight every single day without fail. Next month, United Airlines will launch its first direct flight connecting Incheon and Newark. Our airlines have also moved deeper into Europe. Asiana has opened new routes to Milan and Budapest. Flights to and from major European countries have increased overall in the past year, and among them, the route to the UK has grown by 34.7%. However, opening the skies is not enough. There is one more hurdle to clear. To visit Korea, you normally have to obtain entry permission in advance. It’s a process called Electronic Travel Authorization, where you apply online and get approval before boarding your flight. This requirement is currently waived for people from the U.S. and major European countries. It has been extended once more until December 31st of this year. This means that people from those regions can enter Korea just by booking a ticket the day they decide they want to come. For someone to actually move, three doors must be opened. The first door is the mind. Because if there’s no desire to go, nothing can begin. The next door is the path. You need actual seats available to be able to move. The final door is the threshold. Because the country itself must open its door for you to enter. The past few years are when these doors opened one after another. Now that the doors are all open, people are coming in. But the story changes a bit when you look at when these people leave. According to a survey from a few years ago, comparing within the same chart, visitors from Japan stayed in Korea for 6.6 days. And visitors from the UK, Germany, and France stayed for 14.1 days on average. That’s more than double. Staying in a country for over ten days is not a sightseeing itinerary. That’s time to unpack, do laundry, and find a regular spot to eat . Let’s go back briefly to the world No. 1 story mentioned at the start. In the first half of this year, 38.39 million international passengers passed through Incheon Airport. Back in 2002, Incheon Airport was only tenth in the world. From there, it climbed up step by step to reach the top this year. Let’s flip the order here for a moment. We usually think people came because there were more flights. But airlines aren't charities; they only fly when they think seats will sell. The number of seats increased because there were people who wanted to come first. The air routes were not the cause, but the result. So what happens to the people who get off those planes? The moment they step out of the airport doors is actually the real beginning of this story. Let’s picture how these people who walked out of the airport lived before. When they ride the subway , they wear their backpacks on their front. When the night gets deep, they avoid alleys and take the main road home. Leaving a laptop on a table at a cafe to go to the restroom is something they never do back home. For people who arrive with those habits, the first thing they experience here is the night . There is a site called Numbeo that collects data on the cost of living and safety of cities by asking residents directly. As of mid-year, the crime perception index for Seoul was 25.4 points. In the same survey, New York, London, and Paris scored about twice as high as Seoul. What matters more than the scores themselves is what follows. Anyone who has lived for eight days in a city where they can walk down an alley alone at 11 p.m. without incident can't forget that feeling after they leave. The second thing they experience is the sense that this city is still alive late into the night. CNN once named Korea the country with the most convenience stores per capita. If you count all the convenience stores in our country, the number exceeds the total number of McDonald's locations worldwide. However, there is something in this country that is even more common than convenience stores. It's cafes. The number of coffee shops nationwide has surpassed 100,000. CNN even published a separate article calling Korea the "Republic of Cafes." The third thing is walking. Time Out, a British media outlet that ranks world cities annually, selected the best cities for walking this year. Seoul was tied for first place. But there is one thing we must point out about this ranking. The people who gave these scores were not tourists. These are scores from people who actually live in the city and walk those streets every day. 93%of people living in Seoul answered that it is good for walking. Let’s recall the path you walked today. The path where you came up the subway stairs, passed a convenience store, waited for the light to change, crossed the street, and turned into an alley, passing under the signs of restaurants. That path you walked without much thought is now ranked as one of the best for walking in the world. In the world city rankings by the same outlet, Seoul also climbed to 9th place this year. Last year, it was 42nd. It jumped over 30 spots in just one year. Because of this, people are staying for longer periods of time. Looking only at foreigners visiting Seoul, the average length of stay has increased by over half a day in one year. And they keep coming back. They aren't coming to see the landmarks again, but to relive the feeling they experienced once before. But if people stay longer and keep coming back, what happens next? Anyone who has hosted guests knows. A guest who stops by for a cup of tea and a guest who stays for days without leaving the table leave a completely different mark on the house. The guests coming to Korea now are the latter type. The amount of money spent by foreigners in Korea in the first half of this year increased by 36.4% compared to the same period last year. But more striking than that number is the fact that the direction of the balance has shifted. From March to June , the money foreigners spent here was more than what we spent while traveling abroad. It has been a very long time since this balance was tipped in our favor. Before that, for 72 months—in other words, for six straight years— we had been the ones spending more. And from here on is the next layer of this story. The people entering this country aren't just tourists coming for fun. Last year, the number of foreign patients who received medical care in Korea reached 2.11 million. This is the first time since we started keeping records. It's not a gradual increase, but the result of the figure doubling every year for three years. In the first half of this year alone, foreigners spent about 1.19 trillion won in Korean hospitals. That's over six times the amount compared to the same period before COVID-19. The places where money is spent have also shifted. This spring, medical and wellness services accounted for 24.8%of foreign credit card spending in Seoul. It means this isn't the kind of consumption from the days of buying bags at duty-free shops. People often say Myeong-dong has declined and Seongsu has risen. But the numbers don't say that. During the same period, sales in Myeong-dong increased by 33%and Hongdae by 19%. Both have made a comeback. And Seongsu-dong was added on top of that. Foreign visitors to Seongsu-dong grew from 330,000 in the first half of last year to 910,000 this year. It's not that the guests moved, but that there are more places to go. It’s not just the number of people coming for fun that has increased. There are also people coming for meetings. The Union of International Associations. That is, there is an organization that counts international conferences held around the world. In last year's tally, Korea ranked 2nd in the world with 491. It's right after the United States. If we narrow it down to cities, Seoul was number one in Asia. It outperformed Tokyo, Bangkok, and Singapore. People who fly in for meetings stay for a few days, eat meals, and walk the city when the meeting ends. This trend doesn't stop in Seoul, either. Compared to last year, the number of foreigners who booked high-speed trains from Seoul to Daejeon increased more than 18-fold. This year, 1.57 million foreigners entered through regional airports, the highest number ever. Now, I know I've only shared good news up to this point. I think it's time to pause for a moment . Let me shake up the story I've built so far with my own hands. First, the four-month surplus is just for those four months. Looking at the first half of the year as a whole, the money foreigners spent here is still less than what we spent abroad. The deficit has only narrowed significantly; it hasn't completely flipped yet. The direct flights to London, which were said to start running daily, are the same. Five months after launch, they decided to reduce the winter season flights to a few times a week. The seats were almost full in the spring, but the number of empty seats increased as summer approached. It means demand is seasonal. The problems on our end regarding hosting guests still remain. Overpriced service controversies happen every year, and this year, a street vendor at Gwangjang Market was suspended for three days for overcharging, and another was suspended for ten days for selling mixed meats. Bukchon is already under management. The Jongno District has designated it a special management area because the neighborhood has become too uncomfortable for residents due to the influx of tourists. Starting this year, we are making it clear that tourists who enter the alleys outside of permitted hours will be fined. This does not mean that tourism should replace manufacturing. The pillar that supports this country is still making and selling goods. However, it is up to us to decide how to welcome guests who have come to our doorstep. While the choice to visit is theirs, making them want to return is our responsibility. Nevertheless, these people keep coming. In a Korea Tourism Organization survey, 80%of respondents said they would still visit Korea even if travel costs were 20%higher than expected. This means they aren't coming just because it’s cheap. Then, one final question remains . What exactly are these people coming all this way to see? I mentioned three doors earlier. That the interest grew, the paths opened, and the barriers were lowered. But if we look back at what opened those doors, it wasn't something newly built by a tourism agency. It was the local bathhouse, the convenience store with its lights on at 2 a.m., and the night alleys where you could walk alone safely. It means it was the daily life we live every day. The Korea Tourism Organization released a report on this with a specific title. "The Daily Life of Koreans as a Travel Experience" was the theme of that report. A state agency documented that what foreigners are purchasing isn't just famous sites, but our daily lives. Famous sites can be imitated. Palaces, pagodas, and streets can be built by any country if they set their mind to it. But a "day" cannot be manufactured. The late-night streetlights, a casual greeting exchanged in an alley, and a night road where nothing happens. These are things that only emerge when people have lived that way for decades. That is why they come back. 54.7%of foreigners visiting Korea are on their second visit or more. It means more than half have bought tickets again. Just a generation ago, when we went abroad, we were asked where Korea was. While answering that question, we went to factories, opened shops, and swept the alleys. We are the people who earned a living that way to send our children to school. The day that people from the other side of the globe come to experience is the day built, one by one, by those who came before us. What was just "today" for us has become something worth flying 12 hours to experience for someone else. How about you?

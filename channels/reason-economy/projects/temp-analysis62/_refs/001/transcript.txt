There is a taxi that brings in 200,000 won a day. But to sit in that driver's seat, the driver first had to pay 100 million won. There are no daily payments for private taxis. There is no company and no boss. Since the driver is the boss, it all looks like the driver's profit. However, before that 200,000 won on the meter reaches the driver's bank account, several hands take a cut along the way. We will count those hands one by one and divide the remaining money by the time the driver sat in the driver's seat. That division is the conclusion of this video. It’s 11 p.m. at a downtown taxi stand. Taxis with "vacant" signs on are lined up, and driver Choi Young-nam is somewhere in the middle of that line. He is 59 years old and in his third year as a private taxi driver in Seoul. He quit his previous job, borrowed 100 million won from a bank, and bought a license plate. The car is an LPG Sonata taxi, and he is subscribed to a franchise platform and receives calls. There is something I must clarify first. Driver Choi Young-nam is not a real person. He is a character created to track these earnings and losses to the end. Among the drivers standing at this taxi stand , many are older than driver Choi Young-nam. According to the Korea Transportation Safety Authority, the number of private taxi drivers at the end of 2025 was 164,345. Among them, 55.3%are 65 or older. 3%. The number of rides these taxis provide per day is 520,042. The average fare per ride is 13,205 won . Let's assume driver Choi Young-nam brings in 200,000 won a day. If he drives 22 days a month, his annual revenue becomes 52.8 million won. There is a survey that shows where this revenue stands in Seoul. The Seoul Institute of Transportation Culture and Education surveyed Seoul private taxi drivers in 2025. 68.0%had an annual revenue of less than 60 million won. Only 0.5%had an annual revenue of 100 million won or more. So, driver Choi Young-nam is not a driver who earns more than others, but one who belongs to the largest group. All the numbers mentioned here are revenues. It is not the money that remains in the driver's hands. 200,000 won a day. Do you see that? Does it look like a lot? Let's break it down piece by piece. Driver Choi Young-nam brings in 200,000 won a day for 22 days a month. His monthly revenue is 4.4 million won. From this money on the meter, it now starts to slip away bit by bit. The first thing to be deducted is fuel. Let's assume that driver Choi Young-nam drives 200 km a day. The Sonata taxi has a city fuel economy of 8.3 km per liter. 3 km. Then you would fill up about 530 liters of LPG per month. Looking at the report on Opinet prices, the Seoul average for automotive LPG butane used by taxis was 1,156 won per liter. This is as of August 27th. Filling up for a month at this price costs about 610,000 won. The government returns a portion of the taxes on fuel as a fuel subsidy. Subtracting that, let's estimate the actual burden at around 530,000 won. For reference, the fuel tax on butane is currently discounted by 25%. This reduction has been extended until the end of November. So, these calculations are based on the period through the end of November. Subtracting the 530,000 won for fuel, 3.87 million won remains. Fuel costs are money tied to the distance driven. Even while driving empty looking for passengers, the odometer goes up. Even if there isn't a single passenger, this expense does not decrease. The next category is the cost of the vehicle, which quietly drains away without appearing in your bank account. The price of a Sonata taxi is 25.95 million won. Assuming this car is used for 7 years, let's set aside money for the next car every month. That comes to 310,000 won per month. This money doesn't appear to be leaving your account each month. But if you don't set it aside, it comes back as a lump-sum bill on the day you replace the car. There is also a system that lightens the load. When buying a car for private taxi operations, the acquisition tax is halved, and this reduction is in effect until the end of 2027. Subtracting the vehicle cost, it leaves 3.56 million won. The next category is for accident preparedness. Private taxis must prove they have liability insurance when they start operating. Driver Choi Young-nam says he joined the Private Taxi Mutual Aid Association. Since premiums vary greatly by driver, let's estimate it at 2.5 million won per year. Divided by month, that is 210,000 won. This is a value assuming a year without accidents . If an accident occurs, this category increases. How much it increases varies from person to person, so it's hard to state a single number. Subtracting insurance, 3.35 million won remains. Everything up to this point was money tied to the car and the distance driven . The following categories are different in nature. It is the franchise platform fee. The old franchise structure was a method of taking 20%of revenue as a fee. And of that, 15%to 17%was returned to the driver under the name of partnership agreements for driving data or advertising. Now, there is a product available that charges a single 2.8% rate without a return structure. We will calculate assuming VAT is added separately. If gas money is tied to distance , the commission is tied to the fare charged. On days when no passengers are picked up, there is no commission. It’s like having two columns with opposite directions written side by side in the ledger. After deducting the commission, it’s 3.24 million won. The next column is money that leaves the account before it even enters. According to Seoul city statistics, the card payment rate for Seoul taxis in 2025 was 88.7%. It was 7%. Let’s set the card commission rate at 1.5%. That comes to 60,000 won per month. This money is deducted before deposit, so drivers don’t even feel it as a cost. Subtracting this leaves 3.18 million won. Next are the small expenses for running the car. Let’s estimate maintenance, tires, oil changes, and car washes at 150,000 won a month. A car driven 200km every day wears out consumables quickly. Subtracting this leaves 3.03 million won. Finally, the small columns remain. We’ll assume 50,000 won for union dues, communication fees, meter usage, and driver insurance. Subtracting this leaves 2.98 million won. 2.98 million won. If this is what remains from 4.4 million won in revenue, it doesn't look bad. However, this is still before interest and taxes. There is one column not yet recorded in this ledger. The value of the driver's seat where the driver sits. But ironically, the money comes from here. The biggest investment in this business isn't the car, but the license plate. Driver Choi Young-nam bought his license plate from a previous taxi driver. This is called a license transfer. How much does that license plate cost? In Seoul, 2024 reports showed prices between 110 million and 120 million won. There were reports that it rose to 200 million won in some new towns in Gyeonggi-do. A report from April this year stated a trade in Ulsan reached 124 million won. The same report said it had risen 27.8% in half a year. However, these reports are based on intermediary effects and are not official market price statistics. The reports cite several reasons for the price increase. Requirements to receive a license were eased, forced day-off systems were lifted, and fares increased. Demand from retiring baby boomers is also cited as a reason. There is also mandatory training to buy a license, and the validity period was shortened from 3 years to 1. This took effect from April 2024. Driver Choi Young-nam bought this license plate for 100 million won, all through a bank loan. Let’s assume an annual interest rate. Then, leaving the principal aside, the interest alone is 330,000 won per month . This is like monthly rent the driver pays for their seat. Even though they are the boss of their own taxi, the rent for the seat they sit in is an extra cost. Subtracting the interest leaves 2.65 million won. Then, would this column disappear if they bought it with cash? No interest payments go out. Instead, they have given up the interest they would have earned if they had deposited that 100 million won. This is called opportunity cost. Whether through a loan or cash, the fact that 100 million won is tied up in the license plate remains the same. I mentioned earlier that 2.98 million won remained. Do you remember? That figure was before subtracting the license plate interest and the payments to the government. Now, let's look at those columns in order. First is the VAT. With revenue like Mr. Choi Young-nam's, he qualifies for simplified taxation. It's a method of lightly taxing the VAT of businesses with small revenues. So, private taxi drivers pay very little VAT compared to their revenue. After subtracting tax credits for card sales, it comes to about 80,000 won per month. Subtracting the VAT leaves 2.57 million won. Next is the national pension. This year, the national pension insurance rate is 9%. If you are an employee, the company pays half, but private taxi drivers are local subscribers and pay it all themselves. Assuming a reported income of 2.65 million won, it's 250,000 won per month. It is money that returns as a pension later. However, it is money leaving their hands right now. Subtracting this leaves 2.32 million won. The health insurance rate this year is 7.19%. It is. Adding long-term care insurance to this and assuming no other assets, it is 210,000 won per month. This leaves 2.11 million won. Lastly, there are income tax and local income tax. Taxes vary from person to person depending on dependents and deductions. So, let's estimate it at about 210,000 won per month. Subtracting this leaves 1.9 million won . If we look at the columns we've subtracted so far, a pattern emerges. The previous columns were tied to distance traveled and contracts. The columns we just subtracted are tied to revenue and the cost of the seat. Even in months with few passengers, the license plate interest leaves the account on the same day. The bank doesn't ask how many people you carried that month. Now, all that's left is to divide the 1.9 million won by the time the driver worked. Let's go back to that taxi stand. The time spent waiting in line with the "vacant" sign on is all working time for Mr. Choi Young-nam . Because he couldn't leave the driver's seat whether there was a passenger or not. We assumed he sits in the driver's seat for 10 hours a day, 22 days a month. That's 220 hours a month. The money left in his bank account this month is 1.9 million won. It’s less than half of the 4.4 million won in revenue. Before dividing this figure by the hours, let’s go over the assumptions behind this calculation. Insurance and maintenance costs assume a year without any accidents or major breakdowns. It only ended up this way because it was that kind of year. Also, things that would be included for an employee are missing from this ledger. There is no severance pay and no annual leave allowance. For an independent taxi driver, a day off means a day with zero revenue. There is a flip side to this as well. If you make 250,000 won a day, the remaining profit certainly increases. However, that revenue only comes from driving for that much longer. If you add more hours, the denominator in our division also grows. There is another change as well. Starting May 11th this year, franchise fees cannot be charged for passengers hailed on the street or picked up at taxi stands. These are called street-hail passengers, so if a driver has a high ratio of them, their commission costs decrease accordingly. Let’s do the division. Divide 1.9 million won by 220 hours. That comes out to about 8,640 won per hour. The minimum wage in 2026 is 10,320 won. Mr. Choi Young-nam’s hourly rate is nearly 1,700 won below that. What if the license plate had been purchased with cash? With the 330,000 won in interest removed, the remaining amount becomes just over 2.23 million won. That works out to about 10,140 won per hour. It’s a level that falls just short of the minimum wage. This is a simple comparison without recalculating taxes. And as we saw earlier, it’s a value that ignores the interest one would have earned by depositing 100 million won instead. You work an entire month under the title of "boss." When you condense that month into an hourly wage, you’re just hovering near the minimum wage. These numbers aren’t a result of the driver being lazy. 200,000 won a day was the most common revenue bracket for private taxi drivers in Seoul. Then, to whose bank account did the remaining 2.5 million won out of the 4.4 million go? Let’s line up where the 4.4 million won went. First place is the driver themselves. That’s 1.9 million won. Second place is the government. This combines VAT, income tax, health insurance, and national pension. It is the net amount after subtracting the money returned as fuel subsidies. It also includes the value of the pension to be returned later. Third place is the fueling station. 610,000 won in fuel costs goes there. Even that fuel cost includes fuel taxes. Fourth place is the financial institution that provided the license plate loan, at 330,000 won. Fifth place is the car company, at 310,000 won. Sixth place is the mutual aid association, at 210,000 won. Seventh place is the maintenance shop, at 150,000 won. Eighth place is the franchise platform, at 110,000 won. 9th place is 60,000 won for the card company. The final 10th place is 50,000 won for the union and communication fees. Adding it all up, it is exactly 4.4 million won. Two things catch my eye in this list. First, the person who took home the most is the driver themselves. But if you divide that share by 220 hours, it’s below the minimum wage. Next is the platform's position. When talk of taxi income comes up, people often think of the platform first. In this ledger, the platform took 110,000 won. That's eighth place. Starting May 11, 2026, the law stipulates that fees only apply to passengers called via the platform app. In second and third place are the state and fuel costs. Neither the mutual aid association, financial firms , nor car companies are the villains. They simply take the share written in their respective contracts. The problem is not who takes the money, but in what order they take it. The shares at the front are determined first by kilometers, liters, and contracts. They don't decrease even on days with few passengers. Only the final remaining amount fluctuates based on the number of passengers picked up. The person who gets that share is the driver who sat in the seat for 220 hours. Not all private taxi drivers across the country receive the same numbers. However, the order in which money flows out follows the same structure. Ultimately, the most expensive cost in this business isn't the 100-million-won license plate , but the driver's time.

Now, let's get started. First, let's verify where we started. There were originally four islands where the airport stands today. They were the islands of Yeongjongdo, Yongyudo, Sammokdo, and Sinbuldo. And between those islands, there were seas and tidal flats. If you look at an old map, you’ll see a completely different picture than today. The only land to speak of were four small islands. Sea and tidal flats filled the space between them. According to Incheon International Airport Corporation data, the airport site is 5,619,600 square meters. 82%of that is reclaimed sea area. Simply put, it means over 82%of the airport site was originally underwater. Tidal flats are places that appear when the tide goes out and submerge when the tide comes in . They poured soil into such places to raise the height and blocked the water to turn it into new land. Key airport facilities like runways, aprons, and control towers were built right here on this reclaimed land. It means that most of what we think of as an airport was drawn upon the sea. This fact alone changes how we look at this airport a little bit. Then, naturally, a question like this comes to mind. Is it really okay for hundreds of tons of aircraft to land every day on land created by filling in the sea? To answer this, we first need to know how big the land that was created is. The site secured in the first phase of the Incheon airport construction was about 56 million square meters. That’s about 18 times the size of Yeouido. It isn't just one Yeouido that was filled in. They created new land large enough to fit 18 Yeouidos. However, there was something that had to be done before making the land. It was blocking out the seawater. If you pour soil where seawater flows in and out freely, it just gets washed away by the currents. So, they started by building a seawall to surround the area. In simple terms, they built the rim of a large bowl first and then filled it with soil. Let’s imagine how the construction proceeded scene by scene. The first scene is the sea. Dump trucks endlessly carry soil over the mudflats where tides come and go. The seawater keeps trying to push the piles of dirt away. In the next scene, the seawall is gradually connected. As the wall forms a continuous line, the sea inside turns into a massive pool of water. The final scene is the moment the inside is filled with soil. Soil piles up where the water used to be, and the outline of new land appears. But this wasn't the end of the scene. The real test started from that point on. Construction records state that the total length of this seawall is about 17.3 kilometers. It’s a distance that would take an adult nearly four hours to walk without stopping. The amount of soil used is said to be about 1 million dump truck loads. Even if you carried it at a rate of 100 loads a day without stopping, it would take over 27 years. However, we must pay attention to one thing here. In civil engineering, the truly difficult part isn't the moment of creating the land. It is predicting how that land will change in the future . Then, what exactly happens beneath that ground? Just pouring soil doesn't make it solid ground immediately. Below the sea and the mudflats lies a soft clay layer saturated with water. What happens if you pile a massive amount of soil on top of such ground? As the water between the soil particles slowly drains out, the ground gets compressed. In technical terms, this is called consolidation. Simply put, it’s like placing a heavy object on a sponge soaked with water. At first, you don’t notice it. But over time, the water inside the sponge drains out and the object slowly settles. This is the basic principle of land subsidence. There is something important we must distinguish here. The collapse of the ground and the subsidence of the ground are completely different things. It is not like a sinkhole where the ground suddenly disappears one day. It is a phenomenon where the ground is compressed over a long period, causing the elevation to gradually drop. What the airport builders feared most was not a collapse. The truly scary part was that it was sinking so slowly that it was barely visible. Today, it is fine. Even a year later, it is still fine. But the story changes if it continues to sink little by little over 10 or 20 years. When you place a heavy load on a thick mattress, it doesn't show at first. But if you remove it after a few days, a deep mark remains. The ground, too, is compressed very slowly like that. There is an even bigger problem, especially at airports. One side sinks a lot, while the other side sinks very little. This is called differential settlement. Simply put, it is a phenomenon where the ground settles unevenly. What would happen if one side of the runway sank several centimeters while the other stayed put? Tiny slopes and bumps would form on the road. For an airplane traveling at hundreds of kilometers per hour, even a tiny bump on the runway is a major problem. So, this problem had to be solved. And the solution that came out was the exact opposite of common sense. If the ground sinking is the problem, what should you do? Usually, you would start by thinking of ways to stop it from sinking. But at the Incheon Airport site, they did the exact opposite. They piled more soil on the ground. The name of this technique is preloading. In simple terms, it means pressing it down in advance. Let’s imagine the situation at the site for a moment. A massive terminal and runway are going to be built on this land. If the ground starts to sink only after that heavy weight is added, the completed facilities will tilt along with it. But waiting for the ground to settle enough would delay the construction schedule indefinitely. It must have been a great concern for the engineers at the site. It was at this crossroads that the decision was made. The idea was to make the ground sink before building the airport. The calculation was that if the ground was going to sink anyway, they would force it to settle before constructing the facilities. When you buy a new sofa, it sinks a bit for the first few days before it eventually settles into place . The ground is the same. Looking at the site preparation data for Incheon Airport at the time, it states that the preloading height was set between 1.9 m and 5.6 m. The pressing period was basically about six months, and it was adjusted while monitoring the actual measurement results. Let’s visualize this process. This is a story reconstructed based on the situation at the site at the time. Here is the first scene. In front of the people preparing for construction lies a vast field of soil made by reclaiming the sea. On the surface, it already looks like solid ground. However, underneath it lies a layer of soft clay saturated with water . Here is the second scene. Soil is piled up on the ground to a height of 1.9 to 5.6 meters. They are pre-loading it with the weight that will be added later. Here is the third scene. Even if the set period ends, they do not just remove the soil blindly. They check the measured values to decide whether to extend or shorten the duration. Here is the final scene. Only after confirming that the ground has sufficiently stabilized do they remove the piled soil and begin the main construction. This method had another advantage. It is that they can experience what will happen after the airport is built during the construction process. Because even if a problem occurs, they can fix it before the facilities are built. Can you see the core of this method now? It is not about making the ground not settle. It is about making it settle first before building the airport. It means that waiting was safety itself. But there was another problem here. It is that no matter how high you pile the soil, if there is no way for the water to escape, the ground will not settle quickly. Even if you press down on water-saturated soil, if there is nowhere for the water to go, the settlement proceeds very slowly. So , they had to create a way for the water to drain out of the ground first. For the Incheon Airport site development, they used methods called Sand Drain and PBD. Sand Drain is a method of creating drainage paths in the ground using sand. In simple terms, it is like inserting sand columns into the ground densely. PBD is a method of inserting plastic drainage materials into the ground. Simply put, it is like sticking straws densely into the ground . According to technical data from that time, the diameter of the sand drain is about 40 cm, and the installation interval is around 3 meters. For PBD, there are cases where 10 cm wide drainage materials were installed at intervals of 1.6 to 2 meters. Both methods share the same principle. It is to shorten the distance the water has to travel to escape. It is like wringing out a thick wet towel; squeezing it in several places at once dries it much faster than squeezing only at one end. You do not need to memorize all these numbers. The key is that drainage paths are laid out like a dense net underground. When soil is piled on top, the water in the compressed ground escapes along these paths. As the water drains, the ground lowers little by little. They finish this process in advance before the airport is built. If these paths had not existed, the airport's opening date would have been pushed back. But there is one really important question left. Just how long are we supposed to keep pressing down and waiting? The answer to that is measurement. Building the site for Incheon Airport wasn’t a project that ended with applying just one construction method. The process was managed by continuously measuring how much the ground actually settled and how far the consolidation had progressed. Why go to such lengths? Because calculations alone cannot determine everything. The engineers on-site must have reasoned like this. They didn't just assume it would be fine because they had calculated it. They installed sensors in the ground and kept checking how much it had settled. Even within the same airport site, some areas have thicker clay layers, and some have better drainage. Simply put, it’s like how the soil properties differ from spot to spot, even in the same field. Let’s imagine a situation like this. You look at the measurements for a certain area and see the ground is settling slower than expected. However, the project schedule already lists the date for the next phase. If you push forward according to the schedule, the project finishes quickly, but you end up building a runway on ground that hasn't fully settled. Conversely, if you trust the measurements and wait, the schedule is delayed, but the ground becomes firmly stable. At this crossroads, Incheon Airport chose the time set by the ground, not the time set by the calendar. This has a parallel in our daily lives as well. It’s like waiting for the ground to harden before building a house. In simple terms, measurement is like a health check-up for the ground. If the check-up results are good, you move on to the next step. If it doesn't meet expectations, you wait a little longer. In that case, the measurement values, not the calendar, become the standard. Just as you monitor your health by checking your blood pressure regularly, you keep checking how much the ground has settled. The records accumulated that way have become data spanning nearly 30 years. In other words, the ground at Incheon Airport was an object of constant monitoring even after it was built. That data became the standard for determining whether the ground moved as expected. So, what were the results that the data told us? Just how much did it actually settle? I will now reveal the numbers. According to the sustainability report from Incheon International Airport Corporation, the cumulative settlement of the runways from 1991 to March 2020 ranged from 0.042 cm to 2.9 cm depending on the location. The area that settled the least is about the thickness of four sheets of paper. Even the area that settled the most is only slightly larger than the diameter of a 500-won coin. The predicted settlement during the design phase was 7.5 cm. That's about the length of three 500-won coins lined up side-by-side. This means the actual cumulative settlement didn't even reach half of what was predicted during the design phase. From 1991 to March 2020 is about 29 years. It means this is the record of the ground moving over nearly 30 years. Based on the area that sank the most, the simple average over nearly 30 years is about 1mm per year. So, as the title suggests, saying it sinks every year is not incorrect. However, the speed is completely different from the horror we typically imagine. The designers did not assume there would be no settlement from the start. They calculated in advance that it could sink by about 7.5 cm. But when actually measured, it didn't even reach half of that limit. There is something to think about here. What if these numbers were the other way around? If the estimate was 2cm but it was actually 8cm, it would mean the calculations were wrong. So, the fact that it sank less than predicted can hardly be seen as a mere coincidence. It means they understood the nature of the soft ground and chose the right construction method. It doesn't mean it didn't sink at all. It did sink. It’s just that it moved well within the predicted range with plenty of margin. Of course, this result is not a guarantee that there will never be any settlement in the future. What is confirmed is the record for that period , and that is why the airport corporation continues to monitor the ground. The important thing is not whether there is settlement or not. The key is how much it sank, how evenly it sank, and whether that can be predicted and managed in advance. Then, what happens if we compare these numbers with airports in other countries? The Three-Runway System at Hong Kong International Airport was a massive project to create a new runway by reclaiming about 650 hectares of land north of the existing airport. 650 hectares is about twice the size of Yeouido. However, this place also had soft ground issues. Looking at Hong Kong Airport’s data, a significant portion of the area to be reclaimed had been a dumping ground for contaminated mud in the past. So, Hong Kong used a method called Deep Cement Mixing, or DCM for short. Simply put, it's a method of mixing cement into soft soil to make the ground itself solid.

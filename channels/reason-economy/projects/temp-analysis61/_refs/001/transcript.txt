If you hold on, you eventually win. This is what people who leveraged everything to buy a home five years ago told each other. In fact, the average price of an apartment in Seoul rose from 1.2 billion won in October 2021 to over 1.6 billion won this past August. Looking at the numbers alone, they won. Yet, something strange is happening. The number of apartments and villas nationwide put up for auction by creditors because owners couldn't pay their debts hit 24,848 last year, the highest in 11 years. This year, the number already exceeded 18,000 by August. Let's assume someone borrowed 300 million won in the fall of 2021. A person who used to pay about 1.28 million won a month could find themselves having to pay over 1.7 million won due to interest rate resets . While the actual amount varies by loan terms, they need hundreds of thousands of won more each month. If home prices went up, why are people losing their homes? Today, we look at the calculations the 2021 leveraged buyers trusted and why their assumptions crumbled five years later. Watch until the end to learn three questions to check the risks of your own loans. In 2020 and 2021, lunch breaks at work were always filled with talk about who bought which apartment. Screenshots of actual transaction prices were posted in group chats, with comments below asking why you hadn't bought one yet. The Seoul apartment real transaction price index rose 23.07 %in 2020. And it rose another 13.46%in 2021. No matter how much of their salary they saved, it was hard to keep up with home prices. It was common for a house you planned to see on the weekend to be sold before then. Does this seem like the story of just a few greedy people to you? In 2021, those in their 20s and 30s made up 41.7%of people who bought apartments in Seoul. Over four out of ten buyers were from the younger generation. It was the highest ratio since relevant statistics began in 2019. Around that time, the term "beggar by choice" also became popular. It referred to those who worked hard and saved but felt relatively poor because they didn't buy a house. Conversely, those who leveraged as much as possible to buy a house were called "leveraged buyers." Those who waited without buying were treated less like cautious people and more like people left behind. What pushed them wasn't just pure greed to make a lot of money. It was the anxiety that if they didn't buy then, they might never be able to afford their own home. That anxiety was transferred directly into bank loans. In July 2021 alone, bank household loans increased by 9.7 trillion won. Countless people made similar decisions at the same time . There is also the case of Kim Min-young, who was mentioned at the time. Let's take a closer look at what went wrong. Introduced by a pseudonym, he was 34 at the time of the article, a 7 th-year employee at a major company. In 2021, he bought an old apartment in Seoul for 850 million won. I’ll tell you what that house became worth a few years later in a moment. What kind of calculations led people to borrow such huge sums of money? There was usually a specific order to "reaching out" for loans. First, they took out a mortgage, then filled the gap with a credit loan. If that still wasn't enough, they asked their parents for help or emptied their savings. The reason they could borrow so much was that interest rates were low. The Bank of Korea's base rate dropped to 0.5%in May 2020. In the second quarter of 2021, the average mortgage rate was 2.70%. They calculated that as long as house prices rose faster than interest, it was a profit. It sounded natural to say it was better to pay for your own mortgage than monthly rent. But for that math to work, rising house prices alone weren't enough. You had to be able to afford the monthly repayments until the very end. Let's imagine two people who both bought a house worth 1 billion won. Person A used 800 million won of their own money and borrowed 200 million won. Person B used 200 million won of their own and borrowed 800 million won. If prices drop by 200 million won, A's equity shrinks from 800 to 600 million won. On the other hand, for B, their initial 200 million won effectively vanishes. It's the same price drop, but one person loses some equity, while the other loses it all. Now, let's say prices stay the same, but interest rates rise from 2%to 5%. Excluding principal, A’s annual interest increases from 4 million to 10 million won. For B, it jumps from 16 million to 40 million won. Even though the house price didn't fall a cent, B has to find 24 million won more each year. House values don't appear in your bank account, but interest is deducted on schedule. The danger of "reaching out" lay exactly in that difference. Ultimately, cash flow is what matters even more. There was another major misunderstanding here as well. Rising prices mean your asset valuation grows, not that you can spend that cash immediately. If you’re short on living expenses, you must borrow against the house or sell it. However, bank loan limits and buyer demand were things you couldn't control. That calculation began to waver significantly in 2022. Banks use an index called COFIX when setting variable mortgage rates. You can understand it as a value that represents the cost for banks to raise money. The new COFIX, which was 0.86%in January 2021, jumped to 4.29%by December 2022. It increased about fivefold. Loan interest rates followed suit. In early January 2023, the upper end of the floating mortgage rates at the five major banks reached 8.12%. For those who took out loans when rates were in the 2%range, this was an unthinkable figure. If you borrowed 500 million won at 2.5%per year, the monthly interest is about 1.04 million won. If 4.3%is applied to the same amount, it increases to about 1.79 million won. That's 750,000 won every month. In one year, that means paying an extra 9 million won. The entrance and the living room are the same, but the cost of living in that house has gone up. If your salary stays the same but interest is deducted first, you have to cut back on dining out and travel. If that still wasn't enough, you had to break into your savings or find money elsewhere. But at this time, housing prices even fell. The Seoul apartment real transaction price index dropped by 21.78%in the year 2022. The gains accumulated over two years were significantly wiped out. So, did all the "debt-fueled" buyers collapse back then? That was not the case. Some people had a way to catch their breath. On January 30, 2023, the Special Bogeumjari Loan was released. It was a policy loan designed to allow people to use up to 500 million won under certain conditions regardless of income, if the home was worth 900 million won or less. It could also be used to switch existing loans to long-term fixed rates . Of the applications received in the 19 days after its launch, 57.9%were for the purpose of refinancing existing loans. This means nearly six out of ten people applied to reduce their interest burden. There were also people who did not immediately experience interest rate hikes in the first place. These are the people who received mixed-rate loans where the rate is fixed for the first five years, around 2021. Such loans handled by the five major banks from November 2020 to December 2021 exceeded 24 trillion won. Even as market rates soared, they were able to maintain their existing rates for the contracted five years. Even if house prices fell, if their monthly repayments stayed the same, they could afford to wait a little longer. And in fact, Seoul housing prices rose again. The problem is that the time written in the loan agreement has also passed. If you took out a loan in September 2021, five years later is exactly September of this year. Many people think that if it's a fixed-rate loan, the rate will stay the same until the end. However, mixed-rate mortgage loans are often fixed only for the first five years and then convert to floating rates. There are also periodic loans that reset interest rates every five years. Although the structures differ, they are the same in that a new rate applies once the agreed period ends. For some who took out loans in 2021, that day is now arriving. If you borrowed 300 million won at 3.11%per year—the rate in September 2021—with a 30-year principal and interest repayment plan, your initial monthly payment is about 1.28 million won. After five years, the new rate applies to the reduced principal and the remaining maturity. If the rate climbs to the high 5%range, the monthly payment could exceed 1.7 million won. The actual amount varies by product and loan conditions. But the core issue remains the same. Even if your salary stays the same, you might need to come up with hundreds of thousands of won more each month starting from the day your interest rate is reset. This is not just a problem for a few people. The scale of bank mortgage loans with interest rates changing between October and December this year has reached 165 trillion won. This amount excludes policy loans and includes various types of variable-rate and rate-reset loans. It doesn't mean all of these are the debts of the " panic-buyers" from 2021. The interest rate environment has also become burdensome again. The Bank of Korea raised the base rate to 2.75%on July 16 this year and hiked it further to 3%on August 27. The average interest rate for new mortgage loans in August was 4.66%, the highest level since November 2022. In 2023, there was a path to switch to policy loans. Will the government open the same path this time ? As of now, no separate large-scale relief measures for all borrowers facing 2026 rate resets have been confirmed. While there is talk of expanding long-term fixed-rate loans, not everyone can switch to their desired terms. Whether one can use individual refinancing loans or debt adjustment programs also differs from person to person. However, the share of fixed-rate loans among new mortgages dropped to 31.9%this July. This means many people are choosing loans where the interest rate can fluctuate. It’s like taking on risks similar to those from a few years ago. This raises a question. If Seoul housing prices have risen again, couldn't they just sell their homes and get out? First, just because the average has risen doesn't mean every house has gone up. Some who bought homes at the peak in 2021 have yet to recover their purchase price. An 84-square-meter unit at Hillstate Lake Songdo 2nd in Incheon was traded for 1 billion won in July 2021. But in June 2025, the trading price for the same size was 600 million won. Compared to the peak transaction, that is a difference of 400 million won. In August 2021, a 41-square-meter unit at Sanggye Jugong Complex 14 in Nowon-gu, Seoul, was traded for 639 million won. However, it was traded for 450 million won this past February. Of course, prices vary by floor, condition, and timing of the transaction, even for the same area. A few individual transactions do not represent the entire housing market. But it also means you cannot say everyone who bought in 2021 made a profit based solely on Seoul’s average prices. So, are homes that have recovered their purchase price doing okay? Not necessarily. You have to calculate the acquisition tax paid when buying, interest paid while holding, and brokerage fees when selling. If you sold a house for 850 million won that you bought for 800 million won, it looks like a 50 million won gain. But once you subtract the costs incurred during that time, the actual profit or loss can change. Especially for those with high debt, options become limited even when it comes time to sell. You must pay off your mortgage with the proceeds from the sale, and if you have additional personal loans or money borrowed from family, the math gets complicated. That is why you cannot simply look at the transaction price to say how much you earned. A bigger problem is whether there is anyone to buy it. In 2021, buyers rushed in, thinking if they missed out then, they would never be able to buy a home. Now, even if you like a house, you have to first check if you can borrow the necessary funds from the bank. This is because loan limits vary depending on your income and existing debt. The seller may want 1 billion won, but the buyer might not be able to raise that amount. If you lower the price, the money left in your hand after settling the loans shrinks. But if you wait, you have to pay interest every month. Transaction regulations also have an impact. Following the measures announced in October 2025, the entire area of Seoul was included as a land transaction permit zone. To buy these homes, you must consider permits and actual residency requirements. Such restrictions can become factors that make transactions difficult. Ultimately , news that home prices have risen and the reality of being able to sell your home at the price you want are different. Then, couldn't you just refinance your loan to hold on longer? That isn't simple either. Banks check not just the home value, but also your current income, existing debt, and credit status. Even if you own a home and have no payment defaults, you might not be able to get a new loan with your desired terms. On paper, you are a homeowner, but in your bank account, you are someone worried about next month's repayment. At first, you cut back on dining out. Next, you break your savings, and if that’s not enough, you look for other loans. But there is a limit to paying off debt with more debt when your income isn't increasing. At this stage, news of a rebound in home prices offers little comfort. Because they need to come up with the money they are short this month by today. As the delinquency drags on, interest and credit issues can compound. Then one day, they fail to come up with the funds by the scheduled payment date. If delinquency continues for a long time, financial institutions can file for an arbitrary auction on the mortgaged home according to agreements and legal procedures. It's not that the homeowner wants to sell, but that the creditor is requesting a court sale to recover the debt. In July of this year, the number of apartments and villas actually sold via arbitrary auction in Seoul was 264. That is an increase of about 33%from the month before. These are cases where the houses didn't just end up in the auction process, but actually changed owners. In November of last year, the number of houses for which arbitrary auctions began in Dobong-gu, Seoul, was recorded at 214. The month before, there were ten. However, this surge in a specific period cannot be seen as solely the result of "panic buying"( yeonkkeul). Every homeowner has different circumstances. Auction information provider GG Auction has explained cases where houses bought by some individuals in their 20s and 30s with low funds and credit—even using secondary financial sector loans— during the 2021 market boom are now coming up for auction. But if a house is sold at auction, does the debt end there? There are cases where that is not the case. The auction proceeds are distributed to creditors in an order set by law. Only after all debts are paid and money remains can it return to the homeowner. For example, suppose the loans and other debts total 600 million won, but the house sells at auction for 500 million won. While actual distribution priority and costs must be considered, simply put, there is a 100 million won deficit. It means you could still have debt left to pay even after losing your home. Do you remember Kim Min-young, whom I mentioned earlier? She is an office worker in her 30s who bought an apartment in Seoul for 850 million won in 2021. According to reports, her house was in a "tin can" state, where selling it wouldn't even be enough to pay off the loans. Lee Chang-yi, a person in his 30s mentioned in the same article, also saw his Seoul apartment, bought with a loan in 2021, put on an arbitrary auction list. Both are pseudonyms, and the final auction results have not been confirmed. At the moment they bought the homes, it was a success in achieving home ownership. But a few years later, the house became an asset that is difficult to even settle the debt with. Of course, not all homes appearing in auctions are due to panic buying. There are other reasons like unemployment, business failure, and the slump in self-employment. Conversely, even if people bought houses through panic buying in 2021, many are likely holding up without trouble if they have stable income and spare funds. Last year, individual bankruptcy filings reached 149,146, the highest ever. In the first half of last year, more than half of the bankruptcy applicants at the Seoul Rehabilitation Court were in their 30s and 40s. However, statistics alone cannot determine how much of this is due to home purchases. Let’s retrace the path that led us here. In 2021, home prices rose and interest rates were low. People took on debt, anxious that they’d be left behind if they didn't buy then. In 2022, home prices fell and interest rates rose. Yet, some avoided the shock thanks to fixed-rate periods and policy loans. Meanwhile, Seoul home prices climbed once again. The saying that you’ll eventually win if you hold on made a comeback. But the clock on the contracts signed with banks kept ticking. The day arrived, five years later, for interest rates to be reset. Even if your assets grow as home prices rise, that money isn’t immediately in your pocket. On the other hand, the loan repayment date arrives without fail. This is why you can endure if your income and savings are sufficient even if prices fall, but you can collapse if you lack repayment funds even if prices rise. This doesn't mean that choosing your home wisely isn't important. Where you bought, at what price, how you set your rate, and your other debts also change the outcome. In the end, even if two people bought similar apartments the same year , the results differ for everyone. And even now, young people are still buying homes. This February, 265 people in their 30s bought apartments in Nowon-gu , the highest number in 5 years and 1 month since January 2021. Of course, this doesn't mean they all took on excessive debt. But depending on the product, the loans taken today will also face a day when interest rates are reset in a few years. That is why when looking at a home, you must examine future monthly payments as much as home price forecasts. Now, I will give you three questions to check the status of your loan. First, do you know the date your loan interest rate is set to be adjusted? Start by checking if it’s a hybrid, periodic, or variable rate. This information is in your loan agreement or bank app. If you don't know the date, you need to check that first. Second, could you still pay your loan for a year if interest rates rose by 2 percentage points? Calculate the increased monthly payments based on your remaining principal and maturity. You need to factor in whether you’ll need to cut living expenses and how much emergency cash you’ll have left. Third, check recent transaction prices to see how much you would actually have left if you sold your home now, then subtract your remaining debt and selling costs. It’s also important to see if there are buyers at your desired price and if transactions are steady. These three questions are a starting point for assessing risk, not a checklist that guarantees safety. You must also verify the stability of your income and other debts. Still, knowing when and how much you have to pay, and how much would remain if you sold, makes a big difference. Five years ago, those who bought homes by scraping together every penny told each other that holding on would eventually lead to a win. That statement isn't entirely wrong. In fact, there are people who held on and profited from rising home prices. However, some could not make it past the time when interest rates were reset. While some sold their homes and cleared their debts, others lost their homes and were still left with debt. There was one missing condition in the saying that holding on eventually leads to a win. That condition is: provided you have the money left to hold on. What is your take on the choice to scrape together every last bit of savings to buy a home in 2021? Was it due to the greed of individuals who took on excessive debt? Or was it due to market anxiety that made people feel they would be left behind if they didn't buy a home?

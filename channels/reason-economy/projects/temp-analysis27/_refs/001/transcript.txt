It’s January 28, 1999, in the dead of winter, on the banks of the Han River in Yeouido. Over 8,000 employees of LG Semicon, who would normally be operating semiconductor lines, filled the riverside. They had only one demand . They were against the merger. A government-led "Big Deal" for conglomerates had concluded that this entire company must be handed over to its competitor, Hyundai Electronics. The head of the financial authorities had even nailed the coffin shut, threatening to cut off new loans if they refused. So, to save this company, the entire LG Group had to put its own financial lifeline on the line. In the end, unable to withstand the pressure, LG handed over the world’s 6th largest DRAM company to Hyundai for 2.56 trillion won. But wait. Now, exactly 27 years after LG left the semiconductor industry, that name has returned to the scene. This time, it's not the chips, but the blade that makes the chips. And they’re doing it with next-generation equipment that no one has perfected yet. Where did the semiconductors of the people who stood on that riverside that day actually go? And why did LG choose the blade instead of the chip? Let me tell you one thing first. Half of the factories of the world’s top AI memory company today were originally LG’s. On June 22, 2026, the#1 spot on the Korean stock market, which hadn't changed for 25 years and 7 months, finally shifted. SK Hynix’s market capitalization reached 208.03782 trillion won, surpassing Samsung Electronics’ common stock for the first time. The difference was 13 trillion won. It was a spot Samsung hadn't yielded even once since November 2000. When SK acquired the company in 2011, its market cap was just over 15 trillion won, so it has grown about 138 times in 15 years. That’s enough to make your jaw drop. Memory that runs right next to AI chips. The company that makes that is now South Korea’s number one. But take a look at the map of this company’s factories. It has two pillars. Icheon in Gyeonggi-do. And Cheongju in Chungbuk. Do you know what the industry calls these two? Icheon is the Hyundai side, and Cheongju is the LG side. The Cheongju semiconductor plant, which Goldstar built in 1988 by even diverting water from the Daecheong Dam. That is the current SK Hynix Cheongju Campus. While the sign changed from Hyundai Electronics to Hynix, and then to SK Hynix, one side of its ancestral roots was always LG Semicon. In a sense, half of the factories of the world’s#1 company originally had a different family's nameplate on the door. But the owner of that nameplate didn’t just give up the house willingly. The 8,000 people who stood on the Yeouido riverside. Those were the very LG Semicon family members who were running these factories. Usually, when a company is sold, people assume they must have lacked the technology. This company was the exact opposite. If you look at the 1997 global DRAM rankings, Samsung Electronics was at 18.8%, NEC at 12.1%, Hyundai Electronics at 9%, Hitachi at 8.2%, Micron at 7.9%, and right next to them was LG Semiconductor at 6.7%. It was a company that proudly ranked in the top ten in a field dominated by powerhouse semiconductor nations like Japan and the U.S. In June 1998, they were the first in the world to develop the 64MB Rambus DRAM, which Intel had tapped as the next-generation PC memory. By November of that same year, they even became the first in the world to sell the final mass-produced version. So why on earth did a company like this end up in someone else's hands? It was decided by paperwork, not by actual performance . Those who experienced the 1997 foreign exchange crisis firsthand will remember how chilling the term "Big Deal" was back then. It is said that the initial blueprint for the "Big Deal ," where the government forced major conglomerates to swap businesses, already earmarked Hyundai for automobiles, Samsung for semiconductors , and LG for petrochemicals. That is how much of a predetermined scenario it was from the very top. Then who was in charge of grading whether Hyundai or LG would be the owner of the semiconductor business? It was the American consulting firm ADL. Imagine this. You're taking an exam, but the proctor wasn't chosen by the school, but by the parent-teacher association. One student asks to see the grading criteria first, but is told to just take the test. When that student refuses to submit an answer sheet, the proctor grades them based solely on their school records. The student next to them submits a blank power of attorney and even opens up their own home to be inspected. It’s obvious how the results would turn out without even looking. In reality, ADL was the firm chosen by the Federation of Korean Industries, and LG refused to sign the contract, demanding that the evaluation criteria be disclosed first. So, ADL graded LG based only on public data, while Hyundai even allowed factory inspections based on their blank power of attorney. On December 24, 1998, the report card was released. Hyundai was consistently in the lead. On the same day, the head of the Financial Supervisory Commission declared that there would be no new loans if the results were rejected. On Christmas Eve , the report card and the ultimatum arrived all at once. Three days later at Gimpo Airport, Chairman Koo Bon-moo flatly stated that the ADL report was unfair. LG Semiconductor CEO Koo Bon-joon also held out, insisting they could never give up the semiconductor business. But here is what is even more baffling. Right in that very December, the semiconductor market was recovering , and LG Semiconductor was expecting a 40 billion won profit in that month alone. They were just about to start making money, and they were being told to give up the company. At this point, it’s enough to drive anyone crazy. And ten days after the day they said they would never give up, on January 6, 1999, Chairman Koo Bon-moo visited the Blue House and announced his intention to let go of the semiconductor business . Since LG was an electronics and telecommunications-centered group, semiconductors were essential, and it is said he could not hide his deep regret until the very end. It is said that he went into seclusion for four months, leaving word that he had decided to abandon everything. It was three weeks later that 8,000 people gathered in Yeouido. In the end, a 59.98%stake in LG Semicon was sold for 2.56 trillion won. One trillion won of that was on a one-year grace period and two-year installment plan; it was essentially handing over the company and getting paid in installments. So, was the party that acquired the company smiling? Far from it. The real irony begins right here. If you place the debt-to-equity ratios of both companies side-by-side from the first half of 1998, when the assessment was underway, it is staggering. Hyundai Electronics at 934.7%and LG Semicon at 617%; the side with more debt became the owner. That bill arrived much faster than expected. After merging the two companies, the interest alone came to 2.7 billion won per day. In a year, over 1 trillion won simply vanished without being able to do anything. It means that over 100 million won in interest was melting away every hour. But right at that moment, the sky itself collapsed. As the dot-com bubble burst in 2001, memory prices plummeted by about 80%. The 64M DRAM, which used to cost 7 to 8 dollars per unit, dropped below one dollar. They had to manufacture and sell chips that were worth less than a cup of coffee. That year, revenue was 4 trillion won, but the deficit was 5 trillion won. Can a company with more losses than earnings survive? Eventually, the company changed its name to Hynix and entered creditor-led management, so-called a workout. Then, someone reached out as if they had been waiting. It was Micron of the United States. The conditions were very blatant. They wanted to take only the cream of the crop, like factories and technology, and not inherit the tens of trillions of won in debt. It was like saying they would eat the meat and leave the bones behind. In 2002, the Hynix board of directors rejected this proposal. Instead of being sold off, they chose to survive on their own. A factory that had once passed into someone else's hands this time pushed those hands away. The problem was that there was no money to survive alone. Semiconductor manufacturing is a business where you have to bring in new equipment every year to stay afloat. But there was no money to buy new equipment. So, the project the engineers pulled out was the "Blue Chip Project." Instead of buying new machines, they tore down the existing equipment and redesigned the process from scratch to fit it. They achieved mass production at the 0.15- micron level, which others were doing with expensive, cutting-edge scanners, using old steppers. The investment cost was one-tenth of the conventional amount. The money saved in that way produced an investment effect of over 1 trillion won. They repaired machines that were about to be discarded and accomplished what others were doing with new ones. Since there was no money , they used their heads. And there is a foundation for this technology that the company has revealed itself. Following the 1999 integration with LG Semiconductor, the two companies combined their technologies. The technology from the side that lost it and the side that took it became one within the struggling company. Half of that intellect came from the people who stood on the banks of the Han River in Yeouido. Hynix graduated from its workout program 3 years and 9 months later, 1 year and 8 months ahead of schedule. That was in July 2005. Yet, no one wanted to buy the company that had been revived. When Hynix hit the market again in 2011, there was widespread speculation in the business world that LG would take it back this time. However, it is said that Chairman Koo Bon-moo didn't even give it a second look. The business world attributed the reason to the wounds of 12 years prior. Hyosung, Hyundai Heavy Industries, and STX all stepped away one by one. The last name left was SK. On the acquisition deadline of November 10, 2011, the SK board meeting was only able to convene at 3 p.m. The opposition was that strong, with concerns about whether they would be overwhelmed by taking on a company their own size. It is said that Chairman Chey Tae-won saw the big picture and felt the timing was right. He reportedly pushed for it, saying, "I will carry this through." The acquisition price was 3.4267 trillion won. During the quarter when the acquisition was taking place, there was a deficit of 290.9 billion won. At the time, everyone called it the "winner's curse." Now, everyone calls it a " stroke of genius." But there remained one problem that no one spoke of loudly . We had secured the memory, but the machines that produced it were not our own. Imagine this. You have the world's best chef. Their cooking skills are beyond compare. But every knife, oven, and grill in that kitchen is borrowed from next door. What happens when the neighbor suddenly asks for them back? The kitchen stops operating that very day. This was the true face of our semiconductor industry. When the U.S. added HBM to the export control list against China in December 2024, about 95%of the world's HBM was Korean-made. Yet, according to Nikkei Asia, South Korea holds a mere 2%share of the global semiconductor equipment market. The localization rate for domestic semiconductor equipment has also remained at around 20%for 15 years, down from 22%in 2011. This means that if Samsung and SK pour 100 trillion won into factories a year, about 80 trillion won goes to the U.S., Europe, and Japan. You remember when Japan blocked material exports in 2019, don't you? At that time, we raised the localization rate for some materials from 10–20%to the 70%range. But the equipment rate stayed at 20%. The U.S. showed us directly just how frightening this is. On October 7, 2022, while blocking China, the U.S. cut off the equipment used to make chips, not just the chips themselves. It was a measure requiring a permit to sell equipment to Chinese firms making DRAM under 18nm. Reuters assessed this as the biggest shift in technology policy toward China since the 1990s. It proved to the whole world that those holding the sword can close others 'front doors. However, just a few years earlier, a small piece of equipment was growing where no one was looking. Equipment is not sold based on performance alone. A fresh graduate applies to companies, but the answer is always the same. Do you have any experience? You can't get hired because you lack experience, and you can't gain experience because you aren't hired. Semiconductor equipment is exactly the same. If it hasn't run on an actual mass-production line, no one will buy it, no matter how good it is. Hanmi Semiconductor broke through this wall by co-developing with a client. The challenge was equipment that stacks and bonds chips. If you increase the bonding hands from one to two, the speed will surely increase. But if two hands move simultaneously, their mutual vibrations cause the precision to collapse. Think about two people trying to thread a needle on the same desk at the same time. If one person even slightly moves their elbow, the desk shakes and the other person's needle misses. On a chip, that miss happens at a scale much smaller than the thickness of a hair. In 2016, engineers from Hanmi and SK Hynix stayed on the line to fix this vibration together. As soon as the demo equipment passed quality testing, it went straight into the production line. In February 2017, its productivity was double that of the Japanese competitor, while also saving floor space. But at the time, almost no one paid attention to this news. The HBM that the equipment was stacking was a niche memory used only in supercomputer accelerators or some high-end graphics cards. The world's eyes were focused only on Samsung's earnings and DRAM prices. No one knew what that small piece of equipment would trigger a few years later. Then, as the massive wave of AI rolled in, that niche memory moved to the center of the world. What is HBM? AI chips calculate incredibly fast, but if the path for data is narrow, that fast brain sits idle. So, HBM is memory stacked high right next to the chip. Imagine this. If regular memory is a detached house on a large plot, HBM is a 16-story apartment built on a plot the size of a fingernail. Since you can't expand outward, you build upward. But elevators and water pipes must connect perfectly on every floor, and if even one floor is misaligned, the whole building is useless. The problem is the thickness of each of those floors. To stack 16 layers, you have to shave each floor down to 30 micrometers. That's less than half the diameter of a human hair. Even for 12-layer chips, they were 50 micrometers, but you have to shave them down further to stack 16 layers. And those 16 stacked layers must fit within a height of 775 micrometers. Since a credit card is about 760 micrometers thick, it's like cramming 16 chips into the height of a single card. The machine that bonds these chips, which are thinner than paper, is the TC bonder. You can think of it as a machine that combines a hot iron with precision tweezers. It melts the solder bumps on every layer with heat and pressure, pressing and bonding them one by one. If even one layer goes slightly wrong, the entire stack above and below becomes defective. So, who in the world sold the most of these tricky machines? As of the third quarter of 2025, 71.2% of all TC bonders for HBM worldwide were from Korea’s Hanmi Semiconductor . This figure was compiled by TechInsights, a firm specializing in semiconductor analysis. The cumulative revenue is 247.7 million dollars. That’s about 366 billion won in our currency. That is seven out of every ten units. Not a US company, not a Japanese one, but a single Korean company. In 2017, that equipment, which no one paid attention to, has seized the global market in just eight years. But wait. This is not the end. Micron of the US was a company that originally used equipment from Japan's Shinkawa. However, industry analysis suggests that starting from 12-layer HBM, they are only using Hanmi equipment. There have even been reports that switching to Hanmi equipment has increased yields . It is said that Shinkawa has also lost its position as a key supplier to Samsung due to performance issues. A US company has put down Japanese equipment and picked up Korean equipment. Do you know how strange a scene this is in the semiconductor equipment industry? For decades, it was always the other way around. Korea was a country that bought and used Japanese and American equipment. From Japan's perspective, it must be absolutely maddening. The result is clearly reflected in the revenue figures. In the first half of 2025, the overseas share of Hanmi Semiconductor’s revenue jumped from 22%to 82%in just one year. Just a year ago, only about 2 out of every 10 won of revenue came from abroad, but now over 8 won comes from across the sea. It means Korean equipment is filling American memory factories. They have accumulated around 150 patents related to HBM equipment, and there are even forecasts that revenue will exceed 1 trillion won in 2026. Wall Street’s assessment is even hotter. The TC bonder market is growing by 13%annually until 2030, and Hanmi, with its overwhelming share, is expected to grow even faster than the market. Growing faster than the market means they are further expanding their current position. Do you understand what this means? A country that used to cook with knives borrowed from others is now selling this one type of knife to the world. It’s even pushing out Japanese tools to dominate the U.S. market. The equipment card that the U.S. wields like a weapon against China is, in this domain, held by South Korea. This wasn’t just a job well done. It was the event that turned the tide, moving us from a country that makes chips to one that sells the tools to make them. Do you know who really made money during the Gold Rush? It was the people selling pickaxes, not the ones digging for gold. We are currently making those very pickaxes for the AI Gold Rush. Hearing this, you might think HPSP is truly remarkable, right? But what’s truly surprising is that this isn't just one tree, but an entire forest. The runner-up in the same market was a Samsung affiliate with 13.1%. Just 1%. Third and fourth place were Singapore's ASMPT and Japan's Yamaha at 5.6%each, and fifth was Hanwha CEMA at 3.2%. Three of the top five are Korean, and together they account for 87.5%. That means nearly nine out of ten machines are Korean-made. You probably think that’s it for the Korean equipment story, right? But a name nobody expected has returned to the game. It’s LG. Of those who stood in Yeouido 27 years ago, who could have imagined that name would return in this form? Even after losing its semiconductor business in 1999, LG held onto one wafer manufacturer, LG Siltron . But in 2017, they sold a 51%stake in that last semiconductor materials company to SK for 620 billion won. The newspaper headlines that day read that they had completely exited the semiconductor business after 28 years. It was a company that walked away not once, but twice. Then, eight years later, in 2025, a single news report sent ripples through the industry. It was the news that LG Electronics' Production Engineering Research Institute had jumped into developing HBM hybrid bonders. They aren't trying to make memory chips again. They set out to build the machine the industry calls the "dream equipment," the successor to the TC bonder. Here is what that is. Current TC bonders melt solder bumps between chips to attach them. If you think of it like Lego blocks, it’s like stacking them by putting a piece of gum between each block. The tower gets taller by the thickness of the gum, and it gets crooked as the gum squishes. Hybrid bonding eliminates the gum entirely and sticks the block surfaces directly together. In reality, it bonds copper surfaces directly to copper without any solder. The gap between chips shrinks from tens of micrometers to nearly zero . You can stack more layers at the same height, and it generates less heat. But the precision required is on a whole different level. The tolerance is plus or minus 100 nanometers. It’s like drawing a line from one end of a soccer field to the other without deviating by even 1mm. So, exactly where does LG stand right now? A total of 14 billion won. Of that, 7.5 billion won in government funding is being poured into a national project involving a consortium of five organizations, running until 2029. LG Production Engineering Research Institute has taken on the bonding head, the heart of alignment precision. They are currently in the testing phase using a prototype, with commercialization targeted for 2028 to 2029. But the real point isn't just one piece of equipment. LG Production Engineering Research Institute is also developing laser equipment to draw 1.5-micrometer to 5- micrometer circuits on semiconductor substrates, inspection equipment to examine HBM from all six sides, and even glass substrate equipment. As of April 2026, they have even begun reviewing etching equipment used to carve out circuits. They are aiming for a "turnkey" solution, selling the entire set of back-end process equipment as a single package. The idea is to have customers entrust them with the entire back-end line instead of buying equipment one by one. Remember the barrier for inexperienced newcomers I mentioned earlier? This organization originally generated 90%of its revenue from LG affiliates. So, they chose to build their track record by first supplying equipment to LG Innotek before expanding outward. Industry experts say the fact that LG Electronics is entering hybrid bonding is in itself a significant market signal. Even before full-scale sales, the mere name has made the market take notice. The very same LG that gave up on chips 27 years ago and wafers 8 years ago. It's as if a general who had his sword taken away has returned, having opened his own smithy to forge his own. LG is back. It could have just ended as a heartwarming story here. But let me tell you just how big this game really is. In April 2025, Applied Materials, the world’s largest semiconductor equipment company, quietly acquired a 9%stake in the Dutch firm Besi. Because Besi is the dominant leader in the hybrid bonding equipment market, holding over 40%share. The giants know better than anyone that whoever holds the next-generation sword will win the next round. In 2026, Reuters reported that Applied Materials and Lam Research were even considering a full takeover of Besi. It’s a game that the American giants are willing to buy entire companies just to dominate. Right in the middle of that game, Korean companies—Samsung, Hanmi, Hanwha, and even LG—have lined up. Just as the TC bonder was Korea’s win, they are saying Korea will face the next round head-on as well. As the number of layers increases, there isn't enough space due to the thickness of the solder bumps. This means the first customer for domestic hybrid bonders is right here in the same country.

This spring, a coffee factory belonging to the world's largest food company in Cheongju, North Chungcheong Province, quietly stopped production. It was a Nescafé factory that Switzerland's Nestlé had operated for 12 years in partnership with Lotte, the top distributor in Korea. The accumulated losses over 12 years exceeded 70 billion won, and capital that once surpassed 50 billion won vanished, leaving only 100 million won behind. The market performance is even more shocking. In the Korean coffee mix market, Nestlé’s share was barely one out of a hundred, while Maxim commanded 90. A company that sells products in over 180 countries and earns around 150 trillion won annually failed to beat a yellow-packet coffee that costs just over 200 won per stick. Moreover, this happened in the instant coffee sector that they themselves introduced to the world. Since Nestlé first knocked on the door of the Korean coffee market in 1989, it has been exactly 37 years. Why did a company that ranks first across the globe repeatedly fail only in Korea for 37 years? The journey to find that answer takes us through IMF-era offices, miners trapped 190 meters underground, and an engineer who passed away this spring at the age of 100. By the time this video ends, you will look at those yellow packets in senior centers, by water coolers, and in office pantries with entirely different eyes. The starting point of our story is an era when coffee was treated as contraband. Most coffee distributed domestically in the 1960s were products that leaked out from U.S. military bases. It was a time when dollars leaked out with every cup of coffee sold at local tea houses. In May 1968, a company called Dongsuh Foods was established in a building in Namdaemun-ro, Seoul, and in 1970, they brought in technology from America's General Foods to start making Maxwell House coffee in our own factories. This product was the first instant coffee produced domestically. The starting point was to save foreign currency by replacing smuggled coffee with domestic products. However, simply producing coffee did not solve the problem. It was a time when even milk to put in coffee was difficult to find. Households equipped with refrigerators were also rare. So, in 1974, Dongsuh Foods developed a powdered creamer made from palm oil with our own hands. The name of that product was none other than Frima. Older generations still call all coffee creamers simply "Frima. " Frima is one of the few items for which Dongsuh Foods has even re-registered the trademark. This part takes on significant meaning later. The origin of that expression was right here. It is a case where a brand name has become the term for an entire product category. In fact, the company's situation was quite unstable at the time. The president stepped down due to financial issues in 1973, and the major shareholder passed away the following year. The person who took over the leaderless company in 1974 was Kim Jae-myung, the former president of Cheil Jedang. The first card the new owner played to reorganize the company was people. An engineer joined as vice president just before the company’s new factory was built. That person was Cho Pil-je. Born in Gyeongnam in 1925, he was part of the first graduating class of the Department of Aerospace Engineering at Seoul National University. An engineer who studied airplanes and ships, he worked on building factories for Samsung-affiliated companies. He even led the project to establish the coffee plant. It was unexpected for such a person to come in as the head of technology for a food company. Two years after he arrived, something happened. One day in 1976, an employee in charge of quality control made a suggestion. Coffee, cream, and sugar. If we pre-mix these three and package them, wouldn't customers just need to add water? This is a scene Vice President Cho Pil-je later recorded in his memoirs. At the time, coffee shops had a "national formula." One part coffee, two parts cream, two parts sugar. Depending on the customer or the server, the taste varied; some days it was bitter, and some days it was sweet. The idea was to measure that formula precisely at the factory and put it into a bag. It sounds like an obvious idea now, doesn't it? But back then, no coffee company in the world had ever turned that idea into a product. If you put those three powders in one bag, they absorb moisture and turn into a hardened lump. It was a challenge that could only be solved because of the powder-handling technology accumulated while making Prima. In December of that year, the world's first coffee mix was born. It wasn't the long, stick shape we see today, but a square bag that cost 45 won each. Considering the prices back then, a bowl of jajangmyeon was 200 won. A cup of coffee at a coffee shop cost a little over 100 won. It meant you could drink coffee without going to a cafe for less than half the price of a cafe coffee. It didn't sell like hotcakes right from the start. At first, it was known as outdoor coffee to take along when hiking or fishing. Because if you just brought hot water in a thermos, you could have a cup of coffee even at the top of a mountain. Vice President Cho Pil-je did not rest. Two years after releasing the coffee mix, in 1978, he immediately tackled the next project. In 1980, the result of that work, Maxim, was launched. It was a premium coffee with preserved aroma, made using a freeze-drying method. What's interesting is the label "Maxim." It was originally a brand that the American company General Foods launched in 1964 but soon abandoned. A name that disappeared in the U.S. crossed over to Korea and became the name of the nation's favorite coffee. This story about the label will come up once more later on. Please make sure to remember it. From 1983, the actor Ahn Sung-ki began appearing in the advertisements. "I want to meet someone with a warm heart." Do you remember that? This is where the record for Korea's longest-running advertising model was born, as one person modeled for a single company's coffee for over 30 years. In 1987, the slim stick-shaped packet we know today made its debut. There is a sophisticated design hidden inside this stick. The coffee, creamer, and sugar were packed in order without being mixed. So, if you hold the end of the packet with your fingers while pouring, you can reduce the amount of sugar. It was a product that even considered those who didn't like it too sweet. Still, until the early 1990s, the square packets sold better in big cities like Seoul and Busan, while the stick packets were more popular in the provinces. Packet coffee was still just a supporting role . This is the story of how Korean coffee took its first steps. However, in that very same year, 1987, the restrictions on coffee imports were lifted. And a world-class company boarded a plane to Korea. Let me tell you what kind of company Nestlé is. It started with baby formula in Switzerland in the 1860s and is now the largest food company in the world. It is also the company that introduced instant coffee to the world. Nescafé remains the best-selling instant coffee in the world today. KitKat chocolate, Nescafé, and Nespresso capsules all belong to this company. That company established Nestlé Korea in October 1987 and entered the market in 1989 with a coffee called Taster's Choice. They didn't come empty-handed, either. Nestlé had already established a joint venture with a public corporation in charge of rural development in 1979 and even built a factory in Cheongju, North Chungcheong Province, in 1981. This factory, which initially produced baby food, became their coffee hub. Later on , the coffee made here would be exported to over 30 countries, including the U.S., Japan, and China. It was the very factory I mentioned was stopped at the beginning of the video. The Taster's Choice they brought in was no small rival either. It was synonymous with the premium freeze-dried coffee that emerged in the U.S. in the 1970s. They brought in coffee made in the same way as Maxim, but with the prestige of a world-class brand name. The timing was impeccable. '88. It was a time right after the Olympics when anything foreign was automatically perceived as better. Sophisticated foreign coffee ads poured onto television, and brown glass jars lined the supermarket shelves. What do you think the result was? Within one year of landing, they held 19%of the market share, reaching 40%in five years ; four out of every ten cups were Nestlé. For Dongsuh Foods, which had been running solo for nearly 20 years, the future must have looked bleak. The common belief was that if the world's number one player entered with determination, local firms couldn't hold out. That was the conventional wisdom of the time. Dongsuh’s share, which had been near-monopolistic, began to shrink visibly. Dongsuh Foods stood at a crossroads here. Should they fight fire with fire using the same glass-bottled coffee as Nestlé? Or should they seek victory elsewhere? What Dongsuh chose was the coffee packet. In 1989, the very year Nestlé landed, they launched Maxim Mocha Gold. That yellow box. They called the perfect ratio of coffee, creamer, and sugar to suit the Korean palate the " golden ratio" within the company. While glass bottles were accepted anywhere in the world, this ratio was a product tailored specifically to Korean taste buds. Nestlé went in the opposite direction. A method that worked globally. So, they put their strength into instant coffee in bottles. They only began making coffee mix in earnest once the 2000s rolled around. From the perspective of their global headquarters, it wasn't a strange choice. Coffee packets were a peculiar item found only in Korea, while bottled coffee was a proven product in over 100 countries. However, that decade-long time gap would later decide the outcome . And the event that decisively widened that gap didn't hit the companies, but the entire country. It was the winter of 1997, the IMF financial crisis. Do you remember? The office scenery back then. Until then, every company had an employee whose job was to make coffee. When a guest arrived, someone would measure out one part coffee, two parts creamer, and sugar to serve. But as people were let go due to restructuring , that culture vanished overnight. A world had arrived where even department heads and presidents had to make their own coffee. It also happened to be the time when water dispensers providing hot water were being installed in every office. One paper cup, one packet, hot water. No matter who made it, it was ready in exactly 10 seconds. It was also a time when people had to save on the cost of coffee bought outside. Coffee meant for outdoors, like on mountains or at fishing spots, moved into the center of offices and home kitchens. During this period, the coffee mix market grew by an average of 24%every year. It is as if, during the country's coldest winter, this was what Koreans held in their hands. The center of the market shifted from glass bottles to coffee packets. The coffee mix market, which was worth 200 billion won in 2000, swelled to 1.35 trillion won by 2012. It grew nearly sevenfold in 12 years. As the 2000s progressed, the share of stick-type coffee among instant coffees continued to grow, eventually accounting for nine out of every ten. Dongsuh Foods took full advantage of this trend. Starting in 1996, they began a process of completely revamping the taste, aroma, and packaging every four years. During some four-year cycles, they conducted over 100 consumer surveys annually to prepare for the next overhaul. This meant that whenever the taste preferences of Koreans changed, the ratio of the stick coffee changed along with it. It was a battle between a company bringing in flavors dictated by its global headquarters and a company re-evaluating the Korean palate every four years. By the time Nestle finally released a proper stick coffee, the Korean palate had already been accustomed to the ratio found in the yellow sticks. Nestle, which had focused its efforts on bottled coffee, saw its numbers start to reverse. Their market share, which had been four out of ten, dropped back to two out of ten, falling into the single digits. Dongsuh Foods reclaimed eight out of ten. Hearing this, you might think Dongsuh pushed Nestle out. However, it wasn't Dongsuh Foods that dragged Nestle down to third place. Seeing the market grow, large corporations jumped in one after another in 2010. First, in July, Lotte Chilsung launched Cantata Coffee Mix. Despite being a powerhouse in the beverage industry, their market share three years later was barely over one percent. In December, Namyang Dairy Products, a maker of powdered milk and dairy, joined the fray. They came out with a bold claim. They stated that instead of the synthetic chemical additives used in coffee creamer, they used real milk. This advertisement was judged by the KFDA to be disparaging to others, but it had already made its mark on the consumers 'minds. Within six months of its release, Namyang surpassed Nestle in major supermarkets to climb to second place. In 2012, they sold over 200 billion won worth of coffee mix alone, doubling their sales in just one year. Nestle's numbers fell noticeably. Their market share, which was 13 percent in 2010, fell to nine percent the following year. It dropped to six percent in the first half of 2012. By 2012, Nestle had definitively become a solid third place. The world's number one company had been pushed aside by a dairy company in Korea. In 2013, even Nongshim, the king of ramen, entered the market. The founding chairman personally oversaw everything from development to naming, pledging to reach a double-digit market share within three years. What was the result ? That coffee, which supposedly contained deer antler extract, was criticized for reminding people of ramen soup and vanished from supermarket shelves within three years. The number one in beverages, the number one in ramen, and the number one in the world. None of them could gain any traction in this market. Even the sensational Namyang ended up remaining in the single digits. The industry called the coffee mix market the " graveyard of challengers." In the midst of this, in August 2012, Nestle retired the Taster's Choice brand in Korea after 23 years. The company's explanation was as follows. It was a global headquarters policy to focus on the Nescafe brand. It sounds polite, but they essentially removed a name tag that once accounted for one in ten cups . And two years later, Nestle played its final card. Lotte, having tasted failure after trying to enter the coffee mix market alone, reached out first. In June 2014, the two companies each contributed half the capital to establish Lotte-Nestle Korea. Lotte alone invested 50 billion won. On top of that, Lotte paid 40.8 billion won in advance to Nestle headquarters, covering half of the technology royalty fees for the next 30 years. It was essentially a dowry, signaling a commitment to be together for 30 years. Just think about it. The coffee of the world's largest food company and the shelves of the largest retail company in Korea became one. On the launch day, the Lotte representative said this. "We hope to bring a fresh breeze to the domestic coffee market by leveraging Nestle's product competitiveness and Lotte's strengths." They also said this joint venture would be a stepping stone to becoming a global company. Newspapers ran headlines stating that Lotte and Nestle had joined hands to challenge Dongsuh. But the number one player didn't just sit idle during that time. As coffee shops appeared on every corner in 2011, Dongsuh Foods introduced a black stick coffee called Kanu. It was a product that removed both sugar and cream. They created a product that would take customers away from their own yellow packets. It was a calculation to break one's own rice bowl before someone else did. This Kanu sold over 100 billion won within 10 years. That was the exact place the joint venture chose as its first battlefield. They figured that if they avoided the yellow packets and targeted the growing black stick market, they would have a chance. But Kanu already held 80 percent of that market, while Nestle held only 7 percent. The door did not open in the new market either. A fresh breeze did not blow. The joint venture recorded massive losses for three consecutive years from the start. The market share, which was 5 percent in 2012 before they joined hands, actually fell further after they partnered. Nestle’s annual sales in Korea, which exceeded 400 billion won when they were independent, shrank to 273.7 billion won in 2015. Their coffee mix market share was 7 percent in the spring of 2017, and it dropped to less than 1 percent by the end of 2024. Sales actually decreased from 284.4 billion won in the first year of operation to 240 billion won in 2023. And it's as if the two companies made an announcement on March 17, 2025. That they would stop factory operations and liquidate the company by the first quarter of 2026. After that, they went through the process of reducing the capital, which exceeded 50 billion won, down to 100 million won and recorded a loss of about 63 billion won in just the first half of that year. The technology usage fees, paid in advance for 30 years, were used for only 12 years before coming to an end. Regarding whether they can get the remaining money back, Lotte only stated that they would consult with Nestle headquarters during the liquidation process. The Cheongju factory was shut down this spring. The same Nongshim that tried its hand at coffee mix before withdrawing. It is as if companies that tasted bitterness in this market have now joined hands. However, there was a day when the entire nation realized that this packet was more than just convenient coffee. On October 26, 2022, a mine tunnel collapsed at a zinc mine in Bonghwa, Gyeongbuk. Two people were trapped 190 meters underground. They were a 62-year-old foreman and his 50- year-old assistant. A day passed, then three days, then a week. Everyone said it would be difficult. But after 221 hours, late on the night of November 4th, the two men walked out on their own two feet. When asked how they survived, the foreman told the following story. Before going in, I grabbed about 30 packets from a coffee mix box. On the first evening, I mixed two packets in a paper cup and told my colleague. "This is our dinner for tonight, so let's eat." The two built a tent with plastic sheets, collected dripping water, and survived by sharing that coffee like a meal. Each packet has 50 calories and 6 grams of sugar. The warm coffee also helped them maintain their body temperature. Since that day, the number of people keeping coffee mix as emergency food has increased significantly.

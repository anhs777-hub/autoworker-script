Hey, you know if we just blow that up, we’re all going to prison, right? In the summer of 1998, the faces of officials gathered in a meeting room in Ansan, Gyeonggi Province, had turned pale. They had poured a staggering 620 billion won into blocking the sea, only for it to turn into pitch-black sewage. The smell was so foul that people driving by would literally vomit. Leaving it alone was a national disgrace, but tearing it down meant being dragged to the Board of Audit and Inspection for wasting tax money. It was a complete disaster by any account, but that’s when a brilliant trick emerged. "Since we have to open it anyway, why don't we attach some propellers to try and at least break even?" In June 1987, hundreds of massive dump trucks were lined up off the coast of Oido, Siheung, Gyeonggi Province. Back then, South Korea was truly starved for land. As the Middle East construction boom ended, countless heavy equipment and workers who had been working there were left with nowhere to go. The government needed a plan to put that idle equipment to work , expand land, and create jobs. A grandiose plan was hatched to reclaim tidal flats sixty times the size of Yeouido in Seoul to build factories and farms. That was the beginning of the Sihwa Lake reclamation project. "Chief, the current here is no joke.""Even if we dump rocks, they get swept away at a speed of 8 meters per second.""Hey, we succeeded in the deserts of the Middle East; can't we handle the current of the West Sea?""Tie bigger boulders together and dump them in." Field workers fought the sea day and night, braving freezing winter winds. It was a massive undertaking to pile up a giant stone mountain across a 12.7 km stretch connecting Daebudo and Oido. It took a full six and a half years, and the construction costs alone reached 620 billion won. At the time, 620 billion won was an enormous fortune that would be worth several trillion won today. On January 24, 1994, the final dam project to block the last remaining waterway was completed. The whole country was buzzing, claiming the largest seawall in Asia had been built, perfectly blocking off the sea. High-ranking officials flocked to the site to cheer and toast to the success. "Now that rainwater fills this massive lake, the seawater will turn into sweet freshwater.""We have created a miracle lake where we can farm and run factories without worrying about droughts." Newspapers splashed it on their front pages, saying the map had changed, and officials were grinning from ear to ear, dreaming of promotions . But it took only two years for that festival to turn into a nightmare. The very idea of blocking the sea to create a freshwater lake was a miscalculation from the start. The only rivers flowing into the lake were thin streams passing through the Banwol and Sihwa industrial complexes and the cities of Ansan and Siheung. Do you think environmental regulations were properly enforced back then? Or do you think there were adequate sewage treatment plants in place? Factories secretly dumped untreated wastewater every night, and new apartment complexes poured out domestic sewage like a waterfall. In the past, the tides came in and out twice a day, sweeping all that dirty water away into the West Sea. But with a 620-billion-won concrete barrier blocking the flow, what do you think happened? By the spring of 1996, Lake Sihwa had turned into a giant toilet. With the water unable to escape, it stagnated and rotted, while black, foul-smelling gas bubbled up from the lake bed. The conversations between Water Resources Corporation employees and Ansan residents at the time are staggering to hear even now. "Sir, it looks like industrial wastewater flooded in again after the rain last night.""The color of the water is literally the color of soy sauce."" Don't even get me started.""I came out this morning and saw hundreds of thousands of mullet and gobies floating belly-up, rotting away.""Wow, this smell is making my head feel like it's going to split.""They say drivers passing over the seawall opened their windows and ended up vomiting everywhere." Fishermen cast their nets, only to pull up clumps of black filth instead of fish, beating the ground and wailing. Migratory birds flying overhead drank the rotten water, died in droves, and their carcasses littered the shores of the lake. When the water quality was measured, the chemical oxygen demand level had soared to a staggering 17.4 ppm. Usually, it needs to be under 8 ppm for agricultural use, but this was toxic wastewater that would cause skin disease just by dipping your hand in, let alone using it for farming. The media showed up every day with cameras, bombarding the project and calling Lake Sihwa the " Lake of Death." It was madness. Government buildings and the Water Resources Corporation offices were practically mourning houses. They had boasted about expanding the nation's territory for 620 billion won, only to create the worst environmental disaster in Korean history. The urgent shouts from high-ranking officials in their meetings echoed down the hallways. "Hey , bring me a countermeasure right now!" "The Blue House is calling every day, and the National Assembly is going crazy about holding a government audit! ""Deputy Director, we have plans for sewage treatment plants upstream, but even if we build them all, the water inflow is so low that a freshwater lake is impossible, no matter what.""What?"" Then what are you telling me to do?"" You want me to blow up the wall we spent 620 billion to build with dynamite and turn it back into the sea? ""If we do that, we'll all be dragged to the Board of Audit and Inspection for breach of trust and wasting the budget, lose our jobs, and go to prison ." This was the truly absurd yet nerve-wracking inner reality of the people involved. Environmental groups wore headbands and protested, demanding that the seawall be torn down immediately to save the mudflats. But for government officials and construction workers, tearing down the seawall was tantamount to cutting their own throats. It would be a confession that 620 billion won had been blown away, and who would take responsibility for that administrative failure? Yet, they couldn't just leave it as it was, because the stench and public outcry had become unbearable. Eventually, as a last resort, they secretly began to open a small drainage gate that had been built in a corner of the seawall. "Sir, if we open the floodgates during the day and get caught by reporters, they’ll make a huge fuss about us giving up on desalination.""That's why you should open it bit by bit without anyone noticing, let out just enough rotten water, and mix in the seawater." "Call it a dilution operation." At first, they let in small amounts of seawater in this deceptive, superficial way. It was like struggling to mix a few drops of carbonated water into a lake filled with floating oil slicks. They even poured tens of billions of won more into creating artificial wetlands inside the lake, hoping the reeds would purify the filthy water. But there was no way a lake created by blocking a massive sea could be purified by one tiny floodgate. Finally , in November 1998, the government could no longer hold out and announced its surrender. They officially and completely abandoned the plan to make Lake Shihwa a freshwater lake, which had been the core of the largest land reclamation project since the founding of the nation. It was an unprecedented event where, just four years after completion, they admitted the failure of a national project to the whole world. In most other countries, the project would have completely collapsed at this point, with everyone busy shifting blame onto each other. In fact , overseas, there are plenty of eyesores that have been abandoned for decades after reckless seawall construction caused nothing but water pollution. But the truly formidable thing about Koreans started right from this ruined situation. They had admitted failure and decided to circulate seawater, but a mountain of realistic problems lay right in front of them. To completely replace all the water in Shihwa, a massive amount of seawater has to flow in and out every day. The existing small drainage gate could not flush out the stagnant, rotten water in every corner of the lake. Technicians from the Water Resources Corporation and marine researchers put their heads together and crunched the numbers. "Doctor, how much seawater needs to flow in and out daily to lower the Shihwa water quality to the level of the open sea?""Based on my calculations, about 150 million tons , which is half of the lake’s total volume, needs to flow in and out every day.""To do that, we have to cut through the middle of the current seawall by over 100 meters and build a massive new floodgate.""But the construction cost just to cut through the middle of the seawall will be at least 150 billion to over 200 billion won." Would the Ministry of Economy and Finance ever grant a budget to replace the water for a project that's already failed? Who would just sit by and let that happen? After flushing 620 billion won down the drain, asking for another 200 billion to cut a hole to clear out filthy water meant I’d be fired the moment I brought in the paperwork. Budget officials and K-water staff began losing sleep and wracking their brains. Either way, we were in a corner where the seawall absolutely had to be breached. If we didn't, land prices in the new cities and industrial complexes being built around Shihwa would plummet , leading to losses in the trillions. That’s when an engineer slapped the conference table and threw out a brilliant idea. Wait a minute. 150 million tons of seawater is going to pour through that hole every day anyway , right? The west coast has the largest tidal range in the world; isn't it a waste to just let the water move back and forth? "A waste?" What are you talking about? We're already busy trying to stop the water from rotting; instead of just putting a floodgate in that hole, let's stick a giant watermill-like generator in there. When the seawater pours into the lake, we can use that immense water pressure to turn a turbine and generate electricity . Let’s build a tidal power plant. Hey, then the construction cost jumps from 150 billion to 350 billion won— that’s double! Sir, just think about it. If we ask for 150 billion won for water quality improvement, we'll be called a "tax-eating hippo" and the budget will get cut. But if we say we're building the world's largest eco-friendly power plant to recover the costs by selling electricity every year , the justification changes entirely. Isn't that genius? If we just cut a hole, it will forever remain nothing but a cleanup cost for a failed project . But if we install a generator there to produce electricity, we could package it as a plausible new business that saves the environment and creates energy. On top of that, when we actually crunched the numbers, it wasn't just a simple trick—it was a profitable business. Normally, to build a tidal power plant, more than half of the total construction cost goes into building a dam—a seawall—to block the ocean. Back then, no one anywhere in the world could build tidal power plants because they weren't economically viable. But what about Shihwa Lake? We already had a 12.7 km sturdy seawall right in front of us, built at a cost of 620 billion won. Even though the plan to create a freshwater lake failed, if we recycled that wall as a power plant dam, we could slash the construction costs by more than half. The unique, pragmatic, and formidable Korean logic of recycling a failed facility to recoup our losses began to kick in. Since the dam is already built, all we have to do is cut the hole and pay for the generators. The government finally nodded in agreement with this logic, and in 2002, the construction plan for the Sihwa Tidal Power Plant was officially approved. But just because it was approved doesn't mean the construction was going to be smooth sailing. The task of breaking through a seawall already holding back the ocean and building a massive concrete structure 20 meters below the seabed was an unprecedented engineering challenge. It is said that when the Daewoo E&C technical team arrived at the site in late 2004, they were completely overwhelmed. Before they could even breach the seawall, they first had to build a temporary cofferdam to block the seawater around the construction site. The conversations engineers had on-site at that time were like those in the middle of a nerve-wracking battlefield. " Director, if we build a temporary wall with standard dirt mounds, the rough waves of the West Sea will collapse it, and the entire Sihwa seawall will burst .""Then we’ll build 27 massive steel cylinders, 20 meters in diameter, drive them down to the bedrock, fill them with stone and sand, and create a circular fortress that won't let a single drop of water in." They encircled the middle of the sea with giant steel cylinders, pumped out all the seawater to create dry land, and finally began tearing down the existing seawall. While others struggled just to block the sea once, Korean engineers blocked it, dug it out, and seated an entire power plant building as tall as a 20-story apartment complex underneath it. An even more troublesome issue was the process of importing the core turbine generators to power the plant. At that time, South Korea had never once had the experience of building a tidal power plant. The Rance Tidal Power Plant in France, built in 1966 and holding the title of the world's largest for over 40 years, was their only reference. The production of the turbine equipment was handled by Andritz, an Austrian company with the world's best technology in this field. It is said that the European engineers initially scoffed when they saw the Korean engineers 'blueprints. "Mr. Kim. ""Your calculations seem to be wrong, don't they?""You should be using a two-way system, generating power during both high and low tide to extract even a bit more electricity.""Why are you insisting on a one-way system that only generates power once when the tide comes in?""We cannot raise the lake level at all because Ansan City and the Sihwa Industrial Complex are located right next to the lake.""If we filled the lake to generate power during both tides, the entire city would be flooded when torrential rains pour down in the summer.""But if you keep the lake level low, you can only generate power once when the tide comes in; how do you expect to beat our European Rance power plant like that?" The European engineers shook their heads in disbelief. However, the Korean engineers had already run tens of thousands of computer simulations and completed their calculations to perfection. The method they found was a one-way strategy, sucking everything in at once when the tide came in. At low tide, the floodgates are opened wide to drain the dirty water from the lake into the sea, lowering the level until the lake bed is exposed. Then, when the tide rises outside and the difference between the lake and sea levels reaches 6 meters or more, they open the gates and pour the seawater in like a waterfall. Look closely, you Europeans. Instead of generating power inefficiently by letting water in and out little by little, if you empty the lake completely and drop the water from the maximum height, the force of the fall doubles. Furthermore, if you trap the clean seawater that came in and push it out all at once at low tide, even the rotten residue at the bottom of the lake gets swept away. It was a brilliant calculation to catch three rabbits at once: preventing floods, thoroughly cleaning the stagnant water, and maximizing power production. Even the Austrian and French engineers could not close their mouths in awe when they saw the meticulous calculations of the Koreans. It was an ingenious idea that could only come about because the original purpose was cleaning the lake and preventing flooding, rather than just electricity generation. The construction site was like walking on thin ice when the ten turbines were brought to Korea and assembled. Each generator was a massive monster, with blades reaching 7.5 meters in diameter and weighing a staggering 800 tons. We had to plant ten of these 800-ton steel chunks in the path of the seawater without a single margin of error. Sparks flew every day between the assembly foreman in charge of the site and the Austrian supervisor. If the gap between the blade and the concrete wall is off by even 2mm, the generator would be shattered by the vibration when hundreds of tons of seawater hit it per second. Can the Korean workers really align something that heavy with such precision? Don't worry about it. We are the people who have been aligning propellers for hundreds of thousands of tons of ships at the shipyards in Ulsan and Geoje to the thickness of a human hair. What is 2mm? We'll align it within a 0.5 mm margin, so just sit back and watch. Korea's veteran welders and mechanical engineers worked through the night to install those ten massive turbines perfectly without error. Then, in August 2011, seven years after the project began, the day finally dawned to blow up the temporary barrage and take in the first seawater. In the on-site control room, hundreds of water resource corporation employees and engineers held their breath and stared at the monitors. Someone swallowed hard and pressed the gate opening button. Boom-rumble. With a roar that shook the ground, the blue seawater of the West Sea began to be terrifyingly sucked into the ten holes. As tens of thousands of tons of water per second hit the 800-ton propellers, the ten massive generators began to spin in unison with a magnificent sound. The power production numbers on the control room display began to skyrocket like crazy. Breaking through 100MW, then 150MW, it surpassed 200MW. Vibration levels normal, bearing temperatures normal—all ten units hit the maximum output of 254MW. In an instant, the control room was filled with deafening cheers and applause. Seventeen years after they celebrated the completion of the seawall in 1994 only to fall into hell, a true miracle erupted in that very spot. With a facility capacity of 254MW and an annual generation of 552 million kWh, it was the moment they snatched the world champion belt, shattering the 240MW record held by France's La Rance Tidal Power Station, which had stood firm as number one for 45 years since 1966. To understand just how massive this amount is: It produces 1.3 times more electricity than the Soyanggang Dam in Chuncheon, which we commonly know as Korea's representative dam. An enormous amount of electricity, enough to power the entire city of Ansan with its 500,000 residents for a whole year, is pouring out for free without burning a single drop of oil, relying solely on the moon's gravity and the power of the sea. A golden goose was born, saving 860,000 barrels of oil imported from abroad every year and raking in hundreds of billions to nearly 100 billion won annually from the sea. Then, what happened to the original purpose of cleaning up the polluted water? As the generators turned and forcibly circulated 147 million tons of seawater every day— half of the lake's entire volume—a miracle occurred. Just a few years after the plant began operation, Sihwa's pollution level, which had soared to 17.4 ppm, dropped sharply to 1.8 ppm, the same level as the outer West Sea. The foul, nose-stinging stench vanished without a trace, and the jet-black mudflats began to teem with clams and crabs once again. The expressions of the merchants and fishermen who ran sashimi restaurants around Lake Sihwa changed completely. " Hey, Captain Kim, did you catch anything in your nets today?""Don't even get me started.""Back when it was a cesspool, you couldn't find a single goby, but these days, whenever the power plant gates open, sea bass and rockfish follow the tide and swarm into Lake Sihwa like clouds.""Haha.""My shop is packed with anglers and tourists every single weekend.""The place that was called a' dead lake 'where not a shadow of a person could be seen has become a total treasure trove." Rare birds, including natural monuments like otters, spoonbills, and Chinese egrets that had left, have returned by the hundreds of thousands to build their nests. Even people from environmental organizations were left speechless when they saw the resurrection of Lake Sihwa . But the real jackpot for the Sihwa Tidal Power Station started to hit as we moved into the mid-2020s. As the world has changed, a bolt from the blue regulation has struck companies across the globe. Global giants like Apple, BMW, and Google have issued an ultimatum to their Korean suppliers. If you use electricity generated from coal or gas to make your products, don't even dream of supplying us in the future. You must use electricity produced exclusively from 100% eco-friendly renewable energy. This terrifying trade barrier, known as RE100, has become an immediate reality. For major domestic companies, it’s a crisis that requires urgent action. Korea has limited land and many mountains, so there were clear limits to installing solar panels or wind turbines. Furthermore, solar power doesn't work when the sun sets or it rains, and wind power stops when there is no wind. Executives running semiconductor and chemical plants, which must operate 24/7, were burning with anxiety. "Sir, our European clients say they'll cancel our contracts if we don't bring green electricity certificates starting next year.""I’ve searched all the domestic solar power plants, but there’s not nearly enough supply.""Hey, isn't there any eco-friendly power that doesn't depend on weather and pumps out a massive, steady supply at fixed times every day?" Wait a minute. The time the moon rises and sets will never change unless the universe comes to an end. Don't we have the Sihwa Tidal Power Plant? It was an absolute jackpot. While solar and wind suffer from drops in power when it's cloudy, giving companies headaches, tidal power runs on the forces of the moon and Earth, so we can 100%predict exactly how much power will be generated a year or even ten years from now, down to the date and hour. Plus, it's located in Ansan, Gyeonggi-do, right on the doorstep of the capital region, so there's no worry about transmission lines, and its capacity is overwhelmingly the best among single domestic renewable power plants. Major companies, sensitive to the scent of money and the instinct to escape crises, were never going to sit still. Executives from major companies started lining up at the Korea Water Resources Corporation, wearing down their doorsteps. "Director, we are willing to pay a premium just to buy the electricity coming out of Lake Sihwa directly.""Let's sign the contract right now." In the end, in May 2024, Samsung Electronics, Korea's top company, rushed in and signed a direct purchase agreement for 254MW of power from the Sihwa Tidal Power Plant. Lotte Chemical followed suit in October of the same year, and a fierce battle broke out to secure power from Sihwa. The lake that once rotted from industrial wastewater has now become a relief pitcher, holding the export lifeline for Korea's leading high-tech companies. It wouldn't be like the Korean people if the story ended here. After punching holes in the seawall, engineers and officials crunched the numbers again, looking at the massive available land and the cleared-up lake. "Hey, we've already built the power plant.""And we've got an endless supply of seawater right next to the seawall." In fact, the observation tower and park built in the middle of the seawall now attract over 1.5 million tourists every year who are happy to open their wallets. On weekends, the road across the seawall toward Daebudo Island is packed with bumper-to-bumper traffic from visitors. The tidal power plant, which cost 355.1 billion won, has already recouped its investment and more through water quality improvement savings, electricity sales, and carbon credits. Nowadays, government officials from countries like the UK, Indonesia, and the Philippines even visit Sihwa, bowing their heads to ask Korean engineers for their expertise. It’s said that when the Liverpool City Region Mayor and his delegation visited , they couldn't stop expressing their awe while touring the site. "In the UK, we've been contemplating building a massive tidal plant on the River Mersey for decades, but you’ve already restored a polluted lake and succeeded with the world's largest plant.""What on earth is your secret?" In any country, when you launch a massive national project, plans can go awry and you are bound to face disastrous failures. Some bury those failures out of fear, while others just blame each other, leaving behind multi-trillion won eyesores to rot. However, the drama that unfolded off the coast of Lake Sihwa over the past 30 years shows just how formidable and persistent the Korean people are. Even today, at the bottom of the Sihwa seawall, ten 800-ton turbines are roaring as they draw in the seawater of the Yellow Sea.

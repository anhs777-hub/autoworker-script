The crown prince of Balhae, having lost his nation, crossed over into Goryeo with tens of thousands of followers. King Taejo granted him his own surname and added his name to the royal genealogy. In the thousand years since, the Jurchen, Mongols, Uyghurs, and even the Japanese have entered this land. However, China, divided into 56 ethnic groups, has none of the minority groups defined by our nation. It is not because they did not mix. If you watch until the end to see where those many people were recorded, you will find them in the column right next to your own name. In the last video, I talked about how China failed to swallow up this land in the end. Today, I have the opposite question. Aside from those who invaded, where did all the people who came to this land and settled down go? First, let’s look at a Chinese ID card. If you look at Article 3 of the Resident Identity Card Law created in 2003, the category for ethnicity appears right after name and gender. It means that if you are born in China, one of 56 ethnicities is written on the card you carry for life. But this number 56 was not there from the beginning. During the first census in 1953, China had people write down their own ethnic names, and over 400 names were submitted. According to research by historian Thomas Mullaney, more than half of them, over 260, came from Yunnan Province alone. The government examined these labels one by one. By 1954, the number of defined ethnic groups was 38. The next phase continued until 1964. At that time, 74 reported groups were consolidated into 53. In 1965, the Lhoba people were added, and the last one was the Jino people. The People's Daily newspaper introduces the Jino people like this. "Our country's 56th ethnic group, officially confirmed by the State Council in 1979." Here, " our country" refers to China. And in the 1982 census, the 55 minority groups were solidified. Adding the Han Chinese to this makes 56. Do you know what this means? 56 is not a number representing how many ethnic groups originally existed. The nation examined over 400 labels one by one, sorted them into chest drawers, and 56 is the final count of those organized compartments. Once you are placed in a drawer, the name of that compartment is printed on your card. Even after the compartments were set, people did not stay put. According to the Chinese media outlet The Paper, 5 million people requested to recover or change their ethnic identity during the 1982 census. By the mid-1990s, more than 12 million people had actually moved to different compartments. It means the chest of drawers could not contain everyone. The compartments soon became administration . Ethnic autonomous regions, where minority groups live in large numbers, numbered 155 according to the Chinese government in 2014. The drawers were drawn as boundary lines on this land. In the 2020 census, about 125 million people were recorded in the ethnic minority category. That is 8.89%of the total, but it’s a number more than double our own country's population. So , what about us? The Resident Registration Act, Article 24, lists the following for ID cards: name, photo, resident registration number, address, fingerprints, date of issue, and issuing agency. There is no category for ethnicity. In 2003, the UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination pointed out the lack of ethnic composition statistics in Korea and recommended providing estimates. It effectively meant that an international organization confirmed our country does not count people by ethnicity. So, the zero I’m talking about today means that the number of ethnic minorities officially categorized by the state is zero. However, I will draw a line here. There are many countries besides ours that do not ask about ethnicity. Researcher Ann Morning analyzed the 2000 census data from a staggering 141 countries. Among them, about 37%did not ask about ethnicity. That’s nearly four out of every ten countries. France has essentially banned ethnic statistics through a law enacted in 1978. Zero is not a strange number. Therefore, the real question is not " why is it zero?" The question is, in which column did we record the people who came here over the last millennium? China recorded people in an ethnic column, but we recorded them in a different column. What that column is —that is the answer to today's video. But there is an easy answer to this question that anyone can think of. Let’s first establish that answer properly. The easy answer is this. This land is surrounded by sea on three sides, and the north is blocked by high mountains and large rivers. A single dynasty lasted for hundreds of years. So, the idea is that the people of this land were originally one, and there were no separate ethnicities to record. Yes, it sounds plausible, doesn't it? Across the sea in Japan, the same thing was said. On January 13, 2020, Japanese broadcaster Ryukyu reported what Deputy Prime Minister Taro Aso said at a meeting in Fukuoka Prefecture. "There is no other country than this one where one language, one ethnic group, and one dynasty have continued for 2,000 years. " And the very next day, he retracted his statement, apologizing and correcting it in case any misunderstanding had occurred. Nearly 30 years earlier, official Japanese government documents had written something different. This is a sentence from a Japanese government response dated December 27, 1991. "The government does not claim that our country is a mono-ethnic nation." Here, "our country" refers to Japan. In 2008, the Japanese Diet passed a resolution to recognize the Ainu as an indigenous people, and in 2019, they were officially written into law as such. According to a 2017 survey in Hokkaido, there were 13,118 Ainu people. In Japan , too, a column has been created in the law. Let’s re-examine the answer we just established through our records. This land was not a dead end. It was closer to a corridor where people passed through and entered between the continent and the sea. The "Records of the Three Kingdoms," specifically the " Accounts of the Eastern Barbarians" in the "Wei Shu," recorded the country of Dongye as having 20,000 households. It is a record that various groups in this land and Manchuria lived side by side before being organized into the Three Kingdoms. Ssang Gi, who helped implement Goryeo’s civil service examination in 958 and served as its first examiner, was a person who came as an envoy from Later Zhou in China and stayed behind due to illness. When Kublai Khan’s daughter, Princess Jeguk, came to Goryeo as a queen in 1274, the people serving the princess entered with her. The "History of Goryeo" records that among them, the foreigner Samga changed his name to Jang Sun-yong. The Deoksu Jang clan, which considers him their founder, numbered 24,185 in the 2015 census. The "History of Goryeo" records that the Mongol, Honladae, who followed the same princess, said this to his close friend , the General In Hu. "Since you and I are such close friends, how about I borrow your surname?" That is how he became In Hu. However, Jang Sun-yong and In Hu are figures listed in the " Biographies of Favorites" in the " History of Goryeo," which documents subjects who gained the King’s favor and abused their power. You should view them not as people to emulate, but as records of how those who arrived changed their names. Around 1358, Seol-son of the Uyghurs came to Goryeo to escape the Red Turban Rebellion. The majority view is that the owner of the dumpling shop in the Goryeo song " Ssanghwajeom" is an Arab merchant. At the end of the Goryeo Dynasty, groups of Jurchens came in droves to become Goryeo people, and during the Imjin War , there were even Japanese soldiers who had come as enemies but surrendered. It is not a lineage because we did not mix . Many people arrived. But those people no longer exist as separate groups today. So where did they go? The first clue is in Gaegyeong a thousand years ago. Let’s start by looking at what King Wang Geon gave to that crown prince. Let’s go back to the story of the crown prince I mentioned at the beginning. The people of Balhae did not come all at once. According to the " History of Goryeo," in September 925, 500 people including the Balhae General Sin Deok crossed over first. In the same month, Seong-ho followed, and that winter, 1,000 households of commoners arrived. In just one year, three groups crossed the border one after another. And the "Annals of King Taejo" in the " History of Goryeo" records that in July 934, Crown Prince Dae Gwang-hyeon of Balhae arrived leading tens of thousands of people. Other records date this event to 925 or 926, so the years vary by record. If we translate the sentence from the "History of Goryeo" directly, it goes like this. "As he came and surrendered, leading tens of thousands of people, he was bestowed a surname, named Wang Gye, and entered into the royal family’s genealogy."" Naetu" means that they came voluntarily and entrusted their bodies to the state . The History of Goryeo further records that he was specially appointed as Wonbo to guard Baekju and uphold the ancestral rites. Records also show that his followers were given government posts, while his soldiers were provided with land and houses. There are also figures that allow us to estimate the scale. Professor Park Guk-geol, who wrote a study on naturalized people in the Goryeo period, estimated in a newspaper column that more than 100,000 Balhae refugees came to Goryeo. If we consider the Goryeo population at the time to be 2.1 million, that is about 5 %. According to this estimate, one in every 20 people was from Balhae. This situation can be interpreted as follows . The king did not treat the royals who had built a nation as mere guests. He placed the same nameplate on their front door as his own. Instead of creating a separate category for " Balhae people," he registered them under the name of the Goryeo royal family. At the same time, he allowed them to continue their ancestral rites. He did not uproot them, but brought them under the same roof while letting their roots remain. This approach did not end just once. The civil service examination system of Ssang-gi we saw earlier was a door created to select government officials from those who had entered the country. And through that door, the sons of naturalized people entered. Seol Jang-su, the son of the Uyghur Seol Son, passed the civil service examination at the age of 22 in 1362. A record left in the annals when someone passes away is called a "jolgi" ; according to his jolgi in the Annals of King Jeongjong, he traveled to the Ming dynasty court eight times. The father was a foreigner who fled a war, but the son traveled back and forth to the Ming dynasty representing this country. This son's name appears once more in the Joseon dynasty. The Jurchens were a bit different. There were separate districts grouped for them in Goryeo, too. There is research from Jeju National University stating that after Goryeo began building a fortress on the northern border during the reign of King Deokjong, they grouped the surrendered Jurchen villages into districts called " Gwisun-ju." However, a paper on the naturalization policy during the reign of King Munjong suggests that they encouraged assimilation by making naturalized Jurchens live mixed with Goryeo people. There is also a paper from Ewha Womans University stating that Jurchen residents who settled in the northern border areas were initially called Jurchens, but later blended into Goryeo people. These studies all show that even if there were separate districts, those who settled within the borders eventually melted into the Goryeo people. Goryeo granted them surnames. But the person who organized this category by law was the king of the next dynasty. Joseon created a separate register to record those who entered the country. And they placed an expiration date on that register. Joseon called the process of a foreigner becoming a citizen of this country "Hyanghwa." That person was referred to as a "Hyanghwa-in." However , there is one unexpected scene. Joseon also recorded Hyanghwa-in separately in the beginning. Naturalized Jurchens had their names entered into a register called the Hyanghwa-an starting in 1408 . The following year, in 1409, the number of naturalized Japanese living in Gyeongsang-do reached 2,000. The Annals of King Taejong recorded that they were divided and settled into various counties, and a separate census register called the Oean was created for them. Joseon, too, had such registers. A point worth noting is that these 2,000 people were not gathered in one place but were scattered across different counties. While China's ethnic autonomous regions grouped land by ethnicity, Joseon divided those of the same origin across multiple counties to live among neighbors. Joseon also covered the expenses for those who arrived. According to the civil statutes of the Gyeongguk Daejeon , naturalized people were exempt from land taxes for three years. The military statutes included a clause exempting newly arrived naturalized people from corvée labor for ten years . This meant relieving them of the duty to serve the nation for a decade. They were even provided with homes, but they were prohibited from selling them freely, allowing sales only from the next generation onwards. This is interpreted as an intention for them not to leave, but to put down roots. Depending on their background, merit, and talent, they were granted government posts, salaries, and slaves, and were even placed in the Gyeomsabok, the royal guards. Joseon went on to grant them surnames and ancestral origins (bongwan). The bongwan refers to the hometown where the founder of a clan first put down roots. Do you remember Seol Jang-su, who passed the state examination in Goryeo? In 1396, King Taejo accepted Seol Jang-su’s request and granted him Gaerim— modern-day Gyeongju—as his bongwan. His obituary recorded his ancestors as being from Hoegol, Gochang. Hoegol was the name used to refer to the Uyghurs. A descendant of the Uyghur people received the old capital of Silla as his ancestral origin. This family became today’s Gyeongju Seol clan, which numbered 2,898 people in the 2015 census. In the same survey, nearly nine out of 100 people sharing the same Chinese character for the surname Seol as Seol Jang-su were from the Gyeongju lineage. There were other naturalized people who received ancestral origins. According to a paper by researcher Im Seon-bin, four Han Chinese, two Uyghurs , and two naturalized Japanese received bongwan such as Haeju, Miryang, and Dongnae. While the descendants of the Han Chinese continue today, those of the naturalized Japanese have ceased. Not everyone remained. The story of the Jurchen-born Yi Ji-ran is even more dramatic. His original name was Duran, and he was from Cheongju-bu in the Dong-buk-myeon region. The History of Goryeo recorded that he defected in February along with a hundred households. He became a founding contributor to Joseon and was also named a Jeongsa and Jamyung meritorious official. He received the title of meritorious official three times. The Cheonghae Yi clan, which views him as its founder, had 13,337 members in the 2015 census. Researcher Won Chang-ae observes that Jurchen-origin families like the Cheonghae Yi and Jeonju Yi clans rose to become gentry families through the state examination system. In 1402, a man named Yi Ji, suffering from a serious illness, submitted a petition to the King. The Annals of King Taejong record his obituary, which translates his words as follows. "I am a man from my homeland; if I die in a foreign land, please burn my remains and bury them in my homeland so that I may follow the customs of my home." He also urged the King to cultivate virtue , concluding his letter with these words: "I hope you preserve Joseon forever." King Taejong was deeply saddened, suspending court sessions for three days and granting him the funeral he had requested. A founding contributor of Joseon identified himself until the very end as a man dying in a foreign land. That was the first generation. Then, what about the generation of his grandchildren? The answer lies in the Annals of King Sejong, dated May 29, 1439. The State Council, acting on a report from the Ministry of War, asked Sejong how many generations the descendants of naturalized subjects should be labeled as such and allowed to take military exams under that status. The proposal was to call the naturalized individual and their children "naturalized subjects," but to change the status for those following. "For grandchildren and beyond, as it has been a long time since their naturalization and they have lived here for generations, it is improper to call them naturalized subjects indefinitely; please treat them according to the customs of the native people." King Sejong followed this suggestion. Do you understand what this means? Joseon gave newcomers a name tag labeled "naturalized subject." However, that name tag was designed to be removed by the generation of the grandchildren. The validity period of that label was exactly two generations. From the grandchildren onward, they were considered people of this nation. 48 years later, in 1487, when the Ministry of Rites proposed including the grandchildren of naturalized foreigners and Jurchens in military service, King Seongjong replied: "Wait until the great-grandchildren's generation to impose military duty." The title changed in the generation of the grandchildren, and military obligations became the same as ordinary subjects only by the generation of the great-grandchildren. A legal path was established for people to gradually become citizens of this nation over several generations. In China, the category on their ID card remains for life. In Joseon, the category was created, but erased by the grandchildren's generation. Now the pieces of the puzzle fit together. Newcomers were given surnames and ancestral origins, they were not segregated into separate residential areas, and the label of "naturalized subject" was removed by the grandchildren's generation. Of course, the geography surrounded by seas and mountains, along with the history of a long-standing dynasty, must have played a role as well. Those factors existed, but what the records show is that these three elements functioned in tandem. As hundreds of years passed in this manner , there were no separate registries left. However, this rule was truly put to the test when major wars broke out. People arrived as enemy soldiers, and others arrived after losing their own countries. During the Imjin War, a Japanese general who came to attack Joseon surrendered. His name was Sayaga . His eldest son wrote in his 1675 biography that he was a vanguard commander at just 22 years old. A young general at the forefront had turned his sword around. Surrendered Japanese soldiers were called "Hang-wae," and there is a sentence in a report submitted to the court by Gwon Yul after the battle of Jeongjin in November 1597. "Commander Sayaga and Yeom-ji each took one enemy head." This is a report stating they each took one enemy head. Sayaga appears in the Annals as just one of many such surrendered soldiers. The court bestowed upon him the surname Gimhae Kim and the name Chung-seon. This was the beginning of the "Saseong Gimhae Kim" clan, also commonly known as the Urok Kim clan, meaning a surname bestowed by the King. According to a 2008 Maeil Shinmun report, about 250 households, including his descendants, were living in Urok-ri, Dalseong-gun, Daegu. There is also a tradition that he changed the village name, meaning a village where one lives with deer. Let's set aside one exaggeration here. His anthology contains a passage claiming he surrendered while leading 3,000 men. However, this anthology was published by his descendants in 1798 and is criticized for being exaggerated . A 1998 ethics textbook recorded the number as approximately 500. The records of surrender in the Annals total 42 cases involving 600 people. This is not just the number of Sayaga’s followers, but the total number of surrenders recorded in the Annals. This time, it is about people who came after losing their country. The Ming Dynasty collapsed in 1644. Jin Jiong, the grandson of General Chen Lin who led the Ming navy to Joseon, arrived at Gogeumdo in Wando after sailing from Guangdong. He married a local woman and moved to Haenam, but passed away 16 years after his exile. His 14-year-old son, Jin Seong-mun, wandered before settling in the current Hwangjo Village in Sani-myeon, Haenam, in his early 30s. According to a 2014 Kyunghyang Shinmun article, this village is a clan village for the Guangdong Jin family. In the 2015 census, the population of the Guangdong Jin clan was 2,397. Joseon created a category this time as well. Unlike during the reign of King Sejong, King Yeongjo divided the descendants of naturalized people into two groups. He called the descendants of Jurchens and Japanese "Hyanghwa-in," and the descendants of Han Chinese "Hwangjo-in. " For the Hwangjo-in, he exempted them from military service and the cloth tax , provided them with rice and dowries, and kept a separate registry called the "Hwain Noknok." Joseon did not forget the year the Ming Dynasty fell. In 1704 , Daebodan, an altar to perform rites for the Ming emperors, was built in the rear garden of Changdeokgung Palace. It was exactly 60 years after the fall of the Ming. In 1764, King Yeongjo held a special examination called the " Chungryang-gwa." It was the 120th year since the fall of the Ming, and even descendants of the Hwangjo-in were allowed to take this exam. Nevertheless , the result remained the same. In the 2015 census, their descendants were identified by surnames and ancestral hometowns such as the Gwangdong Jin, Zhejiang Shi, Haeju Seok, and Zhejiang Bian clans. They did not remain as separate groups maintaining their own language and customs, but were integrated among their neighbors through surnames and hometowns. For the same reasons, what happened to those who crossed the sea to other countries? In Vietnam, since the late 17th century , "Ming villages" formed where descendants of Ming Dynasty refugees lived together. They were grouped together as entire villages. According to a Hiroshima University paper, Ming descendants are registered today as Kinh people, the majority ethnic group in Vietnam. Those who remained separate were the later Chinese immigrants, known as the Hoa, numbering about 750,000 in the 2019 Vietnam census. Ships that departed as a nation collapsed entered Vietnam as villages, while they entered Joseon as individual households. In Vietnam, they were grouped into villages, while in Joseon, they remained as individual families with surnames and ancestral hometowns. One more deciding factor. In a speech at Seoul National University on July 4, 2014, Chinese President Xi Jinping’s words were recorded by the Chinese Foreign Ministry as follows. " Descendants of the Ming commander Chen Lin still live in Korea today." A head of state explicitly stated in an official speech that descendants of a Ming general live in Korea. The Gwangdong Jin clan of the Haenam Hwangsang village is that very family. There are records in the opposite direction as well. Li Chengliang, the father of the Ming general Li Rusong, who helped Joseon. Regarding his family , the official Ming history, the*Ming Shilu*, records that his ancestor Li Ying defected from Joseon and settled in Tieling. People crossed in both directions. However, there were also people the census could not fully contain. And on the other side, we began to be recorded in the census of others. There were exceptions as well. In the late Joseon period, small groups called "Jae-ga" lived in the former Six Garrison lands, such as Gyeongwon and Hoeryeong in the Tumen River basin of Hamgyeong Province. They were considered descendants of the Jurchen people. However, researcher Lee Dong-kyu’s 2021 paper argues that such a perspective solidified during the Japanese colonial period. According to the same paper, the state recognized their customs while incorporating them into the national tax and duty system. Researcher Kim Han-soo believes the Jae-ga villages lasted from the 17th century until the 1960s. In 1935, the* Dong-A Ilbo*serialized an article on the Jae-ga that cited an earlier survey . It consisted of 16 villages across five districts in North Hamgyeong Province. There were 3,323 people in 563 households. The author himself admitted these figures were neither a complete enumeration nor the latest survey. And the same article noted that they were gradually becoming assimilated. They were a different face that remained until the 1930s, which the census that spanned a thousand years could not fully capture. The second exception is people who became slaves. The Yangsucheok and Hwacheok of the Goryeo period were renamed Baekjeong during the reign of King Sejong in 1423, but they remained a low-caste class. While some theorize they were descendants of Jurchen captives or Guihua people, others argue they were itinerant Goryeo paupers, leaving scholars divided. Renaming them did not change the fact that they remained in a low caste. This caste was legally abolished with the Gabo Reform in 1894. In 1923, the Hyungpyeongsa society was established to eliminate the discrimination that still persisted . The third group are those left outside the caste: the overseas Chinese (Hwagyo). It began in 1882 during the Imo Incident, when some 40 Chinese merchants landed in Incheon. In 1930, there were 67,794 Chinese living in Joseon. The following year, it dropped to 36,778. Nearly half of them had left in just one year. The peak was in 1942, with 83,000. After the government was established, there was a period when we narrowed the gates. In 1961, land ownership by foreigners was restricted. It wasn't until 1968 that the door opened slightly, but with limits. Residential land was limited to 200 pyeong and shop lots to 50 pyeong. By 2002, the number of Hwagyo had shrunk to about 24,000. Then the doors widened once again. Restrictions on foreign land ownership were lifted in 1998, and permanent residency status was introduced in 2002. As permanent residents were granted local voting rights in 2005, the Hwagyo voted for the first time in the 2006 local elections. However, the tables have turned. We, who had worked to erase the separate boxes within our borders, found ourselves placed in another's box once we crossed the border. It is the exact opposite, like looking into a mirror. The labels we used to shed by the grandchild's generation back home are now what we wear abroad. The Joseonjok are one of China's fifty-six ethnic groups. According to the Chinese census, there were about 1.12 million Joseonjok in 1953. The peak was in 2000 , with about 1.92 million. At that time , they ranked 13th among the fifty-five ethnic minorities. By 2020, that number had decreased to about 1.7 million. Their ranking also dropped two spots to 15th. On the ID cards of our compatriots born in China, their ethnicity is listed as "Joseonjok." It is the very box seen on that card from the beginning. According to the Immigration Services Agency of Japan, there were about 267,000 special permanent residents at the end of 2025. Among them, about 240,000 were registered with South Korean nationality. In the former Soviet territory of Uzbekistan, an estimated 175,000 compatriots lived there as of the end of 2024, according to the Overseas Koreans Agency. And those people are returning. Of those, 77.3%were Chinese-Korean compatriots and 12.4%were Koryoin compatriots. It was 4%. The number of Koryoin compatriots was about 21,000 in 2013. As of December 2024, there were approximately 107,000. It increased fivefold over eleven years. According to Ministry of Justice statistics, as of the end of June 2026, there were 644,464 ethnic Koreans among Chinese nationals residing in Korea. People who had been recorded as statistics are returning to this land and appearing in our data again. Where are the returnees and those arriving for the first time being recorded now? The answer lies in our court documents today. There is a court procedure for naturalized foreigners to create a new surname and ancestral home (bon-gwan). This is called "Changseong-Changbon." According to National Court Administration data, 2,530 foreigners went through this process in 2005. That number reached 4,884 in 2009. It nearly doubled. The court records reported in 2010 show the names of newly created ancestral homes. Let me read a few for you. Mongolian Kim, Shandong Woo, Tsushima Yoon, Thai Tae, and Jilin Sa. The numbers continued to grow after that. According to Supreme Court data, 7,770 people underwent the process in 2011. It exceeded 6,000 annually until 2015. They are not being added to the " ethnicity" column, but to the " ancestral home" column. It is the very same column where King Wang Geon granted surnames and King Taejo of Joseon bestowed ancestral homes a thousand years ago. Becoming a new clan progenitor means writing one's name on the first page of a family registry that no one has opened yet. The number of surnames also demonstrates this. The 2000 Population and Housing Census counted surnames created by naturalized citizens separately. There were 442 of them. 145 were of Philippine origin, and 139 were of Japanese origin. In the 2015 survey, the total number of surnames increased to 5,582. Among them , 4,750 were surnames without Hanja, and the Dictionary of Korean Surnames identifies these as naturalized surnames. According to Ministry of Justice figures, 11,344 people became naturalized citizens in 2025. It is a figure that exceeded 10,000 for five consecutive years. What kind of work are the people who have arrived in this land doing now? According to an analysis by the Korea Employment Information Service, there were 1,109,000 resident foreign workers in May 2025. 44.9%of them worked in the mining and manufacturing industries. This means more than four out of ten were in factories or mines. The shipbuilding industry is even more pronounced. According to the Korea Offshore&Shipbuilding Association, the proportion of migrant workers in the domestic shipbuilding industry was 3.2% in 2007. By 2024, it reached 22.7%. This means that out of every 100 people building our ships, 23 were migrant workers. The number of people who actually entered and worked as seasonal agricultural and fishery workers was 543 in 2021. In 2025, there were 7,411. Compared to 2021, when foreign arrivals dropped significantly due to COVID-19, this is a more than 140-fold increase.

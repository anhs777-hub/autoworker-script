A building in Jeju that once housed a popular tourist nightclub closed its doors, became a shopping mall, and then an indoor zoo. Even then, it couldn't survive and eventually went up for public auction. In Gangnam, a site once famous for its hotel nightclub is now home to a steakhouse and a penthouse. You might wonder who even goes to nightclubs these days, but it’s not that people stopped liking to dance. In a dance hall in the middle of Seoul, over 500 people still come to dance every single day. The very things that made nightclubs the most popular back then are what eventually brought them down. Before we begin, please hit subscribe and like; it really helps us out. In 1994, a nightclub opened in the basement of the Ellui Hotel in Cheongdam-dong, Seoul. The name was Juliana. In front of Juliana, lines of people waiting to get in stretched for nearly 100 meters. There was a starting point for why hotel nightclubs were so crowded. On January 5, 1982, the midnight curfew was lifted. It had been in place for 36 years and four months. Before that, any time past midnight was a time when you had to be home. With the curfew lifted, people finally had time they were allowed to spend outside all night. One of the reasons for lifting the curfew was to promote tourism. People needed places to go during these new nighttime hours. The places that captured that night in the biggest, flashiest way were hotel basement nightclubs. They had large halls, stages, and dazzling lights. So, was it just alcohol that people were buying at these hotel nightclubs? Alcohol was available at local bars, too. People came to hotel nightclubs to buy a place to party all night and the feeling of entering a place not everyone could get into. The 100-meter line in front of Juliana created that very feeling. The longer the line, the more those who passed the entrance felt like chosen individuals. Even the experience of being filtered at the door was a product Juliana was selling. The operators themselves spoke about the kind of customers who came to Juliana. Children of people who made a fortune in real estate would come with bags of cash. Juliana was also a place where singers who hit number one on music shows would throw parties. Running a nightclub like this required a lot of staff. Around 2002, a large nightclub would have over 100 waiters working at a single location. With 100 waiters, a grand hall, a stage, and lighting, hotel nightclubs were businesses on a completely different scale from local bars. Because the business was so big, there was one condition for success. The hall had to be packed for 100 waiters to make a living and to cover the costs of the stage and lights. Hotel nightclubs were businesses that could only function when they were completely full. But even among nightclubs, those with the word "tourist" on the sign had different closing times. Why were there "tourist nightclubs" in the middle of the city, nowhere near actual tourist spots? You've probably wondered about that at least once. Around 2013, a night club in Yeongtong, Suwon, Gyeonggi Province, was operating in a commercial building—not even a hotel —bearing the name "Tourist Night Club ." The word "tourist" once clearly held a certain power. Before February 1990, tourist hotel night clubs and designated tourist entertainment venues were exempt from late-night crackdowns. Night clubs in hotels could stay open until 4 AM, and other tourist entertainment venues could operate until 2 AM. Even when other bars had to shut their doors, tourist venues kept their lights on. The justification was exactly as the name suggested. The Tourism Promotion Act defined these establishments as places equipped with dance facilities suitable for tourists to enjoy. The logic was that since they served tourists, they were permitted to stay open late. However, looking inside these venues, most of the customers were locals. Critics argued that they were abusing these exemptions, and public opinion grew that it was unfair compared to other businesses. At the time, the Ministry of Home Affairs replied as follows: Designating a place as a tourist venue is merely under the Tourism Promotion Act and does not mean it is exclusively for foreigners. In February 1990, Incheon and Gyeonggi Province took action first. They forced even tourist venues to close at midnight. Starting September 1st of that year, Seoul required tourist hotel night clubs in the city to close at midnight. After the "War on Crime" began in October 1990, all entertainment venues nationwide, including those in hotels, had to close at midnight. For the first time, the privileges associated with the word " tourist" were broken. Business owners suffered immediate financial losses. In 1992, the "key money" for entertainment venues plummeted from 200–300 million won to 30–40 million won. The extra money expected when transferring a business evaporated in an instant. There was a time when those privileges returned. In 1994, for "Visit Korea Year," the government allowed bars in tourist hotels to stay open until 2 AM again. The name "tourist" had once again bought them extra business hours. Then, on March 1, 1999, all late-night operating restrictions on entertainment venues were lifted entirely. This time, it wasn't just tourist venues that were freed. All entertainment venues were allowed to stay open all night. Three weeks after the lifting, "24-hour" signs appeared all over the entertainment districts of Gangnam. Once the privilege of being able to stay open later than others disappeared , competitors began staying open all night overnight. Since then, the closing times have been the same, whether or not the name "tourist" is attached. Even so, night clubs had one thing that other bars couldn't replicate. The waiters. In January 2002 , there was a waiter named "Park-sel" at the Riverside Hotel night club. His real name was Kim Hae-dong, and he was 35 years old with 12 years of experience. After losing his job when the nightclub he worked at closed during the IMF crisis, he returned after a year, ditching his old nickname "Kim Jae-bak" for "Pak Se-ri." He calculated that using the name of the golfer who gave the nation hope during the IMF crisis would win over customers . He was not the only waiter who made such a calculation. At the time, there were hundreds of waiters in Seoul going by the name "Pak Se-ri." Kim Hae-dong drove a truck equipped with speakers around Teheran-ro to promote the name " Pak Se-ri." There was a reason why waiters used the names of famous people . Since waiters lived off the sales from customers they brought in, they needed customers to ask for them by name rather than the club's name when they returned. To do that, the name had to be something memorable after just one hearing. The financial structure worked like this. In 2008, nightclub waiters in Daegu were not employees receiving a salary from the establishment. They were individuals who split the revenue from the customers they brought in with the business. The more customers they brought in, the larger the waiter's share became. That is why waiters would go out to the streets before sunset to hand out tickets for female customers. If a customer brought this ticket, they did not have to pay the base entry fee of 30,000 to 40,000 won. Business usually ended around 4 a.m. The reason waiters put so much effort into attracting female customers was for " booking." Booking is when a waiter takes a customer to another table or room to pair them up with strangers. In 2008, at one nightclub in Daegu, over 20 female guests visited a single room in less than two hours. There were even clubs and groups that traveled from Seoul and Busan to visit such nightclubs. Nightclubs did not invent the concept of booking. Back in the cabaret era, a similar practice existed under the name "joining." The boom was hottest from the mid-to-late 1990s through the mid-2000s. The real product of a nightclub was the anticipation that you might meet someone new tonight . It was the waiters, who split the profits, who created that expectation. The more waiters there were, the more customers came, and the more customers there were, the more meetings happened. This is why large nightclubs needed as many as 100 waiters. However, dancing and meeting people like this wasn't something you could do just anywhere in this country. This situation is most clearly seen inside tour buses. During the fall foliage season, karaoke machines run inside tour buses, and wild dancing breaks out in the narrow aisles. It is a scene you have likely seen at least once. In October 2018, a financial sector employee in Daejeon described a scene from a company retreat bus like this. They said once the enthusiastic members start standing up, there is no stopping them. They said they repeat standing up and sitting down to avoid the police. The police had their reasons, too. They said tour buses are high and have tinted windows and curtains, making it hard to see if people are dancing inside even if they pull them over. The reason dance parties spread to the inside of buses is also because there weren't many proper places to dance on the ground. In this country, the type of business permitted to have customers dance is an entertainment establishment . If a place allows both singing and dancing, it's classified as an entertainment establishment; if it blocks both, it’s a general restaurant. Since February 2016, if regular restaurants or bars have sound systems and let customers dance, they face a one-month business suspension for the first offense, two months for the second, and potentially a license revocation for the third. These regulations were aimed at so-called " vibe bars." The only places effectively authorized for customers to dance were entertainment venues like nightclubs. Buses, which are hard to police, became one of the few places outside of nightclubs where people could dance. Yet, the passengers who actually dance at these bus dance parties are not punished. The Road Traffic Act imposes a 100,000 won fine and 40 demerit points on drivers who continue to operate their vehicles while allowing passengers to dance and cause a disturbance inside. There were attempts to punish the passengers as well. In August 2001, the government considered a plan to issue fines to passengers too . In May 2014, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport went a step further. They proposed an amendment to ban karaoke machines and lighting in tour buses and mandate that bus companies prevent passenger drinking and dancing. This amendment was overturned in just two months. The karaoke industry pushed back, and on July 27, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport removed those clauses. That is why, even now, it is the driver, not the passengers, who gets punished for bus dance parties . This is the reason why drivers are saying that passengers should be punished as well. Even as this video is being uploaded, the crackdown is ongoing. The police included bus drinking and dancing as targets for their fall travel season crackdown from October 8 to November 30, 2026. The situation outside of buses is no different. In February 2026, after a video of people dancing to an impromptu performance at singer Lee Ha-neul's tripe restaurant in Eunpyeong-gu, Seoul , went viral, the district office launched a crackdown. In this country, dancing was only allowed in permitted venues, and that permission was held almost exclusively by nightclubs. That was the monopoly of nightclubs. However , that permission came with taxes. Entertainment establishments were a business category where additional taxes were levied on the price of alcohol. The nightclub business, which provided both waiters to facilitate meetings between customers and the permission to dance, survived even the IMF crisis. Many remember the IMF crisis as the reason nightclubs collapsed. But for Juliana, the revenue was the exact opposite. On November 21, 1997, the government officially announced it was requesting an IMF bailout. During that very IMF era, Cheongdam-dong's Juliana saw monthly revenues of 1.8 to 1.9 billion won. These are the figures revealed by Juliana's own operator. Even while the country was gripped by a foreign exchange crisis, the dance floor at Juliana was packed. Not all nightclubs were fortunate, however. Some nightclubs did close their doors during the IMF. But the waiters returned, wearing Se-ri Pak badges to cheer on the nation. The IMF wasn't what completely destroyed the nightclub business. The IMF wasn't the only thing that threatened nightclubs. In 1991, the first karaoke room opened in Gwangalli, Busan, and they soon appeared in every alley. In 2001, Cyworld mini-homepages were launched. Still, nightclubs held on. Karaoke rooms were places for people you already knew. Nightclubs were still the place to meet new people. Nightclub revenue started to decline in 2010. That decline is visible in the individual consumption tax. The individual consumption tax is a levy applied to liquor bills at adult entertainment venues like room salons and nightclubs. The individual consumption tax paid by these venues peaked at 146.2 billion won in 2010. Since then, it has decreased every year , dropping to 83.1 billion won in 2019, before COVID-19 hit. That's a drop of nearly half in just nine years. It means the money spent on drinks at entertainment venues has shrunk by that much. Famous nightclubs mostly closed their doors around this time as well. Juliana ceased operations in 2007. Incheon's Gukil-gwan closed in 2010, and the Riverside Hotel's Moon Light closed in 2014. So, what took the customers away? First, there are clubs. From the late 90s, genre-specific clubs began to appear, and by 2005, the club scene exploded. Clubs had music instead of "booking," and MDs instead of waiters. MDs are staff members who attract customers and arrange tables for them. By 2015, instead of "booking, " MDs at a Gangnam club were introducing guests to one another. Of the ten famous nightclubs in Gangnam during the early 2000s, five had closed and three had converted into clubs by 2015. Second, the method of "booking" itself. The practice of a waiter grabbing a customer by the wrist and taking them to a stranger's table was once the highlight of a night out. But by today’s standards, it feels more like a burden than entertainment. It is natural to assume that even those looking for encounters now prefer to choose who and when they meet for themselves. Third, the alcohol. People are simply drinking less alcohol overall. The proportion of men in their 20s who drink at risky levels is significantly lower in 2025 than it was in 2016. Waiters 'earnings have also decreased. In 2002, there were hundreds of waiters in Seoul who were as popular as Pak Se-ri. By 2015, a waiter’s monthly income was less than 1 million won, and some began working side jobs. During the IMF crisis, what kept nightclubs alive was the opportunity to meet people. But as the way people seek out meetings changed, that very aspect became the first thing to collapse for nightclubs. Business hours were already unrestricted for everyone, so staying open all night was no longer the nightclub's exclusive advantage. As customers left, all that remained were 100 waiters and buildings designed with massive halls. There was a "Mul Night" in the basement of the Riverside Hotel in Jamwon-dong, Seoul. Mul Night opened its doors in 1981, the same time as the hotel. Lee Ju-il and Cho Yong-pil performed on its stage, and it had over 50 private rooms. Mul Night closed down in 2014, after 33 years in business. A large steakhouse was built in its place . In the same hotel, the cabaret was turned into a Chinese restaurant, and the Turkish bath became a spa. The steakhouse in the Gangnam hotel nightclub spot I mentioned earlier is this very place. Incheon's Gukilgwan followed a similar path. Gukilgwan received its entertainment license in 1983 and was the only nightclub in Incheon for nearly 30 years. Gukilgwan closed in January 2010, the building was demolished, and a global clothing store opened there in 2014. The Ellui Hotel in Cheongdam-dong, which housed the club' Jule ', was sold in 2015 and replaced by penthouse apartments. This is not just happening in Seoul and Incheon. You might think nightclubs would survive in a tourist destination like Jeju, but all three of its famous large nightclubs have closed. The 2000 Night in Tap-dong closed its doors in 2007. They said they would tear it down and build a hotel, but construction hadn't started by late 2024, with only fences surrounding it. Aroma Night, famous for its retractable ceiling, closed in 2022, and a mixed-use residential complex began construction on the demolished site. The third is the building I mentioned at the beginning, the former Jeju Tourism Night in Yeondong, Jeju City. Jeju Tourism Night opened in 2002 and folded its business in 2009. After the nightclub left, a Las Vegas-style performance hall moved into the building. It became a shopping mall in 2013. In 2021, it became the largest indoor zoo in Jeju. Even then, it was put up for public auction at the request of a creditor. In December 2024 , the appraised value of this building was 37.3 billion won. Performance halls , shopping malls, and zoos—all three were businesses chosen to attract large crowds. And all three failed to fill these buildings. The reason for these three failures can be guessed by looking at the size of the buildings. Nightclub buildings were constructed for large halls, stages, and countless private rooms. They are sized to run only if they are packed with people every single night. Unless you have a business that draws a crowd every night like a nightclub, it’s hard to manage that much space. Yet, it’s not easy to reopen a nightclub in those buildings either. To open a place where guests dance, you first need a license for an entertainment bar. It’s a heavy license where individual consumption taxes are added to the cost of drinks. As nightclubs disappeared, they left behind massive buildings that couldn't be filled by anything else. Where did the people who used to dance there go? There is a dance hall in the basement of a building near Jegi-dong, Seoul. It’s the place I mentioned earlier, where over 500 people come to dance every day. This dance hall is a " Colatech." In May 2026, those visiting this Colatech were in their 60s to 80s. Admission is 2,000 won, and it’s free after 4:30 PM. At the Jegi-dong Colatech, there are "booking assistants ." Booking assistants are people who connect men and women who don't know each other. The job that the waiter Park Seri used to do at nightclubs remains in the basement of Colatechs under a different name. An 83-year-old patron who visited this place said this : In the end, everyone comes here to meet people. Colatechs weren't like this from the beginning. Colatechs started in the late 1990s as dance halls for teenagers where alcohol was not sold. In April 2000, we began to see a change in Colatechs in Jeongeup, Jeonbuk. Middle-aged crowds flocked to Colatechs, and teenagers were pushed out. The situation, as explained by the police, was this: Cabarets were entertainment bars with high taxes, and they couldn't open before 5 PM. Colatechs could operate without time restrictions just by registering as a business at the tax office. So, dance venues changed their signs to "Adult Colatech." Even back then, the admission fee was 2,000 won. If the word "tourism" once gave them the right to stay open late, this time the name " Colatech" lowered the barriers of taxes and operating hours. Customers left the heavily taxed entertainment bars for cheaper, more accessible Colatechs that didn't sell alcohol. But these Colatechs are also decreasing in number . The number of Colatechs nationwide dropped by over 80 from 2019 to 2022. The turnaround happened in Gangnam. Juli Ana, which closed its doors in 2007, reopened in 2025. The new Juli Ana is located in the basement near Hak-dong Station in Gangnam. The original operator, CEO Oh Deok-soo, and Kim Sung-soo of the group Cool are serving as co-representatives. We open at 7 PM on Saturdays. Customers flood in from 9 PM, and on weekends, there isn't a single empty seat. Every table is equipped with a red light to call a waiter. Seven out of ten customers are in their 40s or 50s. While new clubs aren't opening, the places that reopened their doors from back then are packed again. One club that reopened couldn't return to its original location at a Cheongdam-dong hotel. That site had already become a penthouse apartment. The same goes for the former tourist club building in Yeon-dong, Jeju City. What once filled that building, which was put up for public auction, wasn't just the dancing or the drinks. It was the anticipation of potentially meeting someone tonight. That anticipation hasn't disappeared. Only the large buildings that once held it have become empty. The desire to dance and meet others remains, but if the business model for the venue changes, even the biggest place cannot make a comeback. The desire to meet people will continue to shift toward cheaper, more accessible places. I will return with more hidden economic stories from our daily lives next time.

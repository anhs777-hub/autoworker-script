In January 2023, Baedal Minjok’s card payment volume in Seoul was 195.6 billion won. In the same month, Coupang Eats saw 63.5 billion won. There was a difference of more than three times. But in less than two years, this flipped. In December 2024, Coupang Eats surpassed Baemin in Seoul for the first time. When news like this breaks, the comment sections are quite interesting. Hardly anyone is worried; it’s mostly "serves them right.""Finally" comments keep pouring in. The top company, which exceeded 5 trillion won in revenue last year, is wobbling, and no one is siding with them. First, let's take an accurate look at the current landscape. According to mobile data analysis firm IGA, this February's delivery app market share was 54%for Baemin, 29%for Coupang Eats, and 10%for Yogiyo. Public delivery apps like Shinhan Bank's Ddaenggyo took about 7%. For reference, industry reports from 2023 show Baemin at 65%and Coupang Eats at around 11%. In three years, Baemin dropped over 10 percentage points, while Coupang Eats nearly tripled. Just looking at the numbers, you can see the scale tipping, right? But Baemin is still number one, right? That is correct. Nationwide, Baemin is still in first place. According to a YG Retail survey this July, Baemin had 23.2 million users, and Coupang Eats had 13.13 million. In terms of headcount, Baemin still has nearly double. But the nature of this " first place" is what matters. Mobile Index data shows that as of this July, users increased by 6.8%for Baemin and 20.9%for Coupang Eats compared to a year ago. While one is running, the other is walking. Furthermore, as of this April, the average monthly card spending per user, or ARPU, was about 120,000 won for Coupang Eats and 100,000 won for Baemin. Baemin has more users, but each user spends more on Coupang Eats. Since big spenders are gathering at Coupang Eats, this number might be more painful for Baemin. And in Seoul, the battle already seems over . According to data from eight card companies provided by Rep. Kim Nam-keun’s office, December 2024 payment volume in Seoul hit 179.2 billion won for Coupang Eats and 177.8 billion for Baemin—the first flip. Back in January 2023, the gap was three times, with Baemin at 195.6 billion won and Coupang Eats at 63.5 billion. By August 2025, the gap widened to over 50 billion won, with Coupang Eats at 211.3 billion won and Baemin at 160.5 billion . To be fair, there are limitations to this data. Baemin accepts many simple payment methods like Naver Pay or Toss Pay, so counting only card transactions might result in a lower figure than reality. Still, the trend is clear. It means the top spot has changed in Seoul , where trends emerge first. So, what on earth did Baemin do to make people cheer like this? To understand this, we need to go back in time a bit. Baemin used to be a well-loved brand. "What nation are we?"(The famous Baemin slogan) B-grade sensibility ads, fonts like Hanna-che, and an image as a startup with great employee benefits. With the success story of a homegrown unicorn, it was once a company university students dreamed of joining. Because of these memories, the current sense of betrayal is even greater. If it had been a hated company from the start, people wouldn't be saying " serves them right" to this extent. It's like how getting dumped by your first love hurts more. The first crack appeared in December 2019. The announcement came out that Baemin was being sold to Germany's Delivery Hero. Delivery Hero was the company that already owned the second-place Yogiyo and third-place Baedaltong at the time. The first, second, and third-place companies were all ending up under one roof. That’s when the internet meme " German Nation" was born. I laughed at first too, but it wasn't something to just laugh off. During the Fair Trade Commission's review, the combined market share of the two companies exceeded 99%based on transaction volume . At this level, it's not a market; it's effectively a single company. So, the FTC approved the acquisition on the condition that they sell Yogiyo, and the acquisition price was about 4.75 trillion won. The second incident erupted in April 2020. They introduced an "Open Service" that takes a 5.8% commission on every order instead of the 88,000 won monthly flat-rate ad fee . Baemin explained that over half of the business owners would actually pay less, but the timing was terrible. It was when COVID-19 had just broken out, and self-employed people were standing on the edge of a cliff. It was also a structure where the burden grew heavier for shops that were doing better business. Public opinion eventually exploded, and the CEO had to bow his head. Perhaps from this point on, the formula "Baemin is a company that raises prices as soon as it gains a monopoly" became ingrained in people's minds. And the final blow came in 2024. This is a rather unpleasant part. In July of that year, Baemin announced it was raising the brokerage fee for its own delivery service, Baemin 1 Plus, from 6.8%to 9.8%. A 3 percentage point increase might seem small, but in terms of ratio, the fee went up by 44%. There was an explanation that since Coupang Eats was already charging 9.8%, they were just matching it, but there's no way that would sound reasonable to the business owners. Pressed by public opinion, Baemin switched to a tiered commission system ranging from 2.0%to 7.8%based on sales volume, starting February 26, 2025. But just two months later, on April 14, they began charging a 6.8%commission on takeout orders as well. Customers go and pick up the food themselves, yet the app still takes a cut from that. From the shop owners ' perspective, they couldn't help but ask why they were being charged when the app wasn't even providing delivery. What truly angered people wasn't just the commission itself, but where that money was going. According to reports by CEO Score Daily, Woowa Brothers paid out 412.7 billion won in dividends out of 699.8 billion won in operating profit in 2023. In 2024, they used 537.2 billion won of their 640.8 billion won operating profit to buy back and retire their own company shares. Buying back shares increases the share of remaining stockholders, and the shareholder with a 99%stake is Delivery Hero. In the end, that's about 950 billion won over two years. It essentially means nearly 1 trillion won has been transferred to the German parent company. Shop owners naturally picture the commissions they paid turning into dividends for German shareholders. This is why "Germanic people" changed from a simple joke into a language of anger. Since the beginning of this year, complaints from consumers have also caught fire. On April 22 of this year, Baemin changed its "One Bowl" service—which allowed ordering a single item without a minimum order amount—into a paid service, charging regular users 500 won per order. They kept it free only for Baemin Club subscribers. This service was a star product that saw 27 million orders last year alone. They got people used to it for a year for free, and then started charging. It feels like a gacha game where they hand out free tickets at first, then start pushing for payment once they have a user base. That’s exactly what it is. Bosses. Whether it's shop owners or consumers, Baemin has become a public enemy. But this year, the story began to unfold like a soap opera. From the start of the year, reports poured in that Delivery Hero was planning to sell Baemin. The background was the parent company's financial situation. Reports emerged that debt maturing starting next year was around 9 trillion won, and they eventually appointed JP Morgan to oversee the sale. The valuation of Baemin mentioned in the market was 8 trillion won. They intend to buy it for 4.75 trillion won and sell it for 8 trillion won. It looks like they took dividends from shop owners' commissions and are now trying to make a profit margin by selling the company itself. Public opinion flared up once again, essentially saying, "serves them right. " The twist came this July. Uber, a US company rumored as a potential buyer, stepped forward to acquire not just Baemin, but the entire parent company, Delivery Hero. Valued the company at approximately $ 14.8 billion through a cash tender offer of € 41.5 per share . That's about 22 trillion won in our currency. In the process, the standalone sale of Baemin has been halted. And just yesterday, September 22nd, the Fair Trade Commission announced it has begun a preliminary review of the merger. The funny thing is that Uber is a company that was originally pushed out of the Korean delivery market by Baemin. They entered with Uber Eats in 2017 and withdrew in 2019. But now, seven years later, they are coming back by buying Baemin outright. If you can't beat them, buy them. In movie terms, it’s like a villain kicked out in the first film returning as the chairman in the third. For Baemin, it’s their second acquisition, starting as a Korean company, sold to Germany, and now moving to the U.S. Coupang, laughing on the other side, also starred in their own comedy this year. Late last year, there was a massive data breach at Coupang that affected about 33.7 million accounts. Hearings were held, and there were loud calls to delete the app. Actually, according to reports from Sisa Journal, Coupang Eats 'usage rates were low for a while. But when Coupang Eats stopped charging delivery fees even to non-Wow members starting May 21st, people flocked back. They were angry, but they couldn't resist free delivery. This is the foreshadowing for today's story. Keep this in mind. Hearing this far, the conclusion seems simple. Baemin, which abused its monopoly, is being punished, and Coupang Eats has delivered justice through free delivery. Good prevails and evil is punished. But is that really the case? I believe the real story starts here. Here is the first question. If Baemin has been charging so much and sending trillions of won to Germany, they should be raking in money these days, right? But if you look at the performance disclosed this April, it seems a bit strange. Woowa Brothers' 2025 revenue was 5.283 trillion won, an increase of 22%from a year ago. It's the first time they've surpassed 5 trillion won since launching the service in 2010. However, operating profit dropped by about 7%, from 640.8 billion won to 592.9 billion won. Compared to two years ago, revenue grew by 54.7%, but operating profit actually fell by over 15%. It's a strange situation where the more they sell, the less they keep. The culprit is easy to find. There is an item called " outsourcing service costs," which is simply the delivery fees paid to riders . This jumped 41%, from 2.2369 trillion won in 2024 to 3.1542 trillion won in 2025. It's a structure where about 60% of revenue goes out as rider costs. Total operating costs also rose by 27%, outpacing the 22%revenue growth rate. This is the first twist of the day. I thought all the money Baemin took from chicken prices went straight into their coffers, but looking closely, a significant portion was actually going to the riders. So, are you saying Baemin is an innocent victim? That's not it. 592.9 billion won is still a massive amount, and it’s true that a large part of those profits was sent to their parent company. What I’m trying to say is that Baemin isn’t necessarily "good." We need to look at the structure behind why Baemin raised commissions, added fees for takeout, and started charging for single-serving deliveries. That’s the only way to see where this fight is headed. Let’s start by looking at what kind of business a delivery app actually is. In economics, this is called a two-sided market. It sounds complicated, but the meaning is simple. On one side, you have consumers ordering food, and on the other, you have restaurants selling it. Restaurants flock to apps with many customers, and customers flock to apps with many restaurants. It’s easy to think of Karrot Market. Because everyone in the neighborhood uses Karrot, I list my items there, and since all the goods are on Karrot, that’s where buyers go, too. This is called the network effect. It’s the effect where a service becomes more valuable as more people use it. What’s scary about this effect is that it keeps the number one player on top. Baemin has held the throne for nearly a decade using this power. And once you reach the top, a certain temptation arises. You know the restaurant owners can't leave anyway. Since all the customers are here, the calculation is that they won't leave even if you raise the commission a little. That’s why Baemin’s history of raising commissions looks like a history of monopoly. Then, a second question arises. How on earth did Coupang Eats break that invincible network effect? It’s not like Coupang Eats 'food tasted better or they had more restaurants, right? The answer lies in the fact that Coupang Eats didn’t fight inside the delivery app ring. Coupang Eats' real weapon isn't Coupang Eats itself; it’s the Wow Membership. It’s a bundled product that gives you free Rocket Delivery, Coupang Play, and free Coupang Eats delivery for 7,890 won a month. In March 2024, Coupang launched unlimited free delivery for Wow members, and people who were already paying the fee for shopping thought, "Wait, delivery is free too?" and switched over. Over ten million Wow members instantly became potential customers for Coupang Eats. Let me give you an analogy. Suppose there are two convenience stores side by side. One just operates as a convenience store. The other is connected to a big-box store next door where an annual membership gets you free delivery of convenience store items. No matter how cheaply the stand-alone convenience store sells rice balls, it would be hard to win, right? Baemin is in exactly that spot. Coupang brought the customers it gathered through shopping directly into the delivery market, while Baemin had to survive on delivery alone. Baemin didn't just stand by, either. They copied the free delivery and created a subscription membership called Baemin Club, similar to Wow. They set the price as low as 3,990 won per month and even added bundles with TVING and YouTube Premium. According to the company, 50%of all orders on Baemin last year came from Baemin Club members . But the problem is that free delivery isn't truly free. Riders still have to ride their motorcycles, and someone has to pay that cost. If the consumer doesn't pay, the app pays, the restaurant owner pays, or it gets hidden in the food price. This is exactly why Baemin's rider costs jumped 41%in just one year. Here is the third question. Isn't delivery supposed to get cheaper as orders increase? Usually , businesses become more efficient as they scale up. But this is the saddest secret of the delivery industry. Delivery is a business where economies of scale are weaker than you might think. Economies of scale mean that the more you produce, the cheaper the cost per unit becomes. Think about Netflix. The cost of making one drama series is the same whether there are 1 million or 100 million viewers. As subscribers increase, the cost per person drops significantly. However, in delivery, if one order is added, a rider must make one more trip. If orders double, the rider costs also nearly double. While app development or server costs benefit from economies of scale, the biggest chunk of costs—rider fees—increases almost in proportion to the number of orders. The fact that 3 trillion won out of 5 trillion won in revenue goes to rider costs is exactly what that means. So, Baemin raising fees, adding packaging charges, and charging for single-serving orders can't be explained by greed alone. As costs exploded due to the free delivery war, they looked for ways to cover them and ended up splitting the bill between restaurant owners and consumers. Of course, since they sent nearly 1 trillion won to their parent company during that time, it's fair to criticize them by asking why they couldn't just use that money to endure it. To be honest, I find that part quite infuriating, too. But the structure is far more complex than just summing it up as "Baemin is greedy." Now, here is the second twist. You thought Coupang Eats delivered justice through free delivery, right? Coupang Eats 'free delivery isn't free; it's a "free" service where the bill arrives later. From May 21st to August 31st of this year, Coupang Eats did not charge delivery fees even to regular members who were not Wow members. Just before that, starting May 19th, they filled the gap from 3 AM to 6 AM to begin 24- hour delivery in Seoul, Gyeonggi, and the six major metropolitan cities, and by the end of June, they expanded this to Gangwon, Gyeongsang, Jeolla, and Jeju. After that, they partnered with GS25 to create a system that delivers convenience store lunch boxes even at dawn. It has become a world where you can order tteokbokki at 4 AM. The effect was formidable. According to Mobile Index, Coupang Eats' weekly active users in the first week of June reached 9.22 million, an all-time high. Baemin peaked at 17.45 million in the last week of May before falling to the 16.8 million level in early June. In July, the estimated payment volume for delivery apps hit an all-time high of 3.2787 trillion won, which is an 18% increase from a year ago. Among that, Coupang Eats 'estimated payment volume was about 1.18 trillion won, and along with 13.13 million users, both were all-time highs. According to CEO Score Daily's analysis, about 41%of the increase in payment volume over the year came from Coupang Eats alone. For reference, these figures are estimates based on sample surveys of card payments and smartphone usage patterns, so there may be differences from official company revenue. Think of them as reference material for observing trends. But what is the true nature of this promotion? The industry calls it large-scale experiential marketing. They let people taste free delivery for three months, and then from September, they make them choose between paying the delivery fee or signing up for a Wow membership. It is the same as a first-month gym event. The reason the first month is free is so they can collect money from the second month onward. Coupang Eats stated that it paid the full delivery fees during this period and that there was no additional burden on the business owners. However, five small business organizations, including the Korea Federation of Micro Enterprise, called this policy a poison that chokes local commerce, labeling the platform giant's indiscriminate marketing offensive as such. Customers who have become accustomed to free delivery ignore stores that charge delivery fees, and as a result, the stores are inevitably dragged into discount competition. In fact, it is said that after September, when the free delivery ended, posts saying that Coupang Eats orders had dropped sharply have been appearing in self-employed communities. Stores that prepared ingredients based on the increased customers during the event have actually found themselves in a difficult spot. The most decisive scene came from the Fair Trade Commission. On June 18th of this year, the FTC rejected the consent resolution applications filed by both Baemin and Coupang. A consent resolution is, in simple terms, a system like an agreement that says, "We will fix it ourselves, so please let it slide this time." It functions like an agreement. The allegation both companies face is the demand for most-favored-nation treatment. Most-favored-nation treatment means telling a store, "Don't sell for cheaper on other apps." They’re demanding that stores offer their best terms on their app. According to the KFTC, Coupang began excluding stores that didn’t comply in March 2023, and Baemin followed suit in May 2024. These days, being removed from free delivery listings is practically the same as telling them to go out of business. Baemin is accused of prioritizing stores that use their own delivery service, while Coupang faces additional charges for forcing the use of Coupang Eats through its shopping membership. Baemin offered 300 billion won in support over three years , and Coupang 60 billion won over four years, but the KFTC rejected them. The explanation provided by the KFTC hits the nail on the head regarding the essence of this fight. It states that a market where many businesses tried to compete has shifted into an oligopoly dominated by two firms due to their illegal activities. It means it’s not a case of one being good and one being bad; they both rigged the game in the same way. According to KFTC data, Coupang’s share of order volume jumped from the 10%range in 2023 to the 30%range in 2024, a rapid growth driven by this membership strategy. The estimated fines range from 239 billion to 510 billion won for Baemin, and Coupang could face hundreds of billions in fines when including the tying charges. Do you see it now? While we were cheering for Baemin’s downfall, what actually happened wasn't the end of a monopoly. The market once monopolized by one player has simply shifted to an oligopoly shared by two. With Baemin at 54%and Coupang Eats at 29%, the two together hold over 80%of the market. Yogiyo, once a solid second-place player, has shrunk to 3.55 million users as of July, with Ddaeng-gyo following close behind at 2.49 million. There are only two fighters left standing in the ring. This is the third twist of the day. We thought store owners and consumers would be liberated if Baemin fell, but it turns out it’s a structure where the people on the bottom pay the price regardless of who wins in the ring. So where does that cost eventually end up? This is where the number that upset me the most comes in. A survey by a consumer group found that 20 out of 29 franchise brands—about 69%—were using dual pricing. Dual pricing means selling the same menu item at a higher price on delivery apps than in the store. Are the store owners doing this because they are bad people? After paying brokerage fees, delivery shares, payment fees, advertising costs, and discount contributions, there's nothing left, so they build it into the menu prices. A report by Yonhap News last year also included complaints from owners that delivery fees and commissions combined can reach 40%of the food price. There is a lot of variation between stores, so please take this as a general reference. In any case, when you press down on one side of the "free delivery" balloon, the other side—the food prices— bulges out. You might be happy about saving 3,000 won on delivery, but that money is hidden inside the price of the chicken, leaking out of your card anyway. If it's such a problem, why not just set a legal cap on commissions? Actually, that topic has been raised in the National Assembly. It’s a proposed delivery app commission cap, which would limit the total of brokerage fees, delivery costs, payment processing fees, and advertising expenses to a certain percentage, like 15%of sales. However, this bill couldn't even be put up for a vote on the 16th of this month because the ruling and opposition parties failed to reach an agreement at the National Policy Committee's subcommittee. Why is it so difficult? It’s because of the structure we saw earlier. The biggest chunk of delivery costs is the delivery fee paid to the riders, after all. Even if you legally cap commissions, the cost of the riders' labor remains the same. That is why the platform industry argues that if costs are capped, the burden will just shift, leading to higher delivery fees for consumers or reduced discount benefits. Just because sugar prices drop by 10%doesn't mean the price of soda drops by 10%. Conversely, if you legally cap the price of soda, the company will just reduce the can size or cut discount promotions. Prices are like a balloon; if you press one side, the other side pops out. Of course, that doesn't mean we shouldn't do anything. The burden store owners feel is real, and in a market dominated by two companies, the platforms have too much power to set costs. Ultimately, the issue is about who decides who bears how much of the cost. We can guess how this game ends by looking at overseas examples. Specifically, China. In the Chinese delivery market, Meituan was originally the overwhelming leader, but last year, e-commerce giant JD.com jumped in, and with Alibaba joining the fray, an all-time subsidy war broke out. A shopping giant invading the delivery market with membership perks and capital—that looks like a familiar picture, doesn't it? According to a Korea Economic Daily column, Meituan’s net profit in the second quarter of last year plummeted by 97% compared to the previous year, and it was estimated to have posted a loss of about 20 billion yuan in the third quarter. JD's net profit also dropped by more than half. In the end, Chinese authorities summoned these companies multiple times to warn them to stop the excessive competition. After the time spent cheering for free delivery passed , it turned into a tragedy of "free" where the companies, stores, and delivery riders were all left unhappy. Korea hasn't reached that point exactly , but the direction is similar. But why won't this fight stop? It’s because of the reward structure of the game. The delivery app war is a chicken game where the one who survives until the end takes it all. In a market with strong network effects, the moment the second-place player overtakes the first , users can all switch over at once. So , they figure that even if they burn cash now to secure market share, they can recoup it later. Coupang has the financial stamina to sustain delivery losses thanks to money from shopping and Wow membership fees. Baemin is driven by the fear that if they fall behind here, they will end up like Yogiyo. It’s a game of chicken where whoever hits the brakes first loses, so neither can stop. Now that free delivery is over, the war is shifting into a battle for loyal customers. Baemin says it will revamp its app next month to add a points system that returns a portion of payments. Coupang Eats is testing a stamp reward program in parts of Seoul and Gyeonggi, and a service in areas like Gwangju that offers up to a 5,000 won coupon if deliveries are late. They are also testing Coupang Now, a quick commerce service similar to Baemin B Mart. The focus has shifted from fighting to attract new customers to fighting to keep existing ones from leaving. There is one thing we need to address here. When we say "serves them right" regarding Baemin, that sentiment is justified. The criticism Baemin has faced was genuine, and it is a fact that the money flowed out to its parent company. A monopoly paying the price for ignoring customers is also a sign that the market is working properly. If there were no competitors, Baemin would probably still be raising its fees. Because Coupang Eats appeared, Baemin introduced tiered fees, set Baemin Club to 3,990 won, and created new services like single-serving delivery. It is a clear fact that competition has benefited the consumers. However, I want to add one thing to that applause. The one we should be applauding is not the company Coupang Eats, but the state of competition itself. If Coupang Eats takes Baemin’s spot and becomes the new monopoly, we might be watching a video five years from now saying " serves Coupang Eats right." I mentioned the Coupang personal data leak earlier as foreshadowing. Even though the data of 33.7 million people was compromised, people came back with just one round of free delivery. This is the power of lock-in, or keeping users tied down. Once you are tied into a membership ecosystem, it is difficult to leave even if you are angry. The very weapon that defeated Baemin could eventually become the chain that holds us captive. Then, what should we watch for in this landscape moving forward? There are three main things. The first is Uber. Once the acquisition is complete, Baemin will gain a powerful new parent. According to capital market news, Uber’s free cash flow—the cash remaining after all expenses—is about 10 billion dollars annually. If this money is poured into discounts and promotions in the Korean market, the chicken game will move to the second round. Besides, Uber also handles taxi hailing, doesn't it? The KFTC has also identified this merger as a conglomerate merger combining taxi hailing and food delivery, and announced it will examine whether integrated memberships or data consolidation increase market dominance . If Coupang combined shopping with delivery, Uber can combine mobility with delivery. Industry experts believe the future battleground will shift from a financial war to a technological one focused on dispatch and recommendation algorithms. Since rider costs account for 60%of revenue, even a small increase in dispatch efficiency can significantly reduce costs. The second factor is KFTC sanctions. The KFTC plans to hold a plenary meeting within this year to determine the level of sanctions for both companies. While the fines are significant, if a corrective order is issued against the very method of tying membership free delivery to lowest-price policies, both companies will have to overhaul their strategies. However, if the companies fight back with administrative litigation, it could take years to reach a conclusion, so the market dynamics won't flip overnight. The third factor is the government. Although the price cap bill has stalled, the government has set a budget of 124 billion won for public mutual-growth delivery apps for next year. It's an approach to add another player to the ring rather than suppressing prices through legislation. It is actually gaining traction. The number of users has more than quadrupled from 530,000 in 2024 to 2.27 million this year. However, since many public delivery apps in the past only flickered briefly when handing out coupons before fading away, we must wait and see if it will be different this time. Let's summarize. People saying "serves Baemin right" is not baseless hatred. The bill for ten years of accumulated fee hikes, packaging fees, paid single-item delivery, and nearly a trillion won in overseas dividends has come due all at once. In that sense, Baemin is truly paying the price for its monopoly. Yet, Coupang Eats, which is currently reaping the benefits, is fighting in the same ring using the same methods. Both companies are under the KFTC's scrutiny, and both are in a position where they must eventually pass the bill for free delivery on to someone else. While digging into this story, there is one thing I felt most strongly. The standard for determining the leader in the delivery app market wasn't about who is kinder, but who can burn cash longer. It’s a game won not by the " good" company, but by the one that can endure. Therefore, what we should be watching is not who is in first place. It's whether the second and third players can survive even after a leader is decided, so they can keep the leader on their toes. If the reason Baemin’s fall was satisfying was due to competition, the moment that competition disappears, the satisfaction will vanish as well. The next time you open a delivery app, just think about this once. Who is paying for my "zero-won" delivery fee today? And when will that person hand me the bill? If you take just this one question with you, this war will appear much clearer to you. Thank you for watching.

At the opening ceremony of the world tournament held in Quzhou, Zhejiang, China, a large banner hung stating that the world looks to China for Go, and China looks to Quzhou. However, when the tournament concluded six months later, the leaderboard showed that all top four spots were occupied by Korean players. There was not a single Chinese player among them. China has a population of 1.4 billion, and it is said that over 60 million people play Go. In Korea, even by generous estimates, it is around 6 to 7 million. That is a ten-fold difference. China has over 15 million players who have achieved rank, and about 3 million children start learning Go every year. That means half of our entire Go population enters the scene as beginners every year in China. The state nurtures players, and local governments host tournaments with prize money nearing 400 million won. Looking at the numbers alone, there is no way for us to win. Yet, whenever a major match takes place, the championship trophy strangely ends up in Korean hands. It was like that with Cho Hun-hyun, Lee Chang-ho, and Lee Sedol, and now it is the same with Shin Jin-seo. Is this just luck? Or is there a secret to Korean Go that China will never understand? Today, we will dig into the reasons one by one. I have also prepared a touching story that occurred between a Chinese Go legend and our own at the end, so please stay with us. First, let’s look at a match that took place in Quzhou this fall. Most of the answers to today’s story are contained in this single game. On Saturday, October 10th, in the quarterfinals of the Lanke Cup World Go Open, sitting across from 9-dan Shin Jin-seo was 22-year-old Chinese 9-dan Xie Ke. He is a young, spirited player who climbed to this stage after defeating our player, Shim Jae-ik, in the Round of 32. On top of that, it was a home game for him. The game proceeded strangely from the very beginning. In the mid-game scuffle, Shin Jin-seo was counterattacked, and the momentum shifted decisively. According to the Korea Baduk Association, Shin Jin-seo’s AI-calculated win probability dropped to 1%at one point. The Chinese media wrote that Xie Ke’s win probability had exceeded 98%. That means he would win only one or two games out of a hundred. Xie Ke even had plenty of time left, so he placed his stones rapidly, cornering Shin Jin-seo. Korean fans watching the broadcast probably thought this: "Oh no, he’s lost it today." I’m sure quite a few people even changed the channel. But Shin Jin-seo did not resign. He kept his head bowed low, staring intensely at the board. There’s a joke among his friends that Shin Jin-seo goes deaf when he’s looking at a Go board. They say he doesn't hear you even if you call his name loudly from beside him, and only turns around when you tap him on the shoulder. Numbers like 98%or 1% did not even enter his mind. And then, at move 129, something happens. Xie Ke neglects a critical spot and plays a loose move. It was a move that even Chinese newspapers described as a fatal blunder. Shin Jin-seo did not miss that gap. He struck the so-called "blue spots," the moves rated best by AI, one after another. Errors poured out from Xie Ke, and in the final closing stage, the game was completely overturned. A victory by resignation for Shin Jin-seo at 256 moves. Xie Ke had to resign before even reaching the point-counting stage. The other games on the same day were even more surprising. Byeon Sang-il 9-dan defeated last year's Samsung Cup winner, Liao Yuanhe, in just 116 moves. He was an opponent whom he had played three times before and lost all three. Shin Min-jun 9-dan beat last year's Lanke Cup winner, Dang Yifei, despite a previous record of 1 win and 8 losses. The remaining match was between two Koreans, where Lee Ji-hyun 9-dan beat Park Jung-hwan 9-dan . With this, the final four were entirely filled by Korean players. Before the finals even began, it was confirmed that Korea would take 1st to 4th place. In the history of world tournaments hosted by China, this is a first. Shall we look at the starting numbers again? Out of 48 players in the main tournament, 31 were Chinese and 9 were Korean. It started at 31 to 9, but not a single Chinese name remained on the leaderboard from 1st to 4th place. In soccer terms, it's like we won with three players against their eleven. The final was a showdown between Korean players Shin Jin-seo and Byeon Sang-il. Shin Jin-seo won the first game on the 13th and the second on the 15th consecutively. The grand prize of 1.8 million yuan, nearly 400 million won in our currency, came to Korea. With this, Shin Jin-seo achieved his tenth major world tournament victory. He surpassed Cho Hun-hyun 9-dan’s nine wins, leaving only Lee Chang-ho’s 17 and Lee Sedol’s 14 ahead of him. Since China’s top winners Gu Li and Ke Jie have eight wins each, Shin Jin-seo has already exceeded China’s best record. He is also the only player in Go history to reach the finals all four times since the Lanke Cup was established. The Chinese media did not hide it either. Yangcheng Evening News headlined that Korean players had swept the semi-finals on their home turf for the first time. On the Chinese internet , there were comments like, "We hosted a party for others in our own living room.""We just ended up footing the prize money." It is said that such complaints were circulating. Now, here is the real question. With more people, more money, and state support, why does this keep happening? The first reason is a bit unexpected. Korean Go became stronger because the circumstances were difficult. In the heart of Beijing, there is a six-story marble building called the Chinese Weiqi Association. It has a game hall twice the size of our Korea Baduk Association, plus national team training rooms, dormitories, and even a cafeteria. The players live, eat, and breathe Go there . It is literally a training village for Go players. What about us? According to the national team coach, the Korea Baduk Association has no space for players to eat or sleep. So, many young players spend over three hours a day traveling just to get to the academy. That’s a bit upsetting, isn't it? But there is a twist. The child who did so well in those conditions is now the world number one. Shin Jin-seo was born in Busan. His parents ran a small Go academy in Sasang-gu, Busan. For the sake of their son, they closed their life in Busan in 2012, moved to Seoul, and moved five times to different rental homes. It even led to the saying "Shin-parents ' five moves," a play on the story of Mencius's mother moving three times for her son's education. His father was no ordinary man. On days when young Jin-seo couldn't focus on Go, he would send his son out at 2 a.m., telling him to go to the academy and study. The child would walk alone through the dark night, turn on the lights at the academy, play Go, fall asleep on the desk, and go straight to school in the morning. Try to picture that scene. A single fluorescent light on the second floor of a dark commercial building. An elementary school student sitting alone in front of a Go board. While the Chinese athletes slept in dormitories, our child grew up like that. The day he failed the professional qualifying tournament at age 11, he only ate half the chicken he usually ate every day. He felt he didn't deserve to eat the whole thing. Even now, they say on days he loses, he stays up until 5 a.m. reviewing his games. Shin Jin-seo once said this himself. "I didn't take an elite course; I grew up by constantly being stepped on." The resilience in holding on at the edge of a cliff is different for someone who grew up with everything provided versus someone who persevered through gritted teeth. That stubbornness of not resigning even with a 1%win probability is not something they can teach at an athlete's village. The second reason is the legendary lineage that runs like blood. Let's go back to 1989. The first world tournament, known as the "Go Olympics," created by Taiwanese tycoon Ing Chang-ki, takes place. The prize money for winning was $ 400,000, which is over 500 million won today. At the time , Japan dominated the world of Go, and China had immense pride as the country where Go originated. Korea was just on the periphery. We were so much on the periphery that out of 16 invitees, there was only one Korean player. Cho Hun-hyun. That one person defeated the masters from Japan and China one by one and reached the finals. His opponent was Nie Weiping, a national hero in China called the "Iron Gate" who had won 11 consecutive games against Japan. In a best-of-five series, Cho Hun-hyun won the first game but lost the second and third. With one more loss meaning defeat, he won the fourth game and finished the final fifth game with a resignation victory after 145 moves. September 5, 1989, was the day Korean Baduk first conquered the world. The next day, Cho Hun-hyun held a car parade from Gimpo Airport to the Korea Baduk Association in Jongno. Those who remember that era will recall scenes in every local Baduk parlor of people laying out the game records and playing through them move by move. But the truly terrifying part comes after that. Living in Cho Hun-hyun's house was a young, quiet apprentice with no expression. That was Lee Chang-ho. Cho Hun-hyun himself had moved into his Japanese master's home at age nine and lived there for nine years to learn, and he passed that same tradition on to his apprentice in his own home. And that apprentice eventually took every single one of his master's titles. Lee Chang-ho became a world champion at 16, and for the next decade or so, he was pure terror to Chinese Baduk. Chang Hao was the hope China raised with its pride as the game's origin, but he suffered 12 consecutive losses to Lee Chang-ho and went 4 wins and 20 losses over seven years. Chang Hao suffered six defeats against Korean players in world tournament finals. Three times to Lee Chang-ho, twice to Cho Hun-hyun, and once to Lee Sedol. There is a funny yet bittersweet anecdote. During a banquet with the South Korean president , President Xi Jinping took the president over to Chang Hao and introduced him like this. This is the man who defeated Korea’s "Stone Buddha.""Stone Buddha" is Lee Chang-ho's nickname. Since he almost always lost, the fact that their top leader boasted about the few times he won at a diplomatic event shows that beating Lee Chang-ho was the wish of all of China. In 2015, long past his prime and having dropped to 34th in the domestic rankings, Lee Chang-ho appeared at the draw ceremony for the Samsung Fire Cup in Beijing. Then, China’s Tang Weixing, who became his opponent in the round of 16, chased after Lee Chang-ho as he headed to the elevator and said this. I grew up studying your game records as a child. It is an honor to play against my hero. Then, he handed over his phone and asked for a photo. Next was Lee Sedol. In 2014, a ten-game match was held between Lee Sedol and his peer, Chinese genius Gu Li, to determine who was the true best. Lee Sedol finished it in just eight games by securing six wins. The final game was a fierce battle that went up to 344 moves. Years later, when Lee Sedol retired, Gu Li said this. " Lee Sedol was a lamp that illuminated my path forward." It is said that Shin Jin-seo still studies the old game records of Cho Hun-hyun, Lee Chang-ho, and Lee Sedol. A lineage where a master raises a student, the student surpasses the master, and the child who watched that grows up to surpass the world. This chain has never been broken for nearly 40 years. China may have many people, but they don't have this kind of heritage. The third reason is the most interesting. It’s the heart that endures in big matches. There were three Chinese players who suffered comeback losses in the Round of 16 of this tournament. Ding Hao, Jiang Haojie , and Chen Yiming. In all three games, the AI win probability for the Chinese side exceeded 90%until the middle-to-late stages. The next day, Jin Yucheng let a 98%win probability game slip away. Is it just a coincidence that they are losing games they were leading one after another? There is a long-standing talk in the Go community. It is that Chinese players play with the weight of their country on their shoulders. It is said that the phrase "Serve the country with Go" is posted on the walls of the training rooms at the Chinese Weiqi Association. That might be a source of strength at times. But imagine having to close out a game you’ve already won in a tournament held on home soil, with banners claiming that "World Go looks to China.""What if I lose this game that's already won?" The moment that thought strikes, the hand becomes heavy and the moves become lax. Move 129 might have been exactly that kind of move. Shin Jin-seo is the exact opposite. He said this in his own words . "I somehow become more fierce when competing against Chinese players." And he also said this. "The skill level of professional players is almost identical.""The person who is even slightly more desperate to overcome their opponent wins." Lee Chang-ho was called the "Stone Buddha" because he showed no emotion even when the game was at its worst, and his opponents would crumble while looking at that face. Luo Huenian turned around two games from the brink of defeat. The common trait of Korean competitors is that their eyes light up precisely when a game seems lost. The fourth reason is the tradition of the last person standing, who turns the entire match around alone. There is the Nongshim Cup , a relay tournament where Korea, China , and Japan each field five players, and the winner keeps playing. In this tournament, Lee Chang-ho was the Grim Reaper. He achieved 14 consecutive wins on his own from 2000 to 2005. Every year, China led in terms of remaining players, but they only finished as runners-up because they couldn't get past Lee Chang-ho at the end. Shin Jin-seo has now taken over that spot. In the 2024 Nongshim Cup, all four other Korean players were eliminated without winning a single game, but the lone remaining Shin Jin-seo defeated all five Chinese players in a row. It was the first time in the 25-year history of the tournament, which is how he earned the nickname "China All-Killer." He won the final game this past February as well, giving Korea its 6th consecutive Nongshim Cup win and extending his personal streak to 21 consecutive wins. China always pushes with numbers. But even if 31 people show up, only one person sits across from another in the game room. If you cannot overcome that one person at the very top, there is no championship. The fifth reason is that Korea adapted the fastest to the AI era. When Lee Sedol lost 1–4 to AlphaGo in 2016, many people said that Go was finished. But the generation that grew up watching that scene produced the "Shin-gong Intelligence." It’s a nickname that combines Shin Jin-seo and Artificial Intelligence. In July of this year, he played an official match against KataGo , currently the strongest Go AI. It was a handicap game where two stones were placed first, but AI these days is so strong that even with two handicap stones, it’s hard for a human to win. He lost the first game because KataGo played an unprecedented move from the second turn, making him feel like a month of preparation was wasted in a single move. However, in the second game, he broke the rule that you shouldn't fight against AI and cut off the opponent's group. After being chased for nearly five hours, he won by four and a half points, and by taking the third game as well, he achieved a 2-1 comeback victory. Shin Jin-seo says that you cannot win by just memorizing AI moves; the one who digests them and plays their own game wins. Chinese players also study with AI. But the person who has digested it best is here in Korea right now. Hearing this, you probably think Korea is the best, right ? I think so too. But there is something I must tell you honestly. That’s the only way to truly see how incredible this victory is. Chinese Go is truly strong. Until last year, the Samsung Cup had Chinese players facing each other in the finals for three consecutive years, and the total number of wins is tied at 14 for both Korea and China. In this June's LG Cup held in Jeonju, eight out of nine Korean players were eliminated in the first round, leading to talk of a "major disaster," and China's Wang Xinghao took the championship. Shin Jin-seo himself had also been eliminated early in several major tournaments since losing in the final of the Lanke Cup a year ago. Shin Jin-seo himself said this just this summer. "I'm anxious because only four or five Korean players are barely holding on." Now, watch this tournament again with these words in mind. That’s why it’s even more remarkable. Byun Sang-il consecutively defeated China's No. 1 ranked Shi Yue and No. 2 Ding Hao, and even overcame Liao Yuanhe. Shin Min-jun beat an opponent he had only won against once in nine matches, and Lee Ji-hyun surpassed the domestic No. 2, Park Junghwan. In another world tournament two months ago, Shin Jin-seo was the only Korean to reach the quarterfinals, but this time, five players stood in the quarterfinals. The other players triumphantly overturned the claim that Shin Jin-seo was holding the fort alone. And because the opponents were this strong, the result of sweeping 1st through 4th place is even more valuable. I would like to ask a favor here. On January 14th of this year, Chinese Go hero Nie Weiping passed away at the age of seventy-three . The iron gatekeeper who lost to Cho Hun-hyun in the 1989 Ing Cup finals. At the funeral held in Beijing days later, an old man who flew in from Korea stood among the titans of Chinese Go. It was Nie Weiping. It is said that Nie Weiping told the Chinese reporters this . I have lost a friend who was with me for 50 years. Let us meet again in the next life and play a game of Go. They are the two men who played five games 37 years ago, where one became a hero and the other left the stage. The winner traveled all the way to the loser's country to bid him a final farewell. Chang Hao, who lost so many times to Lee Chang-ho, is now the chairman of the Chinese Weiqi Association and is said to be friends with Lee Chang-ho. Perhaps this might be the final reason. Korean Go did not just learn how to win, but also how to treat the opponent after winning. That is probably why even after losing to Korean players, Chinese professionals call them heroes and even chase them to the elevator to ask for a photo. There is an ancient legend about Lanke Mountain in Quzhou, where the tournament was held. It is said that a woodcutter watched young boys playing Go on the mountain, and when he came to his senses, the axe handle beside him had rotted away. It means that half a day in the mountains was hundreds of years in the real world. China built a global Go park on that legendary mountain, offered huge prize money, and hung up a banner saying that the world of Go looks to China. However, this autumn, the people who took the top four spots on the leaderboard on that mountain were all Korean. With a population of 1.4 billion and 60 million Go players. Still, this tournament showed that we are the ones who hold out until the very end in the big games. Of course, we never know about the next game. China will challenge us again, and Go is always flipped by a single move. Still, as long as we have this lineage connecting Cho Hun-hyun to Lee Chang-ho, Lee Sedol to Shin Jin-seo, and on to Byun Sang-il , Shin Min-jun, and Lee Ji-hyun, we won't collapse easily.

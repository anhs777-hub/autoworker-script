If you are currently watching the Asian Games in Nagoya, Japan, you are witnessing a very bizarre scene. It is an international event held in Japan, a global economic powerhouse, yet national athletes are trapped in prefabricated shipping containers. They receive three pieces of fried chicken and then run to a convenience store to buy bananas. How did Nagoya, which promised to spend 900 billion won to save money, end up wasting 3.3 trillion won and suffering international disgrace? We track the path of that squandered money. When Nagoya first set out to host the Asian Games, the atmosphere across the Japanese archipelago was quite different. Aichi Prefecture, where Nagoya is located, is called the heart of Japan's manufacturing industry. It is a land of self-made success, home to the headquarters of Toyota, the world's top automaker, and thousands of interconnected automotive parts suppliers. That is why the people of this region possess a very unique and strong sense of pride. It is the pride of feeding the Japanese economy with only regional technology and capital, without relying on Tokyo or the commercial city of Osaka. There was one more decisive factor that fueled this ambition. It was the Expo held in 2005. At the time, there were many concerns in Japan that the expo would run a deficit, but Aichi Prefecture achieved a successful conclusion by promoting eco-friendliness and turning a profit. The memory of successfully hosting a large-scale international event with regional power, without help from the central government, was deeply ingrained in the minds of Nagoya’s officials. Then, in 2016, Nagoya shocked the world by announcing a pledge while entering the bid for the 20th Asian Games. We will create a games that does not waste money. The budget proposed by the Nagoya Organizing Committee was only 100 billion yen, roughly 900 billion Korean won. The idea of finishing an international sports event, which usually costs trillions of won, with 100 billion yen was radical in itself. The secret was simple. They would not build new main stadiums or swimming pools that cost a fortune, but instead renovate old gymnasiums scattered throughout the city. They even declared they would not build a single athlete's village apartment, which is the symbol of the games and typically the most expensive part. It was a rosy blueprint that Nagoya, Japan, would show the world a new standard for a sustainable Asian Games: an eco-friendly, smart event that recycles existing facilities and reduces carbon emissions. Japanese public opinion, exhausted by the astronomical deficit of the Tokyo Olympics, initially applauded this move . On the surface, it appeared to be the most exemplary administration, saving taxpayer money. However, this 100 billion yen figure was not a realistic construction cost estimate, but merely a wish list put together by politicians to dodge responsibility. Essential budget items, like the safety renovation costs for aging stadiums and transport systems for the 45 participating nations, weren't even properly included in the initial estimate. However, a fatal miscalculation was hidden within this. A regional expo and an Olympic-level international sporting event are on completely different scales. An expo is a structure where visitors come to see exhibits and leave on their own, but sporting events are different. It involves over 15,000 national athletes and coaching staff from 45 Asian countries. It is a massive, living organism where press from all over the world must breathe, eat, sleep, and compete for two weeks. With shuttle bus networks that must be accurate to the second, strict doping controls, tailored meal plans, and high-tech broadcasting systems, it is like a precision machine where if a single gear slips, everything stops. It was like a local mechanic, confident in his ability to change bike tires, trying to take apart the engine of an F1 racing car traveling at 300km/h. Nagoya, intoxicated by its pride as the "City of Toyota" and 20-year-old memories of the EXPO's success, completely underestimated the complexity of an international sporting event. Yet, the reason behind Nagoya's obsession with such extreme, zero-budget planning wasn't just local arrogance. Behind it lay the dark shadow of a massive historical failure that had once plunged the entire Japanese archipelago into fear and anger. There is one name that can never be left out when discussing large-scale international events in Japan. It is Dentsu, known as Japan's largest advertising agency and a media giant. Behind almost every major sporting event held in Japan— the Olympics, the World Cup, the EXPO —Dentsu was always there. They were the core engine that secured massive corporate sponsors, sold overseas broadcast rights, and laid the complex groundwork for stadium facilities, transport, and translation staff. It was an unwritten rule among Japanese bureaucrats that although it costs a fortune, if you leave it to Dentsu, at least the event would run smoothly. But then, an incident occurred that shattered this ironclad formula. It was the 2020 Tokyo Olympics, held in 2021. The Tokyo Olympics remains a nightmare for the Japanese people. When they bid for it, they boasted that 730 billion yen would be enough, but the final bill reached 1.4 trillion yen, and including related infrastructure, it approached a staggering 3 trillion yen. To make matters worse, the COVID-19 pandemic hit, leading to spectator-less games; the expected tourism revenue vanished, leaving only trillions of yen in debt to be paid by taxpayer money. The real hell began after the event had ended. The Tokyo District Public Prosecutors Office raided the Tokyo Olympics Organizing Committee, opening a Pandora's box of unimaginable corruption. Haruyuki Takahashi, a former Dentsu executive and key figure in the committee, was arrested for taking hundreds of millions of yen in bribes, exposing a massive collusion scheme where agencies like Dentsu rigged 43 billion yen in venue operation bids. The Japanese archipelago was set ablaze with fury. Critics slammed the Olympics, calling it not a national festival, but a massive feast where Dentsu and corrupt bureaucrats pillaged taxpayer money. As the situation spiraled out of control, municipalities across Japan imposed unprecedented sanctions, suspending Dentsu's eligibility for public project bids. This was exactly when preparations for the Nagoya Asian Games were beginning. Having witnessed the Tokyo disaster firsthand, the Nagoya Organizing Committee was gripped by fear. They concluded that hiring a major agency like Dentsu would inevitably lead to astronomical costs and another corruption scandal. In the end, Nagoya City made a drastic decision. We will abandon the authoritarian approach of relying on private agencies. Without private planners, we will run the event ourselves using city officials and volunteers. It was a declaration to nip corruption in the bud and host a frugal event through the pure efforts of citizens and bureaucrats. On the surface, it was a truly noble and clean cause. But the world of business is not a moral textbook. It was like a hotel owner, terrified of being cheated by the chef, shutting down the kitchen and forcing security guards and janitors who have never cooked to prepare a French course meal for 1,000 VIP guests . There was no way general administrative officials, who rotate positions every three years, could handle multi-billion won global broadcasting rights negotiations or complex multinational logistics. While they clung to manuals and regulations to avoid responsibility, the administrative vacuum at the site grew uncontrollably. The price for throwing decades of accumulated mega-event expertise into the trash—driven by a fragile pride to avoid corruption—was devastating. Blinded by the cause of avoiding corruption, they severed the very expertise that served as the backbone of the entire event. And the first destructive blow of this amateur administration, stripped of expertise, fell upon the dormitories where athletes were meant to stay. The heart of any large-scale international event, like the Olympics or the Asian Games, is the athletes 'village. Hundreds of billions of won are spent to build large apartment complexes, providing a controlled environment where athletes from around the world can safely rest and dine. After the games, these apartments are usually sold to the public, turning them into assets that increase the city's housing supply. However, the Nagoya Organizing Committee had a different idea. If the apartments remain unsold after the games, who will shoulder the debt? We will not build an athletes' village at all. The plan they proposed as an alternative was truly bizarre. They intended to dock the Italian cruise ship Costa Serena, capable of holding 4,000 people, in Nagoya Port and build prefabricated container boxes on a barren harbor lot to house 2,000 more. They announced that the remaining 9,000 people would be split up and accommodated in business hotels and motels throughout Nagoya. Furthermore, the Nagoya port site was a desolate facility battered by sea winds, an empty lot lacking even a single convenience store or restaurant. For the 9,000 people in the city hotels, rooms were so scarce they had to beg for space even in low-end love hotels on the outskirts. An absurd situation unfolded where national athletes, who rely on safe isolation and rest, had to share elevators with drunken patrons and tourists. The idea of scattering athletes instead of housing them together was practically a declaration to abandon transport, security, and dining management, but the real ugly secret was hidden inside the container housing. When national teams arrived at the Nagoya container village ahead of the opening, they were absolutely horrified by the scene. The room was packed with just a single bed, and if you opened a suitcase on the floor, the door wouldn't even close. Athletes finishing training had nowhere to put their sweat-soaked sneakers, so they had to stack them right next to the trash bin in the corner. The size of the beds was even more serious. Basketball and volleyball players standing well over 190cm or 2m tall couldn't even stretch their legs out while lying down. They had to spend the night wide awake with their shins and ankles hanging off the edge of the bed. The air conditioning kept stopping, leading to sweltering heat, and when it rained, water leaked through the gaps in the container walls. Why on earth did the city of Nagoya force world-class athletes into these wretched boxes? It wasn't just a simple lack of construction funds. The post-use plan for the container facilities drafted by the Nagoya Organizing Committee held a shocking truth. The plan was to resell these containers as used goods to local governments and private businesses across Japan as soon as the games ended . They calculated that they could recoup some construction costs by selling them as disaster relief housing for earthquake or flood victims or as makeshift guest houses in the countryside. To sell them off, whose physique would they need to fit? They wouldn't sell well in the second-hand market unless they fit the smaller, more compact frames of ordinary people, rather than world-class athletes. They intentionally made the rooms small and installed tiny beds because they thought wider beds and bigger rooms would make the furniture hard to resell later due to non-standard sizing. How is this any different from a shameless host who invites guests over but only buys toddler beds and tiny slippers, thinking about selling them on a secondhand market later? They didn't care at all about the conditions of the athletes, who should have been the protagonists of the international sports event. In the minds of the bureaucrats, only a shallow commercial calculator was whirring away, focused on cutting costs and balancing the books by reselling items. However, the sleeping arrangements were merely the prelude to a much larger catastrophe to come. Because when the athletes walked through the doors of the dining hall, they were forced to face an even more insulting reality. In sports nutrition, diet accounts for half of an athlete's performance. For national team athletes who burn thousands of calories a day, high-quality protein and balanced carbohydrates are absolute weapons that determine victory or defeat. Yet, what unfolded before the eyes of the athletes from various countries as they faced the buffet line at the Nagoya Asian Games was an unbelievable sight. The food on their trays consisted of nothing more than three small pieces of fried chicken and a single steamed broccoli floret. When athletes in combat or team sports who need to maintain weight and power begged for just a little more meat, the masked staff shook their heads firmly. Regulations do not allow for extra servings. If you eat more, you'll exceed your calorie limit. A menu poorer than a standard elementary school lunch was served identically for three days in a row, and even meal choices were strictly limited to just one of two options. As the food was too meager to fill their stomachs, tearful farces began to unfold on-site. Managers and coaches, who should have been directing games and tactical training, began leaving the training grounds to sprint to local convenience stores and supermarkets 30 minutes away . The sight of coaches sweating profusely as they bought up bunches of bananas, loaves of bread, chocolate, and cup noodles looked like a relief operation in a refugee camp. Eventually , delivery scooters from local restaurants began lining up one after another in front of the side gate of the official athletes 'village. At an international event's official accommodation, where outside food should be strictly prohibited for doping and hygiene control, an unprecedented scene occurred where national athletes received rice bowls and pizzas through the side gate just to get a meal. It was like inviting guests to a banquet, handing them a single cup of noodles, and telling them to fend for themselves if they were hungry. The administrative paralysis was not limited to the dining hall. By excluding professional event planners, the civil servants and inexperienced volunteers brought in to run the site couldn't even manage basic transportation schedules. With transport buses failing to arrive on time in a string of so-called "no-shows ," the Indian delegation, having flown for dozens of hours, was left waiting aimlessly on the airport floor. There was even a comedic dispatch blunder where the bus for the South Korean soccer team, headed for training, was sent to the wrong baseball stadium. The diplomatic discourtesy was even more severe. Before a hockey match, the North Korean anthem was played instead of the opponent's, sparking protest, and at the official welcome ceremony for the delegations, a character of Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who instigated the Imjin War, appeared, drawing a fierce official protest from the Korean delegation. It was the height of ignorance, showing not even a shred of consideration for a neighbor's historical wounds just because he was a historical figure from Nagoya. What was even more baffling was the reaction of the Japanese public. Despite this massive international event taking place, Japanese terrestrial broadcasters did not even air the opening ceremony. By making it so that only those who signed up for paid OTT platforms could watch the games, the majority of the Japanese public passed the time without even knowing the Asian Games were being held in their own country. Only after foreign media outlets poured out criticisms in their headlines daily, declaring that the image of developed Japan had completely collapsed, did Japanese broadcasters stage a farce, reporting on these " embarrassing news stories coming from abroad" as if they were disconnected observers. But here, a truly chilling question arises. If they didn't build an athletes' village, kicked out professional agencies, and pinched pennies even on three pieces of fried chicken, wouldn't it be normal for there to be a lot of money left in Nagoya's coffers? However, when the lid was finally lifted on the Nagoya Asian Games 'final settlement, it sent the whole world into a state of shock once again. Nagoya, which confined athletes to containers and gave them three pieces of fried chicken to save money. Then, what exactly was the amount written on the final bill that Nagoya received after the games ended? The total budget Nagoya initially pledged was 100 billion yen, or approximately 900 billion won. But ahead of the games , the final total project cost exposed by Japan's Mainichi Shimbun and local media was a staggering 370 billion yen, approaching 3.3 trillion won in our currency. The budget they claimed they would save had snowballed by a massive 3.7 times, nearly fourfold. Where on earth did all that enormous money disappear to? The result of excluding professional planning firms and having amateur bureaucrats jump in led to a miserable trail of money. The first hole was the "patchwork" outsourcing. Inexperienced civil servants failed to plan the detailed schedules and procurement of goods for a massive international event in advance. As the opening date drew near, they had to scramble to secure transport buses, personnel, and interpreters from the private market at a premium through emergency private contracts. Without the know-how to lower unit costs through bulk pre-contracts, short-term service fees and emergency dispatch costs skyrocketed. The second pitfall was the cruise ships and containers meant to replace the athletes' village. The cruise ships floating on the sea were not free. Tens of billions of yen vanished in an instant on charter fees for overseas vessels to house 4,000 people, port docking fees, massive fuel costs, and crew wages. The container housing built on the barren port side was even more painful. To build accommodation for 2,000 people on empty land, one must install new water and sewage pipes, connect massive power grids, and set up septic facilities. The public funds poured into this temporary civil engineering work— which had to be dismantled after the event—could have easily built a whole apartment complex. The plan to renovate existing gymnasiums also turned into a disaster. The old stadiums built in the 70s and 80s fell far short of modern international standards and safety codes, and renovation costs doubled with every repair, from AC replacement to seismic reinforcement. In the end, absurd contradictions emerged everywhere, with repair costs exceeding the price of building new stadiums. On top of this, the collapse of the yen and the global surge in raw material prices and labor costs dealt a fatal blow. A professional planning firm would have diversified risk through long-term futures contracts or price-indexing, but bureaucrats tied to administrative regulations had to face the soaring bills head-on. It was like trying to save 100,000 won on shop labor by taking the engine apart yourself in the yard, only to break all the parts and send your 30-million-won car to the scrapyard. More than three times the original budget was poured in , while expected revenue hit rock bottom. Disgusted by the corruption surrounding the Tokyo Olympics, the Japanese public turned their backs, and with even terrestrial broadcasts disappearing, ticket sales and souvenir revenue suffered a crushing defeat. Companies also avoided being sponsors for a tournament plagued by scandals, causing the initially expected sponsorship income to shrink as well. All that remained were trillions in debt and the humiliating disgrace of inviting international shame by skimping on competent administration. Nagoya’s confident vow to host the games frugally for 100 million yen ended with an astronomical waste of 3.3 trillion won in public funds and a pathetic meal of three chicken nuggets in a container box. But is this painful paradox truly only a tragedy for Nagoya , Japan, across the sea? We already experienced the exact same nightmare over a decade ago. During the 2014 Incheon Asian Games, the city of Incheon promised an economic boost of 20 trillion won and took on over 1.7 trillion won in debt to build 17 new stadiums. After the two-week spectacle ended, what remained was an annual burden of over 100 billion won in principal and interest, and Incheon citizens are still paying off that debt with their taxes to this very day, through 2029. On the other hand, Hanoi, Vietnam, which had won the bid for the 2019 Asian Games back in 2014, realized it had no money to build stadiums or host the event. Citing that it would be a disaster for the national treasury, they made the historic decision to voluntarily return the hosting rights for the first time ever. They chose a cold, realistic path to avoid national bankruptcy rather than prioritizing face or pride. Mega sports events are by no means magic wands that guarantee the glory of a city. The amateurism that discarded expertise out of fear of corruption, and the incompetence of bureaucrats who threw an unaffordable party and then cut corners on guest accommodations to save costs, have ultimately returned as a harsh debt bill knocking on the doors of the citizens.

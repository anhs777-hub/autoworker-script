This is not the food alley of Gwangjang Market. The scent of oil from frying bindaetteok fills the alley, and long lines of foreigners stretch out in front of the shops. But. There are people who visit Gwangjang Market but just walk right past the food alley. They are clearly tourists, as every single one of them is pulling a suitcase. The place where they stop is none other than a bedding shop. They stroke the blankets with their palms or show the patterns to their families back home via video call. Some individuals even buy dozens of them at a time. On a good day, as many as 200 blankets are sold in this alley alone. But. What's strange is that the shop signs prominently say "Specializing in Wedding Bedding." Yet, not a single Korean customer is to be seen. The spot once filled by our mothers coming to pick out bedding for their daughters ' weddings is now occupied by Taiwanese families who have traveled across the sea. Why on earth would they fly here to buy blankets that are big, heavy, and don't even fit in a suitcase? To give you a hint, the answer is hidden within the blankets themselves. Today, I'll tell you that story. 200 blankets a day is amazing, but there’s an even more staggering figure. In some shops, 99 out of 100 customers are Taiwanese. Merchants say that almost all of their revenue comes from the wallets of Taiwanese customers. It’s a market in the heart of Seoul, but business is effectively being done for Taiwan. At this point, it feels a bit awkward to even call it a Korean market. Let’s take a step inside that alley. It’s only about 50 meters long. In every shop, blankets are piled up to the ceiling, and as if that weren't enough, piles of bedding line both sides of the walkway. It's just wide enough for two adults to walk side-by-side. Between the piles of blankets, there are white papers attached. Written in black marker, the text says "Chinese OK." Next to it, there’s also a sign that says "We accept Taiwan Dollars." Right in the middle of Jongno, they’ve posted that they accept foreign currency. And it's a bedding shop, not a currency exchange. Originally, this was an alley where wholesale bedding merchants of Seoul gathered. Bedding shops from all over the country used to source their goods here. You could say it was the headquarters of our country's bedding industry. Now, that entire alley has been taken over by Taiwanese customers. At this point, it’s no wonder the rumor traveled across the sea. And it actually did. In March 2026, the Taiwanese current affairs magazine CommonWealth covered this alley in a major article. The headline goes like this. Taiwanese people saved an old Korean alley. How a set of blankets turned around a 40-year fate. Saved it, you say? It means that Taiwanese people have saved our market. I never imagined we’d live to see ourselves being featured as a subject for "saving" in a foreign magazine. But honestly, I can’t say I’m upset about it—it’s actually a strange feeling, not bad at all. If a foreign magazine is going to these lengths, it means this isn’t just some fleeting trend. Now, let’s look up and check the sign over there. Specializing in wedding bedding. "Wedding items"— that’s the term you see on so many signs in this alleyway. The term " wedding items." It’s a bit of an unfamiliar word for young people these days, isn’t it? Why would a shop where 99%of the customers are Taiwanese have a sign that says "wedding items"? Gwangjang Market, where this alley is located, opened its doors in 1905. It’s the first permanent market in our country, and it’s over 120 years old now. Throughout all those years, the busiest time for this alley was when every household was preparing for weddings. Those who had their wedding bedding custom-made back then will remember. When sending off a daughter or welcoming a daughter-in-law, mothers would seek out this alley. A heavy quilt stuffed generously with cotton, topped with pillows, made quite a load. Only when the quilt felt heavy in their hands could they finally feel at ease. To those mothers, the weight of the quilt was a measure of their devotion. When the cotton flattened, they’d take those custom quilts to a cotton carding shop to be puffed up again. That’s how they kept warm for 10 or 20 years. A single quilt was effectively the history of that household. But those customers have one by one disappeared. In 1996, the number of marriages in our country was 435,000 . That number dropped to 192,000 by 2022. It wasn’t just that the guests were halved, but the weddings themselves were cut in half. It ticked up slightly to 222,000 in 2024, but compared to 30 years ago, it’s still nearly halved. With the number of marriages cut in half, how could the wedding bedding alley possibly hold on? Even the young couples who did get married stopped getting their bedding at the market. They added items to their Coupang cart on their phones and had them at their door the next morning . The wedding bedding business moved out of the market alleys and into online shopping carts. From the alley’s perspective, they had lost their customers twice over. The ironic part is that around that same time, Gwangjang Market itself started bustling with foreigners. Ever since Netflix featured this market in an Asian street food documentary in 2019, foreign foot traffic has surged. They say that in 2024, seven out of ten visitors were foreigners. If seven out of ten are visitors, the market itself has already become a tourist destination. But the crowds only flocked to the food alley where they fry the mung bean pancakes. The bedding and hanbok alleys just a few steps away could only watch the queues from afar. It was like the house right next door was having a feast, while our own front gate remained firmly shut. In November 2024, 64-year-old Jung Kyung-bae, who has sold hanbok in this market for 45 years, said this. Things are worse now than during the pandemic. There are days when I can’t even make a single sale. He means it’s harder now than back when customers completely disappeared due to COVID-19. For those in business, there’s no statement more painful than that. The head of the merchants' association at the time also acknowledged that the hanbok and bedding shops were in a slump. To anyone, this alleyway was quietly fading away. However, that very same month, a strange article appeared. Another newspaper wrote the exact opposite story about the same market's bedding shops. Taiwanese tourists are swarming in like a house on fire. It was the same month, November 2024. One paper wrote they couldn't make a single sale all day, while the other wrote the bedding shops were booming. Was one of them lying? No. Both were true. Even within the same market, a completely different wind was blowing only through the bedding alley. So, what on earth happened in that alley that winter? If you follow the records from Taiwan, you can find a clue. Starting in 2019, Taiwanese customers began visiting this alley occasionally, and in 2020, a video about it appeared on YouTube. But just then, the pandemic broke out, and the video was quietly buried. Then, as the borders reopened in 2022 and 2023, a wave of "revenge travel" began. Word-of-mouth spread through social media, and with proxy shopping added to the mix, it really took off. So, that winter was truly a crossroads. Just when the hanbok alley was sighing in despair, Taiwanese tour buses began pulling up in front of the bedding alley. Why, of all places, did those buses stop in front of an alley that was dying? Doesn't it seem a bit strange? When Taiwanese people visit Korea, why would they only buy bedding? There are cosmetics and clothes, so why specifically these big, heavy blankets? They don't fit in suitcases and they just take up luggage space on the plane . I gave you a hint at the beginning. The answer is hidden in the bedding itself. Now it's time to peel back the layers of that blanket one by one. The first secret is in the price tag. A similar quality blanket costs about 200,000 won in Taiwan. But in this alley, it’s around 60,000 won. That’s a difference of more than three times. How would that price tag look to a Taiwanese person? Some tourists even said they would have to pay hundreds of thousands of won at a Taiwanese department store. With 140,000 won saved on every purchase, their wallets were bound to open. On top of this, the exchange rate poured oil on the fire. The value of the won has dropped 11.5%against the Taiwanese dollar just this year. Even with the same amount of New Taiwan Dollars, they get much more Korean Won in their hands than last year. Simply put, to Taiwanese people, the whole of Korea looks like a store running an 11%off sale all year round. Remember when the Japanese Yen hit rock bottom and we filled our shopping baskets at Japanese drugstores? We packed our bags until they were bursting with pain relief patches, eye drops, and snacks. That is exactly how Taiwanese people are feeling in Seoul right now. But just being cheap isn't enough of a reason to haul all that bulk onto a plane. After all, there are plenty of cheap goods in Taiwan, too. Price was just the first key that opened the door. The second secret is the weather. There is a twist hidden here. It's easy to think, " Taiwan is a warm island, so why would they need thick blankets?" But it's actually the opposite. Because it is a warm island, the winter actually feels colder. In Taiwan, winter temperatures outside drop into the 10 degrees Celsius range. Doesn't look like much just by the numbers, right? The problem is the house. Homes in Taiwan rarely have heating systems. On top of that, the island's unique humidity seeps right into your bones. There are posts on Taiwanese online forums saying that friends from Germany and Finland find the Taiwanese winter colder. It means the island's winter is harsh enough to make people from snowy countries wave their hands in refusal. Merchants in this alley also explain that because Taiwan has no floor heating culture, blankets are a necessity. Try to imagine what that feels like. You go to a seaside pension in late autumn, and the heating is broken. The outside temperature isn't even below freezing, but the floor is cold and the air is damp. Even if you cover yourself with a blanket, the blanket itself is damp, keeping you tossing and turning all night. This is the kind of night Taiwanese people spend every winter. And it's not just for one night, but all winter long. In our homes, the boiler warms up the floor nice and toasty. The blanket just acts as a light cover for that warmth. But Taiwanese homes don't have that floor heating. So on that island, a single blanket is essentially the boiler itself. Do you get a sense of how important a blanket is to the people on that island? But what would happen if that boiler was soaking wet? Cotton batting is a fiber that absorbs water very well. On a humid island, a cotton blanket becomes a sponge that never dries. It gets heavy and damp when you cover yourself with it, so it can't do its job as a boiler properly. It’s gotten to the point where Taiwanese doctors recommend synthetic fiber blankets instead of pure cotton on humid days. If doctors are advising against pure cotton, which is good for the body, you can guess how terrible that humidity must be, right? The heavy cotton that our mothers once considered a symbol of devotion was a nuisance on that island. On that island, weight wasn't a sign of devotion, but a burden . So, what makes these blankets from this alleyway different? The answer lies in a fabric called Modal. What is it, exactly? It’s a fiber made by dissolving beech wood pulp and spinning it back into thread. Simply put, it’s cotton made from wood. The name might be unfamiliar, but it’s a fabric commonly used for pajamas and underwear these days. It absorbs up to 50%more sweat than cotton and releases that moisture much faster. You know how a cotton T-shirt stays damp and sticks to your back for a long time when you sweat? Modal absorbs even more sweat but dries it off much quicker. Instead of cotton batting, it's filled with synthetic fiber. It’s a type of fill that doesn't clump up even when you toss the whole thing in the washing machine. Just think back for a moment. In the past, you had to rip out all the seams just to wash a blanket. You had to wash the cover separately, starch it , iron it, and sew it all back together , stitch by stitch. Our mothers 'entire day would be consumed by that task. That day-long labor now ends with the push of a single washing machine button . If our mothers saw this, they would say the world has really gotten better. For the people living on that island, there’s no product more welcome than this. A blanket that’s light, absorbs sweat well, dries quickly, and is fully machine-washable. This was exactly what was needed for Taiwanese winters. It was an affordable blanket perfectly suited for the island's weather. However, one problem remains. No matter how light a blanket is, it won't fit in a suitcase, right? But this alleyway had a machine that solved that problem in one go. Now that you know the principle, here is where it gets real. You’ll be jaw-dropped when you see the numbers on how this alleyway cleared that final hurdle. The third secret is volume. Airline luggage isn't just about weight; volume is the real headache. A single puffy blanket can fill up an entire suitcase. The weapon this alleyway brought out here is vacuum packaging. Once you understand the logic, you'll be hitting your knee in realization. The real secret to why a blanket keeps us warm isn't actually the batting. It's the air trapped between the fibers. It’s the same principle as why wearing several thin layers is warmer than one thick coat in winter. That air acts like a blanket that holds onto your body heat. So, most of the volume of a puffy blanket is just air. Anyone who has put a winter parka into a compression bag and used a vacuum to suck the air out will know. That thick parka becomes as flat as a sheet of paper. It’s the same for these blankets. They put the blanket into a large plastic bag and use a machine to suck all the air out. Then, the duvet that was puffed up like a mountain is pressed flat like a board . A duvet that was a hand's span thick shrinks down to the thickness of two or three finger joints. Taiwanese travel blogs claim the volume decreases by over 70%and stays that way for three to seven days. When you get home and open the plastic, the synthetic fibers spring back up like a coil. If it were cotton batting, that would be impossible. It would have clumped up like a squashed rice cake, eventually needing a professional fluffing. The weight remains the same, but the volume has magically vanished. A whole duvet fits perfectly into one corner of a suitcase. This vacuum-sealing spectacle itself became a fascinating Korean market attraction for foreigners and spread via social media. Whenever people see the machine "whoosh" and suck the air out, causing the duvet to shrink instantly, they can't help but pull out their phones. Then, the quantity they buy changed. It doesn't end with just one or two. Every group that visits buys at least five, sometimes as many as ten. A merchant who has been wholesaling bedding for 30 years describes the scene. Young Taiwanese people video call their parents, show them the duvets one by one, and buy five or six at a time. One for mom, one for dad, one for an aunt. In the end, they are buying bedding for the whole family in Seoul. Now, shall we crunch the numbers? At 60,000 won each, ten of them would cost 600,000 won. If you bought the same quality in Taiwan for 200,000 won each, it would be 2 million won. That’s a difference of 1.4 million won. Just by buying ten duvets, you save enough to more than cover the cost of a round-trip flight. At this point, it’s not that they’re coming to Seoul to buy bedding, but that they’re making back their airfare while visiting. What about the ones that won't fit in the suitcase? For just 10,000 to 20,000 won , they will ship it to your home in Taiwan. By paying a little more than a standard shipping fee, the bedding flies across the ocean directly to your bedroom. That’s why you see stacks of flat bundles, labeled with invoices and flight info, lined up in front of the shops. Price, weather, and volume— everything has been resolved. Now, it’s become even harder to find a reason not to buy. But why now, specifically? I mean, this alleyway has always been here. This bedding alley has kept its place for decades. The final, fourth secret is the wallets of the Taiwanese people. Thanks to semiconductors, Taiwan is literally experiencing a windfall of money. The entire country is in the middle of a massive boom. Taiwan's economic growth rate for the second quarter of 2025 was 8.01%. It was 8.01%. The annual growth rate hit its highest level in 39 years. 39 years means this is the first boom that a 40-year-old office worker has seen in their lifetime. The stock market index has also nearly doubled in just one year. If your stock account has doubled, you wouldn't mind opening your wallet while traveling abroad. And there is some painful news for us as well. Taiwan's GDP per capita has surpassed South Korea's for the first time in 22 years. The IMF projects 2026 GDP per capita at $ 41,600 for Taiwan and $ 37,500 for South Korea. That is a difference of more than $ 4,000 per person. People from a country wealthier than ours are now buying quilts from our local markets. With heavier wallets , their visits have also increased. The number of Taiwanese people who visited Korea in 2019 was 1.26 million. In 2025 , that number grew to 1.89 million. It increased by 1.5 times in just six years. That period even included the time when travel was completely blocked by the pandemic. Considering that, it's closer to an explosion than a recovery. There are even forecasts that the number will exceed 2 million this year. Their spending has increased even more steeply. Taiwanese tourists' domestic card spending rose by 39%, which is twice as fast as that of Japanese or Chinese tourists. It means their wallets are opening faster than the number of people is growing. Now, does everything make sense? It’s affordable, perfect for the weather, fits in a suitcase, and their wallets are full. That is everything. However, if the story ended here, it would have just been categorized as a quirky shopping trend. But the real story starts now. If you think about it, there is something strange about these four conditions. Wouldn't there be quilts that are cheap, high-quality, and offer packaging in Japan, too? In 2025, 18.94 million Taiwanese people traveled abroad. Of those, 35.5%went to Japan. Only 9.7%headed to Korea. They visit Japan 3.7 times more than they visit Korea. Would there be no good quilts in Japan? Yet, the nickname " mecca of quilt shopping" was given to a back alley in Jongno, not Tokyo. The secret was in the layout of this alley. Usually, if you want to buy a quilt and send it home across the sea, what do you have to do? You have to choose it at a store, ask for separate packaging, and find a shipping service elsewhere. But in this alley, picking out a quilt, touching it, comparing prices at the next shop, vacuum-sealing it flat, and shipping it home all happen within 50 meters. To use an analogy, it’s like having a department store bedding section, a packaging center, and a post office all crammed into one alley. Tourists can finish their shopping just by walking down one alley. Could there be a place more convenient for group tourists pressed for time? But the truly chilling part starts right now. What was the identity of those mountains of quilts piled up to the ceiling? They were originally wholesale supplies intended for quilt shops across the country. It’s the very stock that kept piling up in the warehouse once demand for wedding bedding plummeted. That mountain of goods that merchants used to sigh at turned out to be a treasure trove full of the joy of shopping for Taiwanese visitors. With so many patterns, thicknesses, and colors to choose from, the experience of selecting them is truly special. It’s as if the entire alley, not just one or two shops, is one massive bedding display. The very reason they lost Korean customers has flipped into a weapon that attracts foreign customers. The reason for failure became the reason for survival. The name of this market, Gwangjang, uses Chinese characters that mean "wide " and "store/hide." It means to gather widely and store carefully. Since the inventory that was just gathering dust has brought in customers, it has finally lived up to its name after 120 years. But there is one more strange connection here. Taiwan also had a tradition of wedding bedding. According to a Taiwanese bedding artisan, the bride’s side would prepare a pair of top and bottom quilts, and someone with a "good zodiac sign" from the groom’s side would roll on them to wish for blessings. In Tainan, southern Taiwan, there was a saying that a bride's dowry filled an ox cart, and there were even streets dedicated to selling wedding goods. A story is told of an elderly couple in their 70s who visited that artisan. They couldn't bring themselves to throw away their 50-year-old wedding quilt, so they brought it in to have it re-fluffed. It’s remarkably similar to the scene of our traditional cotton-fluffing shops. However, the bedding alleys in Taiwan also faded away due to online shopping and large supermarkets. As the wedding bedding cultures of both countries were disappearing side by side, one alley survived thanks to customers from the other. It truly is a strange twist of fate. Of course, when customers flock like this, problems are bound to arise. In December 2025, this report was released. A Taiwanese tourist tried to pay by card, but after being told they would have to pay more, they had to rush to exchange cash. Some shops even claimed it was difficult to issue a cash receipt when asked. There was even harsh criticism that the bedding alley had become a hotbed for tax evasion. Reports continued that market stalls were repeatedly refusing cards and only accepting bank transfers. The issue of price gouging even spread to bedding. In May 2026, a story about a bedding shop in Busan’s International Market became a hot topic in Taiwan. It was revealed that a shop had a "Welcome Taiwanese Customers" sign up, yet charged group tours 50,000 to 60,000 won for bedding worth 35,000 won. It’s embarrassing, considering they claimed to welcome guests while charging them more than 1.5 times the price. Gwangjang Market has already experienced a permanent expulsion system for stalls due to price gouging on assorted pancakes and blood sausage in the food alley. It would be troublesome if the same disgraceful behavior were to repeat in the bedding alley. And there is one final twist left. It’s a paradox created because Korean bedding is just too good. In Taiwan, importers, online shops, and proxy buyers are popping up to sell Korean bedding. They deliver them right to homes in Taiwan at prices barely higher than in Seoul markets. Naturally , local merchants are saying that fewer customers are visiting in person compared to last year. The better the goods, the less reason there is to hop on a plane to buy them. It is a truly ironic situation. It's unclear whether to be happy that products are selling well or worried that the alleys might fall quiet. But doesn't this scene of people sweeping up another country's goods look familiar to you? We had the exact same scene at our airports 40 years ago. Now, can we really just brush this off as someone else's story? In January 1983, 17 members of a housewives 'group in Busan visited Shimonoseki and Fukuoka, Japan. Since overseas travel only became fully unrestricted in 1989, just taking a trip to Japan was a huge deal back then . The scene at customs on the way back home was quite a spectacle. Japanese newspapers reported that there were even women pushing their luggage with their feet because they had so much they ran out of hands. What could have been inside those bags? Do you remember those who went to see the rice cookers at neighbors' houses who had been to Japan back then? It was the Elephant brand electric rice cooker. According to reports from the Dong-A Ilbo at the time, around 1982, over 15,000 Japanese rice cookers were entering the country annually, with 40 to 60 arriving every day. The headline of an article in Japan's Asahi Shimbun at the time went like this. Sales are increasing thanks to Korean customers. It caused an uproar back home in Korea. Criticism poured in that it was a national disgrace, leading to the arrest of travel agency employees and even cases of people voluntarily returning their goods. They bought a rice cooker and ended up getting scolded by the entire nation. Now, place these two headlines side by side. In 1983, a Japanese newspaper wrote that sales were increasing thanks to Korean customers. In 2026, a Taiwanese magazine wrote that Taiwanese people saved Korea’s old alleys. In 40 years, the roles have completely reversed. The heart with which we used to embrace Japanese rice cookers back then is what Taiwanese people now feel for our bedding. Doesn't it give you goosebumps? Those who once pushed luggage with their feet at foreign customs have now become the ones with foreign customers lining up. There is a story after that, too. Local rice cooker makers, criticized for not being able to make a decent pot, gritted their teeth and improved their quality. By the late 1990s, they began to surpass Japanese products, and now foreign tourists specifically seek out Korean rice cooker stores. An item that once brought shame at foreign customs has now become a product we take pride in. So, where did the bedding that the Taiwanese people are taking home come from? The words of a merchant who has run a bedding shop in this alley for 30 years are a masterpiece. They’re all domestic products from Daegu, yet Koreans are the only ones who don't realize how good our quilts are. We didn't know their value because they were things that had always been in our closets. The saying "it's darkest under the candle" is perfect for this situation. Of course, there is still work to be done. Merchants say that because 90%of their sales come from customers from one country, the ups and downs are bound to be significant. Professor Kim Nam-jo of Hanyang University's Department of Tourism pointed out that it might be difficult to sustain this without creating products tailored specifically for Taiwanese customers. At the same time, he noted that this trend could potentially spread to customers in Southeast Asia. Ultimately, the answer might lie in the path taken by the rice cooker. The path of raising quality to meet the customer's expectations. Just like the rice cooker, the quilt has now just passed its first milestone. Now, shall we return to that alley we started with? This time, you'll see it through different eyes. Taiwanese families walking past the smell of mung bean pancakes, holding flat-pressed quilt bundles in their arms. And above their heads, the old sign for wedding quilts still hangs. The shops are the same, the owners are the same, only the customers have crossed the sea. This isn't just someone else's story. From January to August this year, foreigners spent 7.7813 trillion won on cards in Seoul. That’s a 59.9%increase in just one year. Among traditional markets, Gwangjang Market saw the biggest jump, increasing by a staggering 127.4%. It has more than doubled in just one year. What foreigners are flying in to buy aren't souvenirs, but items that were already in Korean homes. The quilts in our closets and goods from local markets are, in fact, exports. When one quilt is sold, that money returns to the quilt factory in Daegu, to the market merchant's shop, and back to our neighborhood. Every time a Taiwanese customer carries away a quilt, it's like a small light flickering on in our local economy. So, whenever you see news about foreigners buying up our goods, just look for these three things . Whether the exchange rate is favorable, whether it embodies our unique lifestyle technology, and whether there's a way to carry it home; the quilt alley hit all three. An 11.5% weaker won, light and quick-drying quilts perfect for the humid island, and vacuum packaging that compresses them flat. When these three things align, it's a different story from just a fleeting trend. Quilts that mothers once measured by weight for their devotion now cross the sea because of their lightness. We, pushing our luggage with our feet at the customs desk in 1983, and the Taiwanese family in 2024 clutching their flat quilts as they board a plane—they look like they overlap.

From April to June this year, Korean Air's revenue exceeded 5 trillion won. This is the highest revenue ever recorded for the April-to-June period. However, during those same three months , operating profit actually fell by 34% . More money came in, but the profit left over decreased. We can see why by tracking just one of the company's planes. It is the two-story, 407-seat A380 that flies daily from Incheon to New York. How much does Korean Air make when this plane makes a round trip to New York? We calculated all the revenue and expenses for one round trip. This includes ticket sales, cargo, fuel costs, airport fees, crew wages, and aircraft ownership costs. Korean Air's revenue starts with ticket prices, but there isn't just one price on this plane. The 407 seats are divided into three categories. There are 12 first-class seats at the front, 94 prestige seats behind them, and the remaining 301 are economy seats. So, how much do the prices differ on the same flight? If you buy the cheapest round-trip ticket departing in November while in the U.S., economy is $ 1,052, or about 1.4 million won. Prestige class is about 8.3 million won, and first class is about 27.7 million won. They board the same plane and arrive at the same time, yet the front seat costs nearly 20 times more than economy. Even within economy class, there isn't just one price. Korean Air divides its economy class pricing into 13 tiers. The reservation computer calculates the minimum required price per seat for each flight and sells only tickets at that tier or higher. As seats fill up, cheaper tiers often sell out, so the later you buy, the more likely only expensive tickets remain. That is why the price of the same economy seat varies greatly depending on when you purchase it. Then, which seats make the most money for Korean Air? In 2019, before COVID-19, Korean Air earned about 2.2 trillion won from seat sales on routes to the U.S. and Canada. Even though there are far fewer business-class seats than economy, they accounted for 37%of that revenue. Conversely, you might find yourself sitting next to a passenger who is flying further than you but paid less for the same economy seat. This is because many passengers on this flight do not start or end their journey in Incheon. In the first half of this year , 44%of passengers on Korean Air flights from Incheon to New York had transferred in Incheon after arriving from other countries. If the flight is 90%full, as it was in the first half of this year, there are about 360 passengers on this A380, and about 160 of them—or 44%—are transfer passengers. Over 40%of the passengers on the return flight from New York are also people transferring in Incheon to reach other countries. How much did these passengers pay? Sometimes it is cheaper than for passengers getting off in Incheon. The same applies to flights returning from New York. Looking at a Korean Air round-trip ticket departing next January purchased in the US, a ticket from New York to Incheon is $ 964, while a ticket from New York to Manila via Incheon is $ 800. You fly one extra leg and go further, yet it's $ 164 cheaper. Since you can also fly from Manila to New York on Japanese or US airlines, they have to match that price to attract customers. Not all cities are like that. A ticket to Bangkok, Thailand, was more expensive than a ticket to Incheon. The ticket price also includes money that doesn't go to Korean Air. Incheon Airport fees. Departure levies paid to the government when leaving Korea. When you combine taxes and fees for entering and leaving the US, it comes to about 125,000 won per person for a round trip. Korean Air just collects this money on their behalf and passes it to the airport and the two governments. When calculating Korean Air's share, these taxes are excluded. Instead, fuel surcharges, which are added to the ticket price when oil prices rise, are included. If we average it from the first-class seats at the front to economy at the back, the amount one passenger pays to Korean Air for a one-way trip is about 1.3 million won. As mentioned earlier, if the seats are 90%full, there are 733 round-trip passengers, so the total amount paid by passengers is about 950 million won. The money Korean Air makes from this plane isn't just from ticket prices. Money also comes from beneath the passengers 'feet. There is another cargo hold below the passenger seats. It’s where they load cargo sent by companies, in addition to passengers' checked bags. What kind of cargo and how much is loaded into this hold? Korean Air passenger flights from Incheon to New York carry an average of about 10 tons of cargo per flight. About 8 tons of that is luggage that arrived in Incheon from other countries and was transferred to this plane. It’s not just the passengers who transfer in Incheon; the luggage transfers too. However, the A380 we are following carries less cargo than other passenger planes. Since there are so many passengers, the weight of the passengers and their bags takes up the plane's weight capacity first. It carries 6.4 tons on the flight to New York and 2.3 tons on the return flight. Even if the amount loaded is small, cargo to and from the US is expensive to ship. Among the cargo Korean Air carried this year, flights to and from the US account for less than 30%by weight, but they make up more than half of the cargo revenue. Since the cost to send 1kg to New York is about 7,000 won , the A380 brings in about 60 million won in cargo revenue per round trip. Adding 950 million won from passengers and 60 million won from cargo, Korean Air earns about 1.01 billion won per round trip. The biggest expense out of this 1.01 billion won is the cost of fuel. How much fuel does an A380 consume for a round trip to New York? It is about 344 tons. That’s enough to fill about 2,000 200-liter drums, or over 8,000 passenger cars. At the price Korean Air actually paid in the first half of this year, the fuel cost for one round trip is about 500 million won . Half of the revenue earned goes toward fuel costs. At last year’s prices, it would have been about 340 million won. They are paying 160 million won more for the same amount of fuel. Looking at the company as a whole , it is even clearer. From April to June this year, Korean Air’s fuel expenses increased by over 1 trillion won compared to the same three months a year ago, more than doubling. That amounts to an extra 11.5 billion won spent each day. However, out of that 1 trillion won increase over three months , only 6 billion won is due to consuming more fuel. The rest is almost entirely due to rising oil prices and exchange rates. The reason oil prices surged is because of the Middle East. After the US and Israel air-raided Iran on February 28, the Middle East war began, and the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which Middle Eastern oil is shipped worldwide, was effectively blocked. Singapore jet fuel , the benchmark for Asian aviation fuel prices, hit an all-time high in daily trading on March 4. It was nearly 2,000 won per liter. Fuel's share of Korean Air's total costs jumped from 27% between April and June of last year to 42%for the same three months this year. Japanese airlines are in the same situation. All Nippon Airways (ANA) also saw its aviation fuel costs nearly double from April to June this year, and the company's total operating profit dropped by 43.5%. In Japan, the government is even providing subsidies to lower fuel surcharges on tickets purchased from summer through the end of the year. Returning to the A380, while the price hike is a major factor, this route consumes more fuel on the way back. Those who have been to New York will know. The return trip is longer than the trip there. The flight time from Incheon to New York is about 13 hours, while from New York to Incheon it is about 15 hours. Why does it take longer to return? There are two reasons. First, the wind. At an altitude of about 9km where planes fly, a strong wind blows from west to east. It is strongest in winter. Flights to New York have a tailwind, while return flights head into a headwind. Second, the return journey is longer. Since March 2022, following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, Korean Air has been rerouting flights from the U.S. to Seoul via Alaska and the Pacific to avoid Russian airspace. At the time, South Korea was not on Russia's list of countries banned from their airspace. Korean Air decided to avoid it on their own. Because of this, the return flight takes up to 1 hour and 40 minutes longer, and it remains that way this year. As a result, the same aircraft uses about 18%more fuel on the return trip. If passengers paid for all that extra fuel through the fuel surcharge on their tickets, Korean Air wouldn't have anything to worry about. For a one-way ticket to New York booked in November, a fuel surcharge of 408,100 won is added. From a passenger's perspective, it’s easy to feel like you're covering all the increased fuel costs through that surcharge. But this surcharge is not meant to cover the entire cost of fuel. The surcharge is applied only when fuel prices exceed a certain level, and it doesn't rise further once it hits a set cap. When the surcharge for May was set, fuel prices had already exceeded that cap. How much the surcharge actually offsets rising fuel costs is clearer when looking at Korean Air’s overall figures rather than a single plane. From April to June this year, Korean Air's passenger revenue increased by 451.4 billion won compared to a year ago. Even if we count all of that increase as fuel surcharges, it only covers about 43%of the roughly 1 trillion won increase in fuel costs during the same period. Korean Air absorbed more than half of the increased fuel costs, and its operating profit for that quarter fell by 34%. There are also months when the collected surcharge is higher than the actual fuel cost. This is because surcharges are predetermined based on fuel prices from one or two months prior. In months where fuel prices drop , the surcharge collected is higher than the actual cost. In June, the actual fuel price was about 25%lower than the price used when the surcharge was set. Passengers flew during the summer on tickets bought with these high surcharges. According to preliminary figures, Korean Air's operating profit from July to September was 421.2 billion won. That is a 12% increase compared to a year ago. I believe it is only half-true to see the fuel surcharge as the passenger paying for all the increased fuel costs. When fuel prices rise, it doesn't even cover half, and when they fall, more is collected than is actually needed. Besides fuel, there are other costs Korean Air pays for each round trip. Four major categories stand out. First, airport usage fees and overflight fees for passing through foreign airspace. Landing at Incheon Airport costs 8,600 won per ton of the aircraft's weight. An A380, which has a maximum takeoff weight of 575 tons, costs about 5 million won to land once. New York airport charges for takeoffs, which is about 30,000 won per ton of the aircraft's weight. For an A380, that comes to about 17 million won. Adding the fees for passing through Japanese and Canadian airspace, the round-trip total is about 30 million won. Second is personnel. For a flight exceeding 13 hours from gate to gate, two captains and two first officers must be on board . The pilots take turns flying the plane. Including the cabin crew's wages and hotel costs for their stay in New York, it costs about 110 million won per round trip. Third is maintenance. The A380 with four engines costs more to maintain than planes with two. It costs about 40 million won per round trip, or roughly 1.3 million won for every hour the plane is in the air. Fourth is the cost of the aircraft. Korean Air spends about 2 trillion won annually on aircraft depreciation and interest for leased planes. If you divide this by the total flight hours of Korean Air's fleet, it comes to about 2.8 million won per hour. Since this round trip is about 30 hours, that adds about 80 million won to the cost. So, how much is the total expenditure? Along with the 500 million won for fuel and the four previous costs, an additional 130 million won is added for expenses like 20,000 won per passenger for in-flight meals, ground handling, ticket commissions, and headquarters overhead. All together, the cost per round trip is about 890 million won. With 1.01 billion won in revenue and 890 million won in expenses, the profit is just over 100 million won. That’s about 12%of the revenue. However, fuel prices soared in the spring, dropped in the summer, and are rising again in the autumn. The improved performance in the summer was only during those few months when fuel prices temporarily dipped. If fuel is purchased at September and October rates, the fuel cost alone increases by 150 million won, turning the flight from a profit into a loss of tens of millions of won. Then what if we flew a smaller plane? Korean Air sends two flights to New York daily, and the other one is a Boeing 777. It has 277 seats, which is 130 fewer than the A380. Instead, the 777 carries more than twice the cargo in its hold. It averages 14.9 tons per flight to New York, while the A380 carries 6.4 tons. It is 6.4 tons. It consumes about 213 tons of fuel for a round trip, which is 130 tons less than the A380. Calculated the same way, the profit for one 777 round trip is about 80 million won. With 130 fewer seats, the revenue from passengers is over 300 million won less . At the first half's fuel prices, both aircraft net around 100 million won. The difference emerges when fuel prices rise. At September and October market prices, the A 380's fuel costs rise by 150 million won, and the 777's by 90 million won. Both turn to a loss, but the A 380's loss is significantly larger. In 2019, Air France decided to retire the A380, citing it consumes 20 to 25%more fuel per seat than new long-haul aircraft. Then, when COVID-19 hit, they retired all remaining A380s. Korean Air, however, delayed their retirement. They maintain they will only retire them once replacements arrive, but no date has been set. The first delivery of the new large Boeing aircraft ordered by Korean Air has been pushed back to 2027. In my view, the longer the A380 stays in service, the greater Korean Air's losses will be when fuel prices rise. If fuel prices rise to autumn levels, the A 380's fuel costs increase by 60 million won more than the 777's. Whether they fly the A380 or the 777, the profit from this route doesn't belong solely to Korean Air. Even that 100 million won or so profit from the first half's fuel prices is shared. If you buy this A380 ticket on Delta Air Lines 'website, the flight number appears as DL7841, bearing the Delta name. It is the same plane and the same seat. Yet, Delta does not operate a single scheduled flight between Incheon and New York. They are simply attaching their own flight number to Korean Air's plane. The two companies have been operating routes between the US and Asia as if they were one since May 2018. The method approved by the US Department of Transportation is as follows. No matter whose plane flies, they pool the revenue from both companies and split the profit and loss based on each company's contribution. This flight to and from Incheon and New York is also included. The specific split ratio has not been disclosed. Delta is also the third-largest shareholder, holding about a 15%stake in Hanjin Kal, Korean Air's parent company. They are a partner sharing profits with Korean Air while also being a major shareholder in the company that owns Korean Air. If the profit is only about 100 million won, couldn't they just raise ticket prices? Korean Air also knows that this route does not yield a large profit. In the report submitted for the merger with Asiana, they explicitly described Korea-US routes as having low profitability. The Korea Fair Trade Commission reached the same conclusion based on 2019 figures. However, until recently, Korean Air could not raise New York ticket prices at will. This was due to the conditions imposed during the merger with Asiana Airlines. In 2019, the number of passengers traveling directly between Seoul and New York was 493,000, with Korean Air carrying 62%and Asiana 36%. If the two companies merge, one single company will end up carrying almost all the passengers on this route. The Fair Trade Commission analyzed that this could raise New York ticket prices by about 22.5%, so they attached conditions while approving the merger. Korean Air completed the process of making Asiana a subsidiary in December 2024, and the two companies will fully integrate this December. There were two conditions. For each seat class, the three-month average ticket price cannot exceed the 2019 price adjusted for inflation. The number of seats cannot be reduced to below 90%of the 2019 levels. I believe there was a loophole in how these ticket price restrictions were designed. The condition only required meeting a three-month average. If they sold many cheap tickets on days with few passengers, the average would drop, so they could charge high prices on busy days and still meet the average . I am not saying any company did this, but that the conditions were structured that way. These conditions were set to be lifted once Korean Air finished transferring some of its airport slots —the takeoff and landing times—to other airlines in New York. Korean Air itself admitted while reflecting on last year that their U.S. route growth was somewhat slower because they could not raise ticket prices at will. United Airlines and the domestic carrier Air Premia were chosen to take over those slots, and the government explains that the transfer for the New York route was completed this year. According to the government's explanation, this means the price cap on the New York route is now set to be lifted. United has been operating daily flights between Incheon and Newark Airport since September 5th. The total number of flights from Incheon to New York across all airlines has increased from 35 to 42 per week. Now, let's wrap things up. Back to the question I asked at the beginning. How much profit does Korean Air make when an A380 makes a round trip from Incheon to New York? Passenger revenue is about 950 million won, and cargo revenue is about 60 million won, totaling around 1.01 billion won in income. Out of that , fuel costs are about 500 million won, and when adding airport fees, labor, maintenance, and aircraft costs, expenses reach about 890 million won. Based on first-half fuel prices, the remaining profit is just over 100 million won. If you buy fuel at September and October prices, you actually lose money, and the remaining profit is also shared with Delta. This is also why the profit dropped by 34% from April to June, as I mentioned earlier. Because fuel prices doubled immediately, while the surcharges lagged behind and didn't even cover half of that increase. I don’t believe the high cost of a New York ticket should be blamed solely on Korean Air's greed. Half of the revenue goes toward fuel costs, and those prices are set in the Middle East, not by Korean Air. What do you think? Next time you book a ticket to New York, I hope you take a look at the fuel surcharge on the payment screen. Also, consider that the passenger sitting next to you might be heading to Manila, not getting off in Incheon, and may have paid less than you.

The Eiffel Tower in Paris, the Colosseum in Rome, the blue roofs of Santorini, the night alleys of Prague, the red train crossing the Alps—the list we keep for that once-in-a-lifetime trip before we die. Everyone has one, don't you? Most of those items are concentrated in Europe. But behind those postcard-like scenes, these countries are now collapsing one by one. In Germany, factories that have been running for over 100 years have shut down. France has seen its sovereign credit rating downgraded due to debt. The UK has left the European Union entirely. These are the countries we used to call "advanced nations." But that term "advanced nation" has become a poor fit for Europe today. To properly tell this story, we must look at how the European Union came to be. The beginning was war. Europe went through two world wars within a single generation. France and Germany were always at the center of it all. When the war ended, cities were in ruins, and everyone shared the same concern. " It's going to happen again." In May 1950, French Foreign Minister Robert Schuman read a proposal before reporters. "Let’s manage the coal and steel of France and Germany under a single institution." Why, specifically, coal and steel? Because those are the materials used to make cannons and tanks. If you put weapon materials in one warehouse and share the keys, no single country can secretly build up its military. It wasn't a promise to trust each other, but a structure where betrayal was impossible. The following year, six countries signed the pact. France, West Germany, Italy, along with Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg. This group, which started with coal and steel, grew larger and larger. They abolished taxes on buying and selling goods, and later, even removed border checks. In 1993, they adopted the name "European Union," and in 1999, many countries began using the same currency, the Euro. Countries that were once enemies had effectively become one body. There was one country that joined this group unusually late and with only one foot in the door. That is the UK. The UK applied to join twice in the 1960s, but each time, French presidents blocked them. The reason was that the UK was closer to the United States than to Europe. They finally joined in 1973, but even after joining, they held onto the Pound instead of the Euro until the end. And in 2016, that same UK opened the door and walked out. The remaining countries aren't what they used to be, either. As their own domestic economies become desperate, they can no longer look after their neighbors. They became one body to prevent war, but now they prioritize their own survival first. It becomes even clearer when you look at the numbers. In 2008, the economic size of the 27 EU countries was 16 trillion dollars. The United States was at 14 trillion dollars. Europe was larger. 15 years later, the European economy has grown by 13%. The U.S. has grown by 87% . Now, the European economy has shrunk to half the size of the U.S. economy. While the weak euro is partly to blame, the gap has widened even when excluding that factor. During that time, there was no war in Europe, nor did people stop working. They just kept doing what they were doing. But doing what they were doing was the problem. The method that made Europe rich had a condition attached from the start. That condition has now reached its expiration date. Today, we will trace three things. How did Europe get so wealthy in the first place? Why has that money flow dried up now? And why did the UK walk away, and how is it doing ten years later? Let’s start with the first question. Let’s unfold a world map from 1920. Each territory is painted, but one color is unusually dominant. One out of every four segments is the same color. 24%of the Earth’s land was territory of one country. Back then, one in four people on Earth lived under rules set in London. If you just say "that's impressive" and move on, you’ll miss the point. You shouldn't look at the map; you should look at the ledger. Let’s go to the Netherlands. Compared to the UK, it’s a tiny country. For 45 years in the mid-19th century, 823 million guilders flowed from Java to the national treasury. To give you an idea, that was about one-third of the Dutch national budget at the time. One out of every three bills entering the government's wallet came from across the globe. Colonies weren’t just adventure stories; they were the biggest line item in the budget. The system that ran this way was shattered by two major wars. Cities collapsed, and colonies broke away one by one. Then, how did Europe get back on its feet? From 1948, $ 13.3 billion flowed in from across the Atlantic over four years. 16 countries shared the aid. The UK received the most, and West Germany, who lost the war, was fourth. Whether they won or lost, the recovery bill was paid by the same source. Even after the buildings were rebuilt, support from across the sea remained. It’s NATO, a military club created to defend each other if one of us is attacked. As of 2016, the U.S. alone paid 72%of its membership fees. Europe made a promise, too. In 2014, they agreed to spend 2%of their respective GDP on defense. That year, only three countries kept that promise. After everyone nodded in agreement, only three. It wasn't until last year that all 32 countries met the target. During the same period, Europe was building something else. Pensions, healthcare, and unemployment benefits. Most of the systems you associate with Europe were designed between the 1960s and 80s. There are two things that weren't included in those plans. They didn't know people would live this long . And they didn't know people would have so few children. Whenever someone asked where the money came from while Europe was prosperous, the finger always pointed outside of Europe. At first, it was other people's land on the map, and later, it was the wallets of people across the sea. Europe had been living with a straw stuck into the outside world. There is one question to ask. Is that straw still inserted today ? Next to the Rhine River in Germany lies the headquarters of the world's largest chemical company. Materials from this factory become paint, diapers , and car seats, but in 2022, a strange line appeared in the company's ledger. The extra money spent just on energy costs in a single year was 3.2 billion euros, even though they hadn't built new factories or produced more goods. They produced the same amount as the year before, but the numbers on the bill had ballooned. In February of the following year, the company announced it would cut 2,600 jobs. The way Germany became wealthy was actually quite simple. Receive cheap energy through pipes, use it to make products, and sell them to wealthy customers. That was everything. One pipe, one customer. It was a country that ran on two straws. Let's look at the pipe side first. In 2021, 55%of the natural gas Germany used came from Russia. That's over half. Half of the bituminous coal used in power plants was also Russian. At the bottom of the Baltic Sea lay a pipeline called Nord Stream. It was a gas pipeline stretching under the sea from Russia to Germany, and gas had been flowing through it non-stop since 2011. To the German people, it was just like a kitchen faucet. Something that flows when you turn it on, something you never had to wonder if it would work or not. In the summer of 2022, that faucet stiffened for the first time. The supply dropped to 40%. A short while later, it became 20%. And that autumn. Nothing came out. There was one more pipe laid under the same sea. Nord Stream 2. Construction was completely finished in the autumn of 2021. Gas has never flowed through this pipe even once. Not a single drop. A pipe that was fully laid but never used . It ruptured under the sea the following autumn. Meanwhile, a different switch was turned off on land . In the spring of 2023, the last remaining nuclear power plants in Germany shut down all at once. The nuclear plants were shut down, and the gas was already cut off. Now, let's look at the other straw: the customer. For a very long time, German cars were the best-selling cars in China. It was hard to walk a single block on a major street in Shanghai without seeing a Volkswagen logo. They never lost that number one spot for 15 years. In 2023, they surrendered that position to a Chinese company. The customer started making the cars themselves. It was as if they opened the same shop in the next alley at a cheaper price. Your ears notice the assembly line stopping before your eyes do. When the conveyor moves, the entire factory hums with a low vibration. When that sound fades, you suddenly hear footsteps. The sound of someone putting down tools, the sound of shutters coming down—that hum was once the pride of many German factories. Last year, 23,900 companies closed their doors in Germany. That is the highest number in 20 years. This picture isn't entirely unfamiliar to us . In the fall of 2021, our own cargo trucks nearly came to a halt because we lacked a single canister of urea solution. For Germany, it wasn't just a single canister, but the entire country . When one pipe was shut and one customer turned away, the economy once touted as the strongest in Europe collapsed on the spot. All that happened was that what we used to get cheaply started costing more. Was Germany the only one to stake its entire livelihood on things coming from the outside? In summer, the alleys of Lisbon are filled with sound first. The sound of suitcase wheels rolling over cobblestones never stops from morning until night. This was the gate Southern Europe had leaned on for so long. A gate where people come from outside, open their wallets, and the neighborhood makes a living on that money. Through that gate, outside money also comes in to buy up rooms. Renting to a guest for a single night pays better than a month's rent. The homes in the alleyways turned into guest rooms one by one, and those who had lived there for 30 years were pushed out 40 minutes away by bus. The alleys that were full of suitcases in the summer turn into streets lined only with dark, empty houses when winter comes. Northern and Western countries kept their gates open for labor instead of just money. The people boarding farm trucks at 4 AM or staying up all night in nursing homes—most of those roles were filled by people from the East. If that gate closes, who will board the 4 AM trucks? The real reason they had to keep accepting outsiders was inside Europe itself. By 2024, the number of children a woman in Europe gives birth to in her lifetime dropped to just over one. This is the lowest level since they began keeping statistics. To keep the population from shrinking, it needs to be over two. Right now in Europe, three working people are barely supporting one elderly person. By 2050, those three will be fewer than two. However, Europe's pension charts are still based on that original design. It’s a chart drawn back when three people could easily support one. France is the country that stepped up to reform that chart. They announced they would raise the pension eligibility age from 62 to 64 in 2023. They bypassed the parliamentary vote and pushed it through using a constitutional clause. From that day on, trains stopped, schools closed, and trash piled up on the streets. The country came to a halt over the single idea of working two years longer. Europe didn't keep its doors open out of the kindness of its heart. Because the moment they closed them, they would have to cover that bill themselves. Yet, there was one country that closed that door with its own hands. If you haven't subscribed yet, please take a moment to do so. In June, the British people lined up in front of polling stations. The voter turnout that day exceeded 72%. Not since 1992 had so many people in the UK come out to cast their votes. They didn't vote out of ignorance; it was the result of a record turnout and a fierce debate. Out of 100 people, 52 voted to leave, and 48 voted to remain. This is Brexit. It’s a term created by combining Britain and exit. A few months before that, a red bus was roaming the streets of Britain. It had large letters written on its side. We are sending 350 million pounds a week to Europe. Let’s spend that money on our hospitals instead. The UK Statistics Authority officially spoke out once in April and again in May that year. They stated that the number was misleading. Once you subtract the amount returned, the actual money leaving was much less. It was about 250 million pounds per week. But the bus kept on running. The text wasn't erased , and people saw it every morning on their commute. Just a few days before the vote, the UK Treasury released a report. It warned that leaving would cause a shock comparable to the 2008 financial crisis. The British people read it, heard it, and then made their choice. The person who decided to hold the referendum was then-Prime Minister David Cameron. He believed he would win . He simply never calculated what would happen if he lost. The morning after the results were counted, he announced he would step down before the public. Now, let’s start counting the time from here. The door didn't actually close until the first day of 2021. It took four and a half years after the vote to finally leave. A year later, a truck stopped at a ferry terminal. Until yesterday, it could have just boarded the ship. Now, you need paperwork. If there are 20 types of goods, you must list all 20 and get them stamped. If even one line is out of place, the truck spends the night at the port. That year, UK exports fell by 27 billion pounds. Looking only at trade with Europe, it dropped by over 13%. Three years later, this is the most painful part. There were about 120,000 British companies selling goods to Europe. That number has become 100,000. 16,400 companies gave up exporting altogether. However, those that disappeared were all small companies with fewer than 10 employees. Exports from large companies did not decrease. The reason is disheartening. Large companies have staff dedicated to handling that paperwork all day. Small companies have the owner. They make goods by day and wrestle with paperwork by night, until one day they just fold. Companies with someone to handle a piece of paper versus companies without . That single sheet of paper is what drew the line. Border inspections also made their way into our shopping baskets. Grocery prices rose by 6%. That averages out to 250 pounds per household each year. And that spring, no one came to the strawberry fields in southern England. Every year at this time, buses would arrive carrying workers from Eastern Europe. Those buses did not come. Strawberries don't wait for people. They ripened, rotted, and returned to the soil. Now, at this point, you might be thinking this. "At least they achieved what they originally intended." Since stopping the influx of people was the biggest reason. "At least that one thing must have worked." Up until June 2023. In one year, the net increase in people in the UK was 906,000. This is the number of people who arrived minus those who left. It was the highest in British history. What decreased were the people coming from Europe. But people from outside Europe filled that void even more. We closed one door, only for another door to open even wider. I’m not here to judge whether it was right or wrong. It just means what happened was the exact opposite of what was intended. The sea was the same. British fishermen had long been frustrated by European boats casting nets in their waters. "Take back our seas." This was the symbol of the exit. They did take them back, in a sense. The increased fishing quota amounts to 12.4%in value. It is 12.4%. The problem is where that went. Mackerel and herring caught by large ships increased by 60%, but catches for small boats decreased. It was the small boats that were loudest at the ports. But the big ships took the profits. Those who were promised the gains were different from those who actually benefited. And in 2025, the UK granted European fishing vessels the right to operate in UK waters for 13 more years, until 2038. Those very seas they said they would take back. Ten years on, we asked the British people again this year. 57%said leaving was the wrong decision, while 30%said it was the right one; public opinion has flipped. But then, it flips once more. When we asked only those who voted to leave at the time, 64%said they still believe it was the right choice. Even after seeing the ten-year bill, only 23 %changed their minds. In other countries, the straw was pulled out on its own. Britain pulled it out with its own hands. That is why Britain is the only country that has shown us for ten years what happens when you pull it out . Once the straw was removed, what lay beneath began to be revealed. And that wasn't just Britain's story. There is a table that ranks the world's most valuable companies from the top. It is a ranking based on how much it would cost to buy the companies in their entirety. I scan down from the very top with my finger. I see an American company, then another, with a few Asian names mixed in between. You have to scroll down quite a bit to find a European name. And the ones who have said this most painfully are the Europeans themselves. In 2024, a former president of the European Central Bank released a report diagnosing the entire European economy. It is a score Europe gave to itself. There is a sentence in it that says this. Over the past 50 years, no company in Europe has exceeded a value of one trillion euros. Not a single one. It is zero. During the same period in the US, six companies emerged that were worth ten times that—over a trillion euros. Think of the phone in your hand, the search bar on it, the payment button you pressed last night, and name the companies behind them one by one. How many European names come up? Even promising companies born in Europe didn't stay long. Whenever they were poised for real growth, 30%packed up and moved their headquarters, mostly to the US. Why don't they grow? The reasons pointed out by the report are surprisingly mundane. One is electricity rates. Electricity for European factories costs two to three times more than in the US. Before they even make a single product, they are already at a disadvantage the moment they turn on the factory lights. The second is capital access. For small companies to grow, there must be channels to inject large funds, but in Europe, those channels are fragmented by country. It is like having 27 different wallets in one club. So, Europe continues to rely on a few industries it has always been good at. A picture of an entire nation resting on a single sector. Doesn't that sound somewhat familiar? And as their strength fades, the bonds that held them together also loosen. In the fall of 2024, the European Union decided to impose additional tariffs on electric vehicles coming from China. The idea was to protect European car factories. However, the vote did not yield a unified voice. Spain did not raise its hand. There was a reason for it. Just before that, China had threatened to take issue with Spanish pork and brandy . Faced with a vote to protect all of Europe, Spain chose to protect its own farmers first. The UK has already left, and the remaining countries have started looking out for their own interests from within. So, is Europe finished? Early this year, the statistical office released a figure. The unemployment rate in countries using the Euro was 6.1%, the lowest number of jobless people since the Euro was first created. This is not a number that could come from countries with truly collapsed economies. The same goes for life expectancy. On average, Europeans live more than two and a half years longer than Americans. They earn less money, but they live longer. But this is where it really gets interesting. There are countries that were once treated as Europe's problems. Accused of just racking up debt and not working. The southern countries that were once called the burden of Europe. Those are the countries running the fastest in Europe right now. In last year's economic report card for developed nations, the country at the very top was Portugal. Followed by Ireland. And Greece was in sixth place. Spain grew at more than double the Eurozone average. These are names that couldn't even hold their heads up at European meetings just over a decade ago. How did Spain do it? Tourists flooded back in, electricity prices became some of the lowest in Europe, and labor shortages were filled by those coming in to work. They started getting fair value for what they had. Germany didn't just sit still either. The German constitution has a "no more debt" rule embedded in it. It is a provision that puts a cap on the new debt the government can incur each year , and for over 15 years, it was a sacred ground no one could touch. In March 2025, the German parliament amended it. They decided to exclude money spent on defense and infrastructure from that rule. When a country that has been holding firm starts changing its rules, it's not the sound of collapsing, but the sound of changing direction. The time Europe sat at the center of the world is, at most, about 400 years. Humans started sowing seeds in the ground 10,000 years ago. In that long story, 400 years is just one chapter, albeit a very thick one. What is ending now might not be Europe. It is that chapter where Europe relied on the outside world to fund its livelihood that is coming to an end. At the Dover port, trucks are lined up again today. It is a sea they used to cross without a single piece of paperwork. Now, they sit with their engines off, waiting for a single stamp . No one knows yet whether that line will get shorter or longer. History Instinct is a channel that looks at who pays the price and when, rather than who got rich first. Because there is always a longer story wherever the bill arrives.

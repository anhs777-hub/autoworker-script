There is a port city in southern India called Cochin. It is home to India's largest shipyard, which is a state-owned company. Last July, Korea's top shipbuilder, HD Hyundai, joined hands with this company. The plan was to build a factory together next to the shipyard to manufacture ship hull sections, with an investment of $ 449 million, or about 620 billion won. However, this past August, HD Hyundai scrapped this joint venture plan. Instead, they chose a port called Tuticorin in the southern Indian state of Tamil Nadu. They stated that they would build their own shipyard entirely from scratch, without borrowing anyone else's name. They turned away from the easier path of a joint venture toward the much more expensive path of building it alone. But there are some aspects of this choice that seem contradictory. One is the fact that India is a country that can barely build ships. The total amount of ships built by all of India in one year is 40,000 tons. Here, the ton is a unit measuring ship volume, not weight, and the shipbuilding industry uses this gross tonnage to count how many ships were built in a year. At 40,000 tons, its share of the global market is 0.06% , ranking 16th. Yet, the planned annual capacity of the single shipyard HD Hyundai intends to build there is 2.5 million tons. In terms of the same unit , this means they are setting up a factory 62 times the size of what the entire country produces in a year. The other issue is money. Usually, when people think of building factories overseas, they assume it's because labor costs are cheap. However, building a ship in India is 25%to 35% more expensive than building it in Korea, China, or Japan. This means they aren't going there because it's cheap. So why did they scrap the joint venture , why that expensive country, and what will happen in Ulsan? Let's look at it in order. First, let's look at exactly what the scrapped contract was. Large ships are not built as a single piece. Ships are divided into dozens of large box-like sections, built separately, and then brought to a dock to be assembled like Lego bricks. These box sections are called blocks. Even a 300- meter-long container ship requires over 100 blocks, and a single large block can be several stories high, like an apartment building. The factory HD Hyundai was going to build with Cochin Shipyard was exactly for making these blocks. The plan was to set up facilities on about 80 acres of land near Cochin Shipyard to produce 120,000 tons of blocks per year. 80 acres is roughly the size of 40 football fields. A block factory is equivalent to a parts factory in the shipbuilding industry. The blueprints are drawn elsewhere, and the completion of the ship is also done elsewhere. The block factory is a place that receives drawings and churns out the pieces to be shipped. So, HD Hyundai's initial plan was to dip its toes into India without actually engaging in the shipbuilding itself. It was a path that required less capital and carried lower risk. And there was one more structural limitation to that path. The fact that the partner was a state-owned company. If a joint venture is established, you have to share equity and decision-making, and you must agree on what kind of ships to build, when, and how. In shipbuilding, delivery schedules and process sequences equal money, but the structure would mean having only half the control. The company did not officially disclose the reason for abandoning the joint venture . However, looking at the path chosen afterward, the direction is clear. They moved away from being a mere parts supplier to taking the lead in the entire process from design to dock. There is one thing here that is easy to misunderstand. Abandoning the joint venture doesn't mean the two companies have parted ways. HD Hyundai is still providing basic ship designs and technical support to Cochin Shipyard. They are also supporting design and procurement, boosting productivity, and training staff for a project where Cochin Shipyard is building six 1,700 TEU container ships for the French shipping company CMA CGM. 1,700 TEU means a ship that carries 1,700 six-meter containers; it is not a large ship but a mid-sized one for shorter routes. The only thing they dropped was the plan to co-invest in building a factory. Then, what kind of place is the land they chose instead? Thoothukudi is a port city located at the southern tip of India, facing Sri Lanka. The site HD Hyundai secured there is approximately 8.3 million square meters. And they have secured a waterfront length of 2.4 km, where ships can be floated and berthed. In a shipyard, this waterfront length determines how many docks can be placed and how many completed ships can be moored for finishing work. No matter how large the land is, if the waterfront is short, it cannot become a large shipyard. You might not grasp the scale of this 8.3 million square meters , so there is a point of comparison. The yard area of HD Hyundai Heavy Industries 'Ulsan shipyard is 7.38 million square meters. The land secured in India is actually larger than the Ulsan Mipo shipyard, which produces the most ships in the world. However, the scale of the planned facilities is much smaller than in Ulsan. The planned annual production capacity of Thoothukudi is 2.5 million tons, while the Ulsan shipyard is 12.46 million tons. It is starting at about one-fifth the level of Ulsan. Securing more land than the main base while starting with one-fifth the capacity means they have secured room for future expansion. The number of people to be hired here is estimated at about 15,000 for direct employment alone. And they aren't stopping with just one shipyard. The plan is to build a shipbuilding industrial complex right next door, larger than the shipyard site itself, to gather steel, equipment, and crane manufacturing all in one place. Since a single ship requires tens of thousands of parts, importing them every time hurts both costs and delivery schedules , so they intend to set up a supply chain right next to it. The investment amount has not been finalized yet. Numbers like an initial investment of about $ 2 billion and a total project cost of up to $ 4 billion are being mentioned, though some reports based on state government announcements suggest a smaller scale. This means it is still open whether it will cost 3 trillion or 6 trillion won in our currency. On top of this, infrastructure construction costs for building breakwaters for the industrial complex and dredging the seabed are added separately. HD Hyundai and the Tamil Nadu state government signed an exclusive MOU for the shipyard construction last December, and after that, they moved into detailed design and site inspections. At this point, we need to take a look at the shipbuilding ledger of India. What exactly is the state of the industry that has the world's top shipbuilder envisioning such a scale? I mentioned earlier that India builds 40,000 tons a year, but in the same year, China, Japan, and South Korea, these three countries, accounted for over 90%of the ships built globally. This means India doesn't even qualify as a shipbuilding nation. But there is a point here that is commonly misunderstood. It’s not because India lacks shipyards. There are 38 shipyards in India. Among them, nine are state-owned and 29 are private, but 75% of the actual building capacity is concentrated in the nine state-owned ones. Looking at the facilities alone is even more surprising. The private shipyard L&T Kattupalli has facilities capable of building 300,000 deadweight tonnage vessels and large LNG carriers, and the Reliance-affiliated shipyard has facilities capable of up to 400,000 deadweight tons. Here, deadweight tonnage, unlike the volume unit mentioned earlier, refers to the weight of the cargo a ship can carry. It means that facilities reaching world-class levels in terms of docks and cranes are already on Indian soil. Yet the entire country builds only 40,000 tons worth per year. It’s not that they don't have the vessels (capacity), it's that the vessels aren't being utilized. On the other hand, the state-owned side, which holds three-quarters of the building capacity, is small in terms of its own weight class. The merchant ships that Indian state-owned shipyards could build were limited to a maximum of 110,000 deadweight tons and a length of up to 250 meters. Modern large container ships are nearly 400 meters long, and LNG carriers transporting liquefied natural gas are ships that must maintain cargo holds at minus 163 degrees. The state-owned shipyards couldn't even enter that weight class. Currently, Cochin Shipyard is working to build the capacity to construct vessels up to 300,000 tons. One of those nine shipyards mentioned earlier is Cochin Shipyard. Founded in 1972, it completed its first yard in 1982 with technology from Mitsubishi Heavy Industries of Japan. In a sense, Japanese technology was the starting point for India’s shipbuilding industry. Today, Cochin Shipyard is one of the few shipyards in the world—and the only one in India—capable of building aircraft carriers. They build cargo ships and tankers, as well as vessels for the Navy and Coast Guard. To summarize, it looks like this. India desperately needs ships, but while they have the facilities, they lack the volume and the ecosystem. If someone can fill that gap, it’s essentially an open market for the taking. Now, let’s move on to the cost issue. Even if it's an open market, one must be able to build cheaply, but I mentioned that India is the opposite. Building a ship in India costs 25%to 35%more than in Korea, China, or Japan. It is not because labor costs are high. Most engines and equipment must be imported, long-term financing for years of construction is hard to secure locally, and low volume prevents economies of scale. Shipbuilding is an industry where costs rise if related ancillary industries are not nearby. Then why go there? The answer lies not in those who build the ships, but in those who use them. India moves 95%of its trade volume by sea. Even in terms of value, 70%travels across the ocean. However, those ships are not Indian ships. About 94%of India’s overseas cargo is carried by foreign-flagged vessels. Even for domestic coastal shipping between Indian ports, 65%to 70%are foreign vessels. As of fiscal year 2023 , the share of overseas cargo handled by Indian-flagged ships was only about 5%. You have to pay to charter a ship. The amount of foreign currency leaving India annually in charter fees is $ 75 to $ 80 billion. That is estimated to be around 110 trillion won in our currency. It is a structure where a significant portion of the money earned from exports leaves again as charter fees for foreign vessels. The Modi government has declared it will change this. They have set a goal to become a top 10 global shipbuilder by 2030 and a top 5 by 2047, the centennial of India's independence, with the target of having at least 20%of Indian exports carried by Indian ships. What matters here is not the target number, but the method. India has begun to attach financial incentives and benefits to ships built domestically. It means they are changing the rules so that you must build in India to be eligible for Indian shipping contracts. That changes the calculations entirely. Even if construction costs are 30%higher, if there is a volume of orders you can only get by being inside that market, the high cost is not a loss but an entry fee. Because no matter how cheaply you build outside, you cannot sell to that market. The story of this entry fee becomes clearer when you see how concrete the plan laid out by the Indian government is. First, there is the money. The Indian Ministry of Ports , Shipping and Waterways is creating a maritime development fund worth 2.5 trillion rupees, or about 42 trillion Korean won. It is formed by pooling surplus government funds and investments from non-banking financial companies, and is used to build or expand shipyards. This means government money is already laid out for those intending to build shipyards. Next are the subsidies. The shipbuilding financial support scheme has been expanded and extended in the 2025 budget. The government covers 20%of the construction cost for general ships, 25 %for tankers and container ships carrying oil, gas, or chemicals, and 30 %for eco-friendly ships using methanol, ammonia, or hydrogen. It’s like the government is discounting about one-third of the price of a ship with taxpayer money. Let’s place these numbers side-by-side with the figures mentioned earlier. I mentioned that India's construction costs are 25%to 35 %higher than other countries, and the subsidy rates almost overlap with that gap. This is no coincidence; it is closer to a figure designed to bridge that very difference. It is not a way to make an expensive country cheap, but a method where the government pays for the excess cost. This is not the end. If you scrap an old ship, they return 40%of the scrap value in the form of a letter of credit. It is a mechanism to induce people to dismantle old ships in India and use that money to build new ones in India. They are investing an additional 702 million dollars to modernize shipyard facilities and building maritime industrial complexes in Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, and Odisha. They lay down the money, bridge the cost gap with subsidies, create a circular structure from scrap to new ships, and build clusters to attach parts industries. The approach is similar in tone to the method South Korea used when it built up its shipbuilding industry in the 1970s. If you recall Ulsan back in those days, the similarities become even clearer. Hyundai won an order for a 260,000-ton oil tanker from a Greek shipowner in 1971 before it even had a shipyard, and held the groundbreaking ceremony for the shipyard in Mipo Bay, Ulsan, in 1972. They excavated the ground and built the ship at the same time, delivering the first large oil tanker in 1974. What Korea lacked back then were the facilities and the technology. They learned how to lay a dock and the construction methods for building large ships in sections from abroad. What India lacks right now is exactly the same thing. The difference is that the partner they have come to borrow it from this time is Korea. The stage India has set with funds and subsidies is a mechanism to entice those with the technology to enter. Now, we must look at the Korean side of the ledger. This is where it becomes clear why HD Hyundai is venturing abroad. In the first half of 2026, China received 4.45 million CGT in global ship orders. That is 85%of the total. During the same period, Korea accounted for 500,000 CGT , or 9%. CGT is a unit distinct from volume, representing the total amount of labor required to build a ship. The larger and more complex the ship, the higher the CGT value, even for the same size. By this metric, China took nearly nine times the work volume of Korea. Even in terms of order backlog, China holds 61.2%, Korea 20.4%, and Japan 7.9 %. It’s not as if the Korean shipbuilding industry is sitting idle. The ships Korea secured averaged about 42,000 CGT per vessel, much larger than China’s average of roughly 26,000. It means they chose only the largest, most complex, and most expensive ships. It’s a strategy focused on high-value , high-margin vessels like LNG carriers and large container ships. The strategy was that even if they lag in numbers, they won’t lag in revenue. In fact, domestic shipbuilders’ backlogs exceed 30 million CGT, with over three years of work piled up. The problem is that this strategy’s endurance is weakening. As of the first quarter of 2026, China commanded 70%of the global shipbuilding market. When volume leans heavily to one side, those shipyards gain expertise and lower costs by repeatedly building the same vessels. The other side experiences downtime while trying to fill their docks. Furthermore, Korean shipbuilders must renegotiate prices for heavy steel plates with domestic steelmakers every six months to maintain margins. An even bigger problem than the docks is the workforce. Three years' worth of work is piled up, but there aren't enough hands to build those ships. The shipbuilding industry has been operating with a shortage of over 10,000 workers annually. The number of domestic production workers fell from 87,601 in 2023 to 72,021 in 2025. More than 15,000 people have left in just two years. The strategy of picking only high-end ships works only when Korea is the sole builder capable of making them . If China starts building the same ships for less and the workforce to fill domestic docks continues to shrink , the options narrow. Moves to secure new docks abroad are attempts to broaden those options once again. So, were there no other cards the Korean shipbuilding industry was waiting for? There were. And that card is currently stalled. The U.S. Trade Representative had prepared measures targeting the Chinese shipbuilding industry. This is about charging fees when ships built in Chinese shipyards enter U.S. ports. The plan started at $ 120 per container, rising to $ 250 by 2028, and also included a method based on cargo weight . Heavier fees were to be applied to ships owned or operated by Chinese shipping companies. If this actually takes effect, the calculations for shipping lines will change. Even if they buy ships cheaply from China, the repeated costs at U.S. ports diminish the reason to buy them. Then, those orders go elsewhere. South Korea was pointed out as the country that would benefit the most. However, ahead of the October 2025 implementation, U.S. and Chinese leaders reached an agreement, postponing the measure for one year. During this grace period, no port fees or tariffs will be paid. Whether it resumes, and what it would look like if it does, depends on negotiations between the two countries. The shipbuilding cooperation Korea established with the U.S. follows the same trend. The shipbuilding investment fund, which served as leverage in U.S.- Korea tariff negotiations, is worth $ 150 billion. And there was a company that made a move even before those negotiations. Hanwha purchased the Philadelphia Shipyard, the only one capable of building large ships on the U.S. East Coast, for $ 100 million. They also announced plans to invest an additional $ 5 billion to boost production capacity from the current one to 1.5 ships per year up to 20 ships. Regardless of whether fees at U.S. ports are deferred, companies with docks inside the U.S. can secure U.S. orders. To summarize, it is like this. The penalties imposed on the other party are pushed back by a year whenever the leaders meet. On the other hand, once a dock is built, it remains. Whether in the U.S. or India, Korean shipbuilders setting out to secure their own docks on foreign soil chose to expand their own ground instead of waiting for opportunities created by others. Then, what will happen to our shipyards? This is the part of the story closest to home. The Tuticorin shipyard is still just a plot of land. Therefore, HD Hyundai announced that it would build the initial orders in Korea until the yard is fully operational. The plan is to bring personnel hired locally in India to Korean shipyards for training, then send them to the completed Tuticorin yard to shift to a local production system. The company explained that because India's shipbuilding industry is not yet mature and they often build ships that are not well-suited for domestic production, the structure involves providing technical guidance, designs, and equipment in exchange for corresponding compensation. In short, at this stage, Ulsan's volume is not being transferred to India. On the contrary, Indians are coming to Ulsan. However, the Ulsan shipyard site had already been changing even before that. The proportion of foreign workers in the shipbuilding industry is estimated to have risen from about 5%in 2021 to 25%as of 2026. It has increased fivefold in just five years. In Ulsan’s Dong-gu, home to HD Hyundai Heavy Industries and HD Hyundai Mipo, there are approximately 8,300 foreign workers employed by subcontractors. A local residents ' committee claims the number of foreign residents per unit area is 287, the highest in the nation, and that this could reach 12,500 if hiring limits under regional visas are halved. This is a claim by a resident group and not an official statistic. However, the direction is clear. To solve the labor shortage, Ulsan City is designing a unique regional visa to bring in workers who have been trained overseas in advance. The docks are full, but for years, it has been difficult to find enough domestic workers to fill those jobs. Thus, the plan to train Indian workers presents two faces for Ulsan. In the immediate term, it means more hands to fill the docks, but years later, those same hands will be building ships in India instead. The plan contains both job creation and industrial relocation. And this is not just about India. HD Hyundai entered Vietnam with a repair shipyard in 1999, and is now increasing its production capacity from 10 ships per year to 23 by 2030. In the Philippines, the company invested $ 550 million to lease the Subic Bay shipyard for 10 years, starting operations this January. Now, India is being added to the mix. A production belt spanning Vietnam, the Philippines, and India is currently being formed. Hanwha Ocean has also pursued technical cooperation with India’s Cochin Shipyard, so it is not just one company looking toward India. This isn't just happening at the working level, either. HD Hyundai Chairman Chung Ki-sun met directly with Prime Minister Modi to discuss shipbuilding cooperation. The company’s top decision-maker has effectively aligned himself with India’s maritime goals for 2047. Therefore, we find it difficult to simply call this "overseas expansion." Expansion is taking what we have and selling it abroad, but what is happening now is closer to moving the actual ship-building process itself. What Korea is losing now is not just the number of docks, but the blueprints , the processes, and the people who know them. And that is precisely what is being handed over to India. The remaining question is how long we can hold onto those final advantages and at what cost. Of course, many things remain undecided. Reports on the total project cost vary from an initial $ 2 billion to as much as $ 4 billion, and no date has been set for the groundbreaking. Which of the subsidies promised by the Indian government HD Hyundai will actually receive has not yet been finalized. There is one thing that is certain now. The world's best shipbuilder has secured land in a country that barely builds ships, larger than its own home base.

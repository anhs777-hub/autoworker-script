A single used Gunam, which had been in service for 30 years, was shared with the Philippines free of charge. However , today, the Philippines has become a key market for the Korean defense industry, having contracted for 12 Korean-made warships and FA-50 light attack aircraft. Just what role did this single old Gunam play in that process? The starting point was the ROKS Chungju, an early vessel of the Pohang-class. Commissioned in 1987, the ROKS Chungju guarded South Korea's seas for a remarkable 29 years, until 2016. Then, in 2016, instead of being scrapped, the ROKS Chungju headed to the Philippines. However, this is where the first twist begins. Although it was a warship already decommissioned in Korea, the situation changed completely once it was transferred to the Philippines. At the time, the Philippine Navy evaluated this vessel as the most powerful ship in its fleet. Equipped with anti-surface warfare capabilities, the Pohang-class was able to instantly boost the military power that the Philippine Navy had been lacking. The ROKS Chungju, which went to the Philippines, did not even have a new name. BRP Colado Yap. But even this name was special. Colonado Job was a Philippine military war hero who died fighting in Korea during the Korean War . Bearing the name of a hero who died defending Korea, the Korean Gunam is now set to guard the Philippine seas. It was a scene that demonstrated just how sincere the Philippines is about its connection with Korea. However, the Philippines relied on more than just symbolism for this warship. To take delivery of this vessel, the Philippines sent over 100 crew members to Korea. They underwent ship operation and combat training for a full 13 weeks . It wasn't just a single Gunam that crossed over; it was the people and operational experience—including how to maneuver and fight with the Korean Gunam—that were transferred. But why did they send over 100 crew members to Korea for training when they were simply receiving a single decommissioned Gunam? Furthermore, in 2019, the Philippine Navy assigned a significantly meaningful role to the BRP Colonadoya: the Critical Transition Platform. It was designated as a core transition platform for the upcoming new Korean frigates. At the time, the Philippine Navy lacked experience in operating modern, new-generation frigates. In contrast, two Jose Rizal-class frigates were already under construction in Korea. The Philippine Navy operated the Pohang-class first, gaining practical experience in large-scale operations and crew training. The older Pohang-class frigates effectively became a combat-ready transition platform for the move to the latest Korean-made frigates. Having completed their first mission of protecting South Korea's seas, the retired ships began their second mission in the Philippines. Furthermore, the Philippines 'choice did not stop there. By continuously selecting Korean weapons—ranging from new frigates to early-stage vessels, offshore patrol ships, and even FA-50 light attack aircraft—the Philippines established itself as a key market for the K-defense industry. Naturally, this leads to the thought: Did they start purchasing the new Korean Gunnam simply because they used the donated Gunnam? However, an even more surprising fact was hidden within this. The Philippines ' new frigate project had already begun in 2013. The performance of the Chungju took place in 2014, the contract for the new Korean-made frigate was signed in 2016, and the Chungju actually entered service as a BRP Colado Yaburo in 2019. In other words, they did not select the first Korean-made frigate after actually using the Chungju. If so , what exactly was the true role of the donated ship? South Korea and the Philippines had already opened the way for direct defense contracts between Korean companies and the Philippine Ministry of National Defense as early as 2009. That is to say, the path for defense cooperation was open long before the Chungju arrived. On top of this, experience in operating actual Korean weapons began to accumulate one by one through the donation of naval vessels, crew training, operational drills, maintenance, and logistics. The Chungju was not merely a means to secure the first contract. It was a platform that transformed the already established defense cooperation into practical cooperation involving the direct operation and training of Korean vessels. The true value of the donated ship lay not in opening a new market, but in connecting that existing market more deeply and sustainably. Then why did the Philippines, which was already trading with various countries, repeatedly choose Korea? The Philippines was not a nation that looked only to Korea from the start. It received TC-90 trainer aircraft from Japan that had been used by the Self-Defense Forces, and recently, it has even been pushing for the transfer of decommissioned vessels. In other words, the Philippines has always compared various countries to select the necessary equipment for the military power it requires. Nevertheless, the repetitive selection of Korea continued. Why was this? The first reason was surprisingly simple. When entrusting patrol ships and offshore patrol vessels to Korean shipbuilders again, the Philippine Minister of National Defense personally cited the fact that systems and parts could be shared and maintenance was easy because the vessels were built by the same shipyard as a key advantage. Once they introduced Korean-made vessels, the burden of acquiring subsequent ships began to decrease. However, there was a scene that could not be explained by this alone. Korean-made combat systems, known as the "brain" of a ship, were installed on the Philippine Navy's vessels. What is even more surprising is that not all of those vessels were built in Korea. Korean-made combat systems were also adopted for the performance upgrades of the three Del Pilar-class ships transferred from the United States. Even though the ships were not built in Korea, the "brains" of those vessels were chosen to be Korean-made. Moving beyond the stage of selling Korean warships, Korean systems began to be integrated into the Philippine fleet already in operation. Furthermore, the relationship deepened to a new level. The introduction of circular patrol vessels led to logistical support for existing Korean frigates, and revenue has expanded to include the establishment of maintenance infrastructure to support Korean vessels and the training of local technical personnel. It did not stop at simply building and sending ships; they began repairing them and training personnel within the Philippines. However, this trend did not end with the Navy. The Philippine FA-50 was actually deployed in the Battle of Marawi and experienced Philippine missions as well as joint exercises with Australia. Consequently, the Philippines contracted for 12 more advanced FA-50s. They chose to re-pursue fighter jets that had been used in actual combat. The military accumulated operational and maintenance experience, while the Air Force accumulated combat experience. Although the fields differed, they shared one commonality: direct operational experience was leading to the next choice. The picture is now clear. Korean defense cooperation did not stop at selling a few Gunnam frigates. It was expanding its scope to every corner of the Philippine military, ranging from naval combat systems and maintenance and logistical support to local technical personnel and air power . Let us return to the initial question . Did a single used frigate, 30 years old, really generate all these contracts? No. The Chungju frigate did not secure the Philippines 'first contract for a Korean-made frigate, nor was it the starting point for all these orders. Rather, that is why the role of the Chungju frigate becomes even more distinct. It was the Gunnam frigate that transformed existing defense cooperation into a relationship of direct training and operation aboard the vessel, turning cooperation on paper into actual experience and trust. And upon that experience, new Gunnam frigates, combat systems, maintenance and logistical support, local personnel training, and even FA-50s were added one by one. The Republic of Korea, which once had to build its military power through foreign aid, has now become a nation that provides retired Gunnam frigates in exchange for new ones and fighter jets, while also contributing to combat systems, maintenance, and local personnel development. A used ship that had been in service for 30 years. Its first mission was to protect the seas of the Republic of Korea.

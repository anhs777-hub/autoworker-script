From the cheapest Mugunghwa to the most expensive tourist train, Haerang, shall we follow the seven reasons why foreigners were surprised while riding Korean trains? The first reason is ticket inspection. Let's follow the story of that US family from the beginning. When the family arrived at Seoul Station, the mother ran to the ticket counter first. The line was quite long, but there was a separate line for foreigners, so she said they could get their paper tickets quickly. The family went down to the platform holding the tickets they had worked so hard to get. But they had no use for those tickets. There were no gates to pass through, and no one came to check them on the train either. In other countries, you usually need a ticket to enter the platform. Even just looking at Japan, you have to insert your ticket and pass through a gate to board the train. Many large stations in Europe also have gates set up in front of the platforms to scan tickets. Most Koreans buy tickets through a mobile app and board the train using the ticket on their screen. Since there's no need to go get a paper ticket, even if you arrive at the station a bit late , you can go straight to the platform. Actually, Korea was like that at the beginning, too. There were automatic ticket gates at every KTX station. But in 2009, KORAIL began removing these machines from KTX stations. They completely changed the way tickets are verified. Then who checks the tickets and when? The answer lies in the small handheld device held by the train attendant. Each terminal shows exactly who is traveling to which segment for every seat. So, if someone is sitting in a seat marked as empty, the attendant just needs to go to that spot . A rail traveler from the UK experienced this firsthand. Because they booked late, their family wasn't seated together, so they all moved to empty table seats. Shortly after, an attendant approached and politely asked them to return to their original seats. He called this a lesson he learned on Korean trains. It’s not that they don't check tickets; it's that they are already checking them all. They just don't make every single passenger present their ticket. Of course, there is a price to pay for sneaking on. If you are caught without a ticket, starting October 2025, you must pay the original fare again as a penalty. Avoiding a ticket costs double, faking a discount costs ten times, and forging a ticket costs thirty times the fare. The rules are strict, but the choice was made to trust the passengers the moment they board. That’s why foreigners learn to verify things themselves instead of using ticket gates. Once you sit down, the cabin screen shows train info, so you can immediately tell if you're on the right train. The train number and destination are written on the side, and everyone queues up on the platform accordingly. Blake, a travel YouTuber who arrived at Seoul Station early in the morning, checked the display board and headed straight to the platform. He said he left his accommodation at 6:30 AM, dragging his suitcase, and took the subway to Seoul Station. Instead of repairing old tracks, the Gyeongbu High-Speed Railway laid new tracks exclusively for high-speed trains. They built the path so curves are as gentle as possible, going through mountains with tunnels and crossing rivers and valleys with bridges. The third reason is that the trains are punctual. The American family who came with their children picked up their camera and cheered when it hit the departure time. >> Blake also said the train left exactly on time, and right after departure, he swapped seats with a family who had been separated. The punctuality rate KORAIL claims is 99.8%, which is based on the International Union of Railways standard where anything less than 16 minutes late is considered on time. Then, what if we set a stricter standard of 5 minutes for high-speed trains? By this standard, the punctuality rate of the KTX in 2023 drops to 79.1%. This means that out of ten times you ride, you will be more than 5 minutes late about twice. Even so, foreigners feel that Korean trains are accurate because they are comparing them to the trains in their own countries. Then, how do Korean trains keep to their schedules? Looking at the video released by KORAIL, the KTX undergoes maintenance every 5,000 km, 20,000 km, and 300,000 km depending on the distance traveled. At the maintenance center, the train is only allowed back out after the cabins are serviced and safety checks are completed. Forty minutes before departure, the train leaves the maintenance center and enters the departure platform. The captain and the train manager conduct pre-departure checks, and from 15 minutes before, they welcome passengers on the platform . At the departure time, the train manager checks the platform and departure signal, and only after seeing that the doors are closed do they notify the engineer to depart. The work of keeping every minute and second is repeated every time in places hidden from the passengers' eyes. Another thing foreigners all agree on is the silence of the cabin. The traveler heading to Japan also whispered into the camera that the person next to them was sleeping. People who need to make calls go out to the vestibule between cabins, and once the door closes, almost no sound can be heard inside. Sometimes, there are even announcements asking passengers to be quiet. One traveler laughed, saying the announcement made when a tour group was being loud sounded like it was aimed directly at them. A public transport travel YouTuber explained it this way. That in Korea, playing sound without earphones is considered an act of disrespect. It’s not because someone is watching, but because everyone understands it’s a shared space. A train with no ticket checks and quiet cabins are essentially scenes stemming from the same root. The fourth reason is the price. With KTX and SRT integrating this September, fares have gone down. An adult ticket for KTX standard class from Seoul to Busan went from 59,800 won down to 54,400 won. Even before that, the price hadn't gone up once in 15 years. It means that while inflation rose, train ticket prices stayed the same. When the SRT first started running in 2016, the fare from Suseo to Busan was 52,600 won, so the two high-speed trains had been competing on price. And with this integration, KTX fares have been lowered to match the SRT level. That British rail traveler was surprised once more by the price. It’s the fact that Korea's KTX costs the same regardless of how early you buy it. Long-distance trains in Europe and the U.S. often see prices jump significantly if you don't book in advance. From the cheapest Mugunghwa to the most expensive Haerang , Korea has a wide range of trains to choose from. But there is a twist hidden in all these stories. Korea didn't actually know how to build these high-speed trains that foreigners admire at first. Construction on the Gyeongbu High-Speed Railway began on June 30, 1992. At that time, Korea couldn't build trains on its own, so it was in the process of choosing a foreign train. Japan's Shinkansen, France's TGV, Germany's ICE. Japan had already launched the Shinkansen in 1964 , so we were starting nearly 30 years behind. In 1994, South Korea chose France's TGV. It was a contract for 46 train sets worth over 2.1 billion dollars. However, there was a condition attached to this contract. The condition was that some of those 46 sets would be built in Korea by transferring the technology. It wasn't just about buying them; it was about learning while we bought them. Construction was delayed by about six years from the original plan, and in April 2004, the KTX finally began to run. Following Japan, France, Germany, and Spain, we became the fifth country in the world to open the era of high-speed rail. But those trains were still built using French technology. Then, in March 2010, the KTX-Sancheon, built with Korean technology, made its first commercial run. It was the moment we became the fourth country in the world to possess high-speed rail technology, after Japan, France, and Germany. The KTX-Eum followed in 2021, and the KTX-Cheongryong in 2024. And in June 2024, something unprecedented in Korean railway history occurred. We began selling domestically made high-speed trains to another country. The buyer was Uzbekistan in Central Asia; it was a contract for 42 train cars based on the KTX-Eum, worth about 270 billion won. In December 2025, the first train was loaded onto a ship and left Masan. Then, on May 5, 2026, the first commercial operation began, running about 1,020 km from Tashkent to Khiva. A train made in Korea began running along the path connecting the cities of the old Silk Road. Hyundai Rotem projected that this train would cut travel time in half, to around seven hours. The country that was choosing which train to buy in April 1992 has now become a country that sells trains. In the meantime, the number of KTX passengers surpassed 1 billion in August 2023, just 19 years and 5 months after opening. Perhaps foreigners admire the KTX because those 30-plus years of history are embedded within it. Platforms without ticket gates, crossing the country in 2 hours and 17 minutes, quiet cabins that depart on time, train fares that haven't risen in 15 years, the Mugunghwa running for over 6 hours, and the Haerang with its beds—the seven things that surprised foreigners were all familiar parts of our daily lives. That American family, who held their paper tickets in their hands but never had to show them. What they felt on that platform must have been the trust extended first by a strange country. Perhaps what surprised the foreigners was that trust, more than the fast technology. A train that removed ticket-checking machines and chose to trust its passengers. And over 250,000 passengers a day are quietly responding to that trust. The ones who endured the time from learning imported technology to building it ourselves and even exporting it were countless nameless engineers, crew members, and the ordinary passengers who boarded the train every day.

Nearly 10,000 restaurants are closing down every day in China. Once-crowded restaurant districts are now covered in faded "for lease" signs. Believe me, this isn't a story about some dying rural town. This is happening right in the heart of Beijing and Shanghai. What's even stranger is that this collapse isn't just because customers have stopped coming. People who bet their life savings on opening shops are closing their doors within three to six months. How exactly did Chinese restaurants reach this point? Let’s break it down step by step. But just three years ago, the atmosphere in Chinese restaurants was the exact opposite. After COVID restrictions were lifted in 2023, spending at Chinese restaurants exceeded 5 trillion yuan for the first time. That’s about 160 trillion won in our currency. It jumped by more than 20%in just one year. That spring, a city called Zibo in Shandong province became famous nationwide for its barbecue skewers. College students flocked there to eat, and by the May Labor Day holiday, Zibo became the busiest city in China. It was a time when customers lined up as soon as a restaurant opened. Three years later, on the evening of June 15th, a food court in the basement of the Oriental Plaza, a large mall in downtown Beijing , held its final day of operation. The food court was called Dase Dai. It opened in the late 1990s and survived for 26 years. With the closure of this branch, Dase Dai has completely withdrawn from Beijing. In its heyday, it had over 40 locations nationwide, but now only 11 remain in mainland China. It’s like a large food court in the middle of Myeong-dong closing down. Customers who heard the news expressed their sadness, saying it was a place they often visited with friends during their school days. Comments even appeared saying there was no one there during the Lunar New Year. Local restaurant districts are experiencing the same thing. In a neighborhood in Panyu, Guangzhou, "for lease" stickers are plastered everywhere, yellowing with age. A nearby convenience store owner said a place that used to be a Western restaurant has been empty for over two years. Mr. Zhu, a business owner in Changsha, Hunan, runs a Sichuan restaurant. Mr. Zhu said that restaurants on his street have been closing one after another over the past six months. Ms. Lin, who runs a snack shop in Futian, Shenzhen, is in a similar situation. Ms. Lin has had a " business for sale" sign up for nearly half a year, but hasn't received a single inquiry. Business owners say this: "In the past, shops would change hands in two or three months, but now, there isn't a single person interested in taking them over." This isn't just happening in a few neighborhoods. According to data from Meituan, the delivery app most Chinese people use, 9,300 restaurants closed every day in 2024. There are about 800,000 food service business owners in our country. At this rate, all the restaurants in the country would disappear in just under three months. A 26-year-old food court has closed, "For Rent" signs in neighborhoods are fading yellow, and no one is stepping up to take over the shops. If your neighborhood feels the same way these days, please leave a comment. But what weighs heavier than the closed shops are the personal stories of the people who opened them. Let’s take a look at their stories one by one. Aran, who lives in Yancheng , Jiangsu, is 43 years old. Aran is a single mother. Aran is raising her young child on her own. In early 2024, Aran opened a shop with a lamb noodle brand she found on social media. Aran invested 400,000 yuan, which is about 80 million won in our currency. She poured almost all her savings into it. The headquarters claimed it was impossible not to make money just by selling noodles. During the grand opening, thanks to a "buy one, get one free" promotion, daily sales hit 10,000 yuan. However, after that, daily sales hovered between 3,000 and 4,500 yuan. That's about 600,000 to 900,000 won in our currency. To break even, the shop needed to sell 5,000 yuan a day, but Aran said she almost never crossed that line. Since it was a 24-hour operation requiring shift workers, fixed monthly costs were 60,000 yuan. That's about 12 million won in our currency. Eventually , Aran couldn't afford her share of the annual rent, which was about 25 million won. So, on December 28, 2025, Aran closed the shop with her own hands. This is what Aran says now. "No one tricked me.""I just didn't know anything about business or management." Vincent in Guangzhou sold coffee and hand-pulled noodles in a small, 23- square-meter shop for four years. Since the price war between delivery apps began in 2025, Vincent's daily sales dropped by over 80%. Yet, the monthly rent actually rose from 14,500 yuan to 18,500 yuan. That’s an increase from about 2.9 million won to about 3.7 million won in our currency. Vincent says that coffee has now become a 9.9- yuan item in people’s minds. So, where does the kitchen equipment from the closed shops go? In Langfang, Hebei , there is a second-hand store where kitchen equipment from closed restaurants collects. The store covers about 20,000 square meters, which is over 6,000 pyeong. An Dawei, the owner of this store, said he collected equipment from about 7,000 restaurants throughout 2025. Among those who handed over their equipment to this store was a young person who invested 650,000 yuan—about 130 million won—into a Taiwanese milk tea franchise after seeing an advertisement. The young man closed his shop. On the day he cleared out the equipment, he reportedly sat in the empty shop and cried all afternoon. CEO Anda Wei puts it this way. Most beginners just can't endure it. Many people say they will never run a restaurant again in this lifetime. Listening this far, it might sound like the story of a few unlucky people. So, let's verify it one by one with the numbers. First, an industry white paper based on Meituan's data. In 2025, 3.39 million restaurants were marked as having ceased operations on Meituan. That is 290,000 more than a year ago. And two out of every three shops that closed had been open for less than two years. Second, closures have not stopped as the year progressed. From last December to June of this year, over 2.05 million places shut down. One in four newly opened shops didn't even last half a year. In the first half of this year, the number of restaurants declined in cities of all tiers, from the largest to the smallest. Third, even large restaurants had nothing left over. Let's look at 2,628 large-scale restaurants in Beijing. The profits of these restaurants in the first half of 2024 dropped by 88.8%in one year. The profit margin was 0.37%. For every 100 yuan sold, only 0.37 yuan remained. In our currency, that’s like selling 20,000 won worth and keeping 74 won. Fourth, well-known restaurants were no exception. China's famous restaurant chain, Xibei, announced in January this year that it would close 102 locations across various cities. It is known that about 4,000 employees worked at the closing branches. Shanghai's famous restaurant, Xiao Nanguo, posted notices of temporary closure for facility inspections and shut down all 13 of its Shanghai branches at once just before the Lunar New Year in early February. Over 300 tables of customers who had reserved for the Lunar New Year’s Eve family dinner were left with nowhere to eat. Everyone, please remember the company name mentioned in the first piece of evidence. It is Meituan. It is like Baedal Minjok to us. This company, which counted and announced how many restaurants closed along with industry associations, reappears in a very important role later in this story. But everyone, this is just the tip of the iceberg. What we have seen so far is the result. From now on, we will look at why this happened. There are three causes. Too many shops were opened. And the money customers spend per meal has decreased. Lastly, delivery companies engaged in a discount war, and authorities failed to issue rules to stop this competition for over a year. First, the first cause. In 2023, over 3.18 million new catering businesses were registered in China. It increased by 24%year-on-year. Over 3.13 million new businesses were registered just by November. So, who is opening these shops? After COVID-19 lockdowns ended, mass layoffs occurred in China's real estate, education, finance, and IT sectors. People pushed out of their companies flocked to the restaurant business. Many of them believed the economy would bounce back quickly and gambled their life savings. When asked why, the answers were similar. Restaurants have low barriers to entry and are easy to learn. You can recoup your investment quickly. At this age, I can't find any other job. Mr. Shin was an office worker in his late thirties. He worked for a bank subsidiary for over ten years. After leaving the company, he opened a noodle franchise called Suanlafen. Mr. Li, based in Beijing, was a 28-year-old youth. He used to be a product planner at a large IT firm. Mr. Li used his severance pay and extra savings to open a noodle shop , but he closed it after one month. The money he lost was nearly 100,000 yuan, about 20 million won in our currency. Mr. Li said that although he was the boss, he worked more than his employees . When there are three chicken shops in a neighborhood, you can make a decent living. But what happens when there are ten chicken shops in the same neighborhood? The people in the neighborhood don't eat three times as much chicken. Ten shops end up splitting the same customers. Do you know how densely packed restaurants are in Beijing? Wang Fuzhong, a representative at Meituan, said the number of restaurants per 1,000 people in Beijing is more than double that of New York. There is a shop that shows what happens when restaurants become this dense. Mr. Zhang’s family in Chengdu, Sichuan, had run a Sichuan restaurant for over 20 years. But within nine months, 11 new restaurants opened within 30 meters in front of and behind his shop. Even though this shop was the most popular in the neighborhood, their earnings dropped compared to before. After paying three employees, their take-home profit dropped significantly, and eventually, the shop closed late last year. Shops that opened chasing trends closed even faster. In Zibo, which we saw earlier, over 820 barbecue shops were newly registered from March to June 2023. However, as tourist numbers dropped from June, over 100 posts appeared on Chinese trading sites looking to sell off Zibo barbecue shops. In 2024, Tianshui Malatang briefly became a hit. According to Meituan, the closure rate for Tianshui Malatang shops was 3.7 times higher than that of regular Malatang shops. From January to April 2023, 740,000 new restaurants opened. Only 53%of these managed to survive for one year. However, we cannot solely blame those who opened these restaurants. For those pushed out of their companies, opening a restaurant was often the only option they had left . If the sheer number of restaurants were the only problem, some would still have survived. But at the same time, the amount customers spent per meal was also decreasing. Let’s take a look at how much Chinese people are spending on a meal at restaurants these days. In the third quarter of 2025, the average per-capita dining-out cost in China was 33 yuan. That is about 6,600 Korean won . That is less than the price of a bowl of soup in my neighborhood, yet it is what an average Chinese person spends on a meal. This figure has fallen by 23.6%compared to two years ago. Out of 44 major cities, Beijing is the only one where people spend over 100 yuan per meal. In eight out of ten cities, the cost is under 50 yuan. For formal Chinese restaurants, the price per meal has dropped by a staggering 41.3%. Wang Puzhong, CEO of Meituan, said this last autumn. The per-capita cost for dining in this year has become very close to 2015 levels. It is as if restaurant price tags have gone back 10 years. However, monthly rent has not gone down accordingly. In Tianjin, there is a famous crepe shop owner who resembles a popular singer. The annual rent for this shop rose from 130,000 yuan to 200,000 yuan. In our currency, it went from about 26 million won to about 40 million won. This shop announced its closure in August of this year. Restaurant owners all over are saying the same thing. When 2,298 restaurants across China were surveyed, 80%reported a decrease in net profit. Over 70%cited the decline in per-customer spending as the reason for the drop in profit. Large restaurants are in the same situation. Revenue for large-scale Chinese restaurants fell by 0.7%in August of this year compared to a year ago. There is one more reason why customers are spending less. Expensive business entertainment dinners have stopped. On May 18, 2025, the Chinese Communist Party and State Council revised austerity rules for party and government agencies, banning luxury dishes, tobacco, and alcohol at business meals. Immediately afterward, some luxury restaurants in Gansu and Qinghai provinces saw their May revenue drop by 70%. A regular customer at the previously mentioned Xiao Nan Guo said there is no longer a need for entertainment and that they have grown tired of the food. Of course, this one regulation is not the sole reason for the restaurant collapse. The industry also stated that since 2012, restaurants that are profitable have already decreased significantly, so the impact should not be exaggerated. However, the China Cuisine Association noted that in some regions, these regulations were enforced more strictly , reducing even ordinary business meals and family gatherings. In an empty shopping mall near the Beijing Olympic Park, there was a bakery franchise. The monthly rent for this bakery was 50,000 yuan. That is about 10 million won in our currency. Unable to afford the rent , the bakery closed its doors after 14 months. The manager said this: People have no money. They don't spend like they used to. Because it's too hard to earn. Up to this point, it was a story about business not doing well. The third cause you are about to see is the core of today's story. This time, it is a story about losing money the more you sell. But there was a name I asked you to remember earlier. It is Meituan. The very company that counted and announced the number of restaurant closures along with the industry association is a party to this fight. The fight began in February 2025. As China's major online shopping company, JD, jumped into delivery, it made this offer to restaurants. If you join before May 1st , we will not charge any commissions for one year. In July, another online shopping company, Alibaba, unveiled a subsidy plan. They announced they would spend 50 billion yuan, or about 10 trillion won in our money, on subsidies over the next year. Here, subsidies are discounts where the app company cuts the food price on behalf of the customer. On a weekend in July that year, zero-yuan milk tea orders poured in. When two large local supermarkets start selling ramen at half price to steal customers from each other, the first to close is the small corner store next door. Supermarkets have the money to hold out, but corner stores do not have that kind of money. The situation for Chinese restaurants was worse than this. Caught in the middle of the discount war, restaurants even had to share the cost of the discounts. A research team from Fudan University examined data from over 40,000 restaurants. After competition intensified, restaurant orders increased by an average of 7%. However, the money restaurants held in their hands per day actually decreased by about 4%. A small restaurant owner in a Beijing hutong during the weekend when zero-yuan orders were pouring in said this: Orders increased, but I didn't make money. Once you subtract delivery fees, ingredients, and the shared burden of platform subsidy commissions, there is nothing left per order. The Guangdong Restaurant Association surveyed 137 restaurants and found that the profit margin of dine-in focused restaurants fell by an average of 68%. The ones that suffered the biggest losses were individual shops operating with only one location. The profits of these shops fell by 85.4%. 92%of the restaurants that participated in the survey asked for the subsidy war to stop. Food prices have also gone down. Let’s look at a cheap delivery order under 15 yuan, about 3,000 won in our money. Before the subsidy war, these orders accounted for 20 to 30%of the total. After the war, this share increased to about 40%. So, do you know how much less these app companies earned in this fight? During the one year of the subsidy war, the combined profit loss for the three companies, including Alibaba and Meituan, was 173 billion yuan, about 34.6 trillion won in our money. Looking at Meituan alone, it had a net profit of 35.8 billion yuan in 2024. However, in 2025, it posted a net loss of 23.4 billion yuan, about 4.7 trillion won. Even with such a sharp drop in profits, the app companies had the money to endure. Local restaurants did not have such money. Then what did the authorities do during that time? Let’s look at it in chronological order. Three months after the fight began, on May 13th of last year, market regulators called in JD.com, Meituan, and another delivery app, Ele.me, to warn them to compete fairly. What happened after that? Two months later, on July 18th, the same authorities summoned these companies for a second time. That month, the China Cuisine Association complained that there were orders but no profits, and the China Chain Store&Franchise Association demanded an end to the subsidies. What happened after that? 11 months after the fight started, on January 9th of this year, the State Council began an investigation into the delivery app industry. And 1 year and 4 months later, on June 17th of this year , a draft of subsidy rules was released . It bans large-scale subsidies and prohibits passing discount costs onto restaurants. As of September, there has been no news that these rules have been officially implemented. In the meantime , the number of restaurants that closed in 2025 increased by 290,000 compared to the previous year. Of course, the authorities were not just sitting idly by. They called in the companies, issued warnings, and conducted investigations. It is also true that the subsidies were a benefit to customers. Peng Jianzhen, head of the China Chain Store&Franchise Association , also said last year's delivery war was a gain for consumers. The problem was the speed. During the year it took for rules to emerge, shops without the funds to survive were the first to close. If you just stop at saying "the platforms were bad," you're only seeing half the story. But everyone, you need to look at one more number here. In 2025, the money Chinese people spent at restaurants was 5.7982 trillion yuan. That is about 1,160 trillion won in our money. It was up 3.2%in a year, the highest level ever. However, the 3.39 million restaurants that closed that same year were also the most ever. The number of restaurants in operation was almost the same as the year before. Customer spending at restaurants has increased, but there are now too many shops sharing that revenue, the cost of a single meal per person has shrunk, and the cut taken by delivery services has grown. The case of 11 new restaurants opening next to Mr. Zhang’s family shop in Chengdu, and the Fudan University study showing that even with more orders, take-home profit fell, all tell the same story. There are also businesses that have grown in this climate. It’s Mixue, a chain that sells most of its drinks for under 10 yuan, or less than 2,000 won in our currency. In just one year, the number of Mixue stores grew from about 46,000 to approximately 60,000. Their revenue also jumped by 35.2%. While restaurants were closing one after another, shops selling at low prices were actually expanding rapidly. Feng Jianyuan, chairman of the China Chain Store& Franchise Association, described the current situation like this: "The industry is undergoing a brutal reshuffling." But is this only a story about China? Let’s take a closer look at why this is also our story. Folks, if you compare what Chinese restaurants are going through to our local shops and your own retirement savings, three things become clear. First, count the number of neighboring shops before you count the number of customers. Before opening a shop, you must first check how many stores in the same industry are within walking distance. Just think back to Mr. Zhang’s family shop in Chengdu. Second, calculate the cut taken by middlemen first. You need to figure out exactly how much profit remains per bowl after deducting delivery app commissions, advertising costs, and delivery fees. Third, do not bet your retirement fund on a fleeting trend. You saw earlier how the Zibo barbecue and Tianshui malatang crazes ended. This is not just a story about another country. In 2024, the number of people who filed for business closure in South Korea exceeded 1 million. This is the first time since statistics were first compiled in 1995. Of these, over 150,000 were restaurants. In 2025, total business closures decreased slightly to 976,000. However, the closure rate for the restaurant industry was 15.14%, the second highest after the retail sector. 68.5%of those who closed their businesses were in debt. For those in their 60s or older, the average debt was 98.97 million won. It cost an average of 12.86 million won just to close down a single shop. 64.4% of those who shut down said they decided to close when their revenue dropped by more than 40%. The situation this year is not good either. According to tentative private data, one out of every six restaurants closed its doors between January and May of this year. This rate is higher than during the COVID-19 pandemic. As of January this year, the number of restaurant business owners in Korea was about 800,000, a 1.9%decrease from a year ago. The " Yellow Umbrella" closure deduction paid to self-employed business owners who shut down their shops reached 1.485 trillion won in 2025 alone. This is the highest amount recorded since the system was introduced in 2007. In the first half of this year alone, 966.7 billion won was paid out, an increase of 15.84%compared to a year ago. Our country also experienced a fierce " delivery discount war." In March 2024, Coupang Eats began offering unlimited free delivery to paid members, and Baedal Minjok followed suit. To cover those costs, Baemin raised its brokerage fees to 9.8%in August of that year. Both restaurant owners and consumers expressed strong opposition. Ultimately, in a win-win agreement in November, Baemin lowered fees on a sliding scale based on revenue, but the delivery fees paid by owners increased instead. The delivery fee, which had been a maximum of 2,900 won per order, became as much as 3,400 won. According to a study released this September, restaurants using delivery apps paid 26.2%of their monthly revenue in fees and other charges. It means that for every 1 million won in sales, over 260,000 won goes to the app companies. 51%of the businesses surveyed responded that a cap on total fees should be implemented. Bills proposing such a cap are currently under discussion in the National Assembly and have not yet been passed. Of course, the platform industry also has a counterargument. They claim the main reasons for restaurants 'struggles are rising food ingredient costs and labor expenses. You might recall a neighbor or relative who left their company during the financial crisis and opened a shop with their severance pay. Try to remember what happened to their businesses afterward as well. Now, let's return to the question I asked at the beginning. Actually, everyone, I asked you this at the start. How on earth did Chinese restaurants end up in this state? The answer was three-fold. First, too many people opened restaurants at once, including those who lost their jobs, leading to market saturation. Second, the amount customers spend on a single meal has returned to levels seen 10 years ago. Third, while restaurants' shares shrank due to the delivery apps 'discount wars, government regulations only emerged as a draft after 1 year and 4 months. Even after investing almost all his savings, Kun Alansi could barely exceed his daily break-even point. A 20-year-old shop in Chengdu shut down because, as neighboring shops increased, the money they actually took home decreased. Even for restaurants where orders increased, the actual profit they kept decreased. Shops don't just fail because they lack customers. Those who first recognize the competition next door and the cut taken by middlemen are the ones who protect their money. Each "For Lease" sign yellowing in the alleys of Guangzhou contains one such story.

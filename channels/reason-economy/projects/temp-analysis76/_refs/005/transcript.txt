No one said it was a good purchase. It was a factory in North Carolina, USA, where white and orange equipment used to come out in rows amidst corn. In narrow spaces where a large excavator would have to move back and forth several times, this equipment would turn around in place. You have likely seen this small equipment squeezing in and scooping up soil at a corner of an apartment complex construction site. In the summer of 2007, Korea's Doosan stepped forward to buy that factory. $ 4.9 billion. That was 5.7 trillion won in Korean currency. It was the amount of money a Korean company had ever spent overseas up until that point. The company's name was Bob Cage. It was double the assets of Doosan Infracore, which was pursuing the acquisition. However, we were originally the type to buy and use American machinery. The market for U.S. construction equipment was divided among Caterpillar, John Deere, and Japan's Kubota. Could anyone have readily believed back then the idea of bringing an entire factory over from that market? And less than a year after the deal was signed, the entire U.S. housing market collapsed. Housing starts eventually plummeted to one-fifth of their peak. Small construction sites came to a halt. The site where Bob Cash worked the most froze completely. The day after Doosan announced it would invest an additional $ 1 billion, the stock prices of Doosan and Doosan Infracore plummeted to the daily limit for two consecutive days. Over the next three years, the deficits Bob Cash accumulated exceeded 1.2 trillion won. It was a chilling figure. Newspapers gave this acquisition a name : the winner's curse. Now, however, there is something strange here. When Doosan had to split up and sell the group again 14 years later, this company, which seemed likely to be the first to be put up for sale, was not on the list. Instead, it had been moved further inside first. In the meantime, nearly three out of every ten new skid steer loaders installed at U.S. construction sites became this company's property. Neither Caterpillar , John Deere, nor Kubota were able to secure those top spots. Was it because they lacked the funds? Or was there something in that factory that money could not buy? What on earth did Doosan purchase in 2007? And why did they not let go of this small white piece of equipment, even at the moment the group stood on the brink of collapse? This item was not originally made in a factory. It was a seven-acre farm in Minnesota, USA, in the 1950s. Manure was piling up on the floor of the two-story barn, and there was no way to scoop it out. You might think, " Couldn't they just use a tractor?" However, inside the barn, pillars supporting the roof stood densely packed together. The tractor that had gotten stuck between the pillars could not move forward or backward. There was simply no machine in the world capable of fitting in there. The owner of the farm went to find the Keller brothers, who were serving as chief ministers nearby. The machine the two built entered the barn for the first time in 1957. It looked crude. However, this machine rotated a full circle on its own inside. This meant it could change direction even while stuck between pillars. They made exactly seven units over the course of that year. The name is rooted in its rotation method. It changes direction in place by sliding the left and right wheels apart to glide along the ground. It is called " skid steer" because it steers by sliding. In 1958, the machine stopped in the Pahnhamhoe, Minnesota. People flocked to see it. That autumn, the Keller brothers packed their bags and moved to a small town called Guerre in North Dacotta. They joined forces with the Mello brothers, who had been making farm machinery there since 1947. Starting in 1962, this machine was given a name. It is none other than the Bobcat. Let me clarify one thing here. This machine originated from a headache for U.S. village chiefs, and it was created by the American "Big Brother" brothers. Korea has no starting point in this story. Furthermore, the market where this machine operated already had owners. Caterpillar of the United States had sales of $ 67.6 billion last year. It is the number one company, holding 15.2%of the global construction equipment market on its own. Japan's Kubota sells for 3.0189 trillion won. Even companies like John Deere of the U.S. and JCB of the UK were companies whose names alone carried a different weight. At that time, we were not on this list at all. However, the market for this small piece of equipment is larger than expected. In the U.S. alone , over 5,000 compact track loaders— equipped with tracks instead of wheels —were sold annually via installment plans or leases. That means more than 150 units found owners every day. Now, however, there is one strange point here. The U.S. company that had owned this business for a long time put it on the market in 2007. However, the buyer was not one of the market's owners. It was a company from a country whose name wasn't even on the list. So, what exactly did Doosan see in the company to place that bet? First, we need to talk about the side of the market. The company that owned this business was Engerrand of the United States. In May 2007, the company announced to the market that it would sell its global business, including Bobcat. There is a point here that is easy to misunderstand. It was not put up for sale because business was bad. Herbert Engel, the chairman at the time, stated that these businesses were world-class, equipped with outstanding people, products, and brands. However, he added this caveat: they no longer fit our long-term strategy. He wrote even more directly in the report submitted to the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission : they were businesses that were volatile due to economic cycles, had slow growth, and consumed a lot of assets. Ingersoll Rand made a profit of $ 2.652 billion from this sale, even after paying taxes, and bought a train from his air conditioning company in December. He sold Bobcat to buy air conditioners. So why did Doosan go in the opposite direction? For Doosan, this deal was not a sudden, calculated move. In 1997, they sold the Coca-Cola bottling business for 432 million won, and starting in 1998, they sold OB Beer for approximately 900 million won. With that money, they acquired Korea General Industries in 2001 and Daewoo Heavy Industries in 2005. It was a decade of selling the things we used to pick up at the supermarket to acquire companies that built factories. Bobcat was the decisive move placed at the end of that decade. Park Young-ma, who was the Vice Chairman of Doosan Fracore at the time, stated the following in an interview. They argue that acquiring a flower vase is attractive because it significantly reduces the time and energy required to grow by developing technology in-house. The answer is contained in that single sentence. What Doosan bought was not a factory, but time. Let's do the math. Building a new factory in the U.S. takes time. However, it takes even longer for a dealer to introduce a Korean brand they have never seen before to their store. It may take even longer for a farm owner to trust that dealer and spend tens of thousands of dollars to select this equipment. Instead of building that time from scratch, Doosan sought out a company that already had that time built up. This becomes even clearer if you look at the list that came with the contract . It included over 2,700 dealers in the U.S. and Europe, 16 production facilities scattered across six countries, and approximately 5,700 employees. But was this dealer network truly an asset that money couldn't buy? Doosan proves it firsthand 11 years later. In 2018, Doosan spun off its heavy-duty equipment business from Bobcat, established a North American subsidiary, and assigned 100 people, stating its goal was to become the fifth largest player in North America within a few years. At that time, the representative of that subsidiary remarked that their market share in North America was somewhat low and weak . The explanation regarding dealers was even more specific. He stated that the core gap preventing them from reaching the top five was the lack of dealers in many markets. There were no dealers even in major cities like Toronto and Pittsburgh. From 2010 to 2018, Doosan replaced two of its 128 existing dealers and expanded its North American branches to just three. It took eight years of dedicated effort to reach only three branches. At the same time, Bobcat had over 1,500 dealers and branches in North America. That is a tenfold difference. Why is this not working? In the U.S., almost every state protects equipment dealers by law . Manufacturers cannot terminate a contract without justifiable grounds, and if they do, they must even relinquish their inventory. Suitable dealers are already tied to other brands, and since those contracts are protected by law, there are no available spots even if you bring the money. This also aligns with why Henckel chose Doosan as the buyer. They stated that Doosan Infracore's strong position in the Asia-Pacific region would provide growth opportunities for these businesses. Park was the number one in North America, but he lacked Asia. Doosan had Asia, but what it sold in North America were medium to large-sized vehicles, such as excavators and wheels. It was a deal where they swapped each other's empty spaces. However, there is one thing that needs to be clarified. While Doosan bought the number one spot, it was not the number one during its prime . Bob Keck's market share for skid steer loaders in the U.S. was 41%in 2002, but it had dropped to 32.1%by 2006—a decrease of 1%. They bought a declining number one at that price. And that money was not their own. Equity accounted for only 20%, and the remaining $ 3.9 billion was borrowed. It was a method of purchasing a company using funds borrowed against the target company as collateral. The transaction closed on November 30, 2007. This debt could have been endured as long as U.S. housing prices continued to rise. However, the opposite was true. And the very next day, immediately after Doosan signed the deal, the opposite became reality. On the day the transaction concluded, Heng-Cain stated that this sale enabled Ingersoll Rand to achieve consistent performance across all phases of the economic cycle. This meant they had divested the business that typically halts first when the economy declines. That statement was proven correct in less than a year. U.S. housing starts stood at 2.27 million in 2006. By April 2009, that figure had dropped to 477,000. That represented a 79%loss from the peak. It was the lowest figure recorded since statistics began in 1959. Why was this number so devastating for Bobsey? To build a house, you must first level the ground, dig, and move the equipment. This small piece of equipment is exactly what does that job. Backyards where large excavators cannot enter, narrow alleys, and landscaping sites were the designated spots for this equipment. Consequently, if houses are not built, the equipment disappears. Farmers, landscapers, and even small-scale construction workers operating with a single truck all change their minds, deciding to get through the year. In August 2008, Doosan announced that it would inject an additional $ 1 billion into its overseas subsidiaries. The company explained that this was a financial restructuring decision made by management on their own initiative, rather than being forced. However, the market interpreted it in the exact opposite way. Starting the day after the announcement, the stock prices of Doosan and Doosan Infracore plummeted to the daily limit for two consecutive days. At that time, the daily price limit was 15%up and down. Nearly three months 'worth of market capitalization vanished in just two days. During the same period, Doosan Heavy Industries fell by 11%, and Doosan Construction by 6%. It was not just a problem for the company; the entire group was shaken. They had to raise money. Even so, it was not enough. Doosan Infracore's total capital gains swelled to 2.38 trillion won by June 2010—six times its value before the acquisition. Interest does not stop even if the factory shuts down . Even if sales decline, the capital does not shrink. After all, there are times when it makes sense to stop the monthly interest payments, even if it means selling them for less than the market value. However, Doosan moved in the opposite direction. Instead of choosing what to discard, they decided to create time to hold out. At the time, no one could know if that choice was right. Then, how do you create time? It is easy to say that they "held out." However, that statement explains nothing. During this long period, what actually took place were three decisions. The first was the additional capital contribution you saw earlier. They chose to inject more money even knowing that the stock price would hit the lower limit. The initial investment went into acquiring the company, and the subsequent investment went into buying the time for that company to survive. The second is the least known decision in this story. In November, Doosan Infracore restructured its remaining borrowings. The amount to be repaid was $ 2.29 billion, of which $ 2.18 billion was newly raised. Why was this decisive? Originally, the maturities were clustered in November for three consecutive years starting in 2012. In the first year alone, it amounted to $ 890 million. They restructured this debt and pushed it back. The debt did not disappear. They moved the red date stamped on the calendar back a few years. However, you might wonder if that was even possible at that time. The second half of 2011 was a time when the flow of money had frozen due to the European financial crisis. In that market, the bonds issued by Doosan Infracore received orders totaling $ 2.75 billion—eight times the issuer's amount. It is equivalent to saying they intended to sell 100 million won worth, but received orders for 800 million won. There was also a price tag attached to buying time. A condition was attached to list the subsidiary within three years, but the listing did not happen.

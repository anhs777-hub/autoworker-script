The colorful airplanes taking off from the airport runway. And the thin blue latex gloves tightly fitted to the hands of doctors in operating rooms everywhere. These two things, which seem to belong to entirely different worlds, actually branched out from the hands of two brothers in the same family. The older brother, who sprinted to 7th place in the business rankings by taking on 10 trillion won in debt. And the younger brother, who was kicked out after warning against such reckless greed. Fifteen years later, the older brother lost his airplanes and even the company name, while the younger brother survived, earning 2.4 trillion won in a single year. We will now trace the shocking path of money that determined the fate of these two brothers. Where exactly did this massive tragedy begin? If we go back in time, the beginning was truly humble, almost beyond belief. In April 1946, right after liberation, the founder, the late Chairman Park In-chon, started the business with just two old 5-seater used Ford taxis he received from the U.S. military. The company, which started with two taxis amidst the ruins of war, grew in size at a terrifying pace. The taxis carrying passengers grew into Gwangju Passenger Transport in 1948, becoming the root of Kumho Express, and in 1960, he established Samyang Tire—now Kumho Tire—to manufacture tires directly. Following this, he founded Kumho Petrochemical in 1970 to produce synthetic rubber, and in 1988, he launched Asiana Airlines, the second civil airline, standing tall as a leading domestic conglomerate spanning both land and sky. A major change came in September 2002 to this solidly built empire. Park Sam-koo, the founder's third son, took the helm of the group. However, in the heart of the newly inaugurated Chairman Park Sam-koo, a unique thirst was taking root. He was not satisfied with the stable business structure inherited from his predecessors and burned with ambition to cross the massive threshold from being a top 10 conglomerate to a top 5 business group in Korea. The stage where that ambition exploded was the mega-sized corporate acquisition battle that shook the capital market in 2006. The target Chairman Park had his eyes on was Daewoo E&C, the absolute powerhouse of the construction industry at the time. The amount Chairman Park Sam-koo bid to acquire a 72%stake in Daewoo E&C was truly jaw-dropping. He poured in an astronomical sum of over 6.4 trillion won. 6.4 trillion won was a massive fortune that would have been difficult for the entire Kumho Group to earn in a year, even by working themselves to the bone. However, as their business ranking jumped from 11th to 8th, Chairman Park Sam-koo continued his relentless sprint. Just two years later, in March 2008, he swallowed up Korea Express, the nation's No. 1 integrated logistics company, for 4.1 trillion won. The plan was to complete a perfect integrated logistics empire by adding airplanes in the sky, express buses on the roads, and logistics services at ports and for deliveries. The total cost to swallow the two giant dinosaurs known as Daewoo E&C and Korea Express reached a staggering 10.5 trillion won. The 10 trillion won mentioned earlier is an unimaginable sum of money that could have shaken the profits and losses of all listed companies in South Korea at the time. Thanks to this, the total assets of Kumho Asiana Group surpassed 26 trillion won in one fell swoop in 2008. With nearly 70 affiliates under its wing and rising to 7th place in the business world according to fair trade authority standards, it truly hit the peak of its golden age. The world poured unsparing praise on Chairman Park Sam-koo, calling him a gambler and a genius of M&A. However, behind the champagne of the glorious celebration, a chillingly precarious truth was hidden. To put it simply, it was like a person pedaling a normal bicycle while carrying two massive dump trucks on their back, weighing dozens of times more than themselves. There is no way the bicycle wheels could withstand that terrifying weight. Even if you scraped together the stock value of every listed affiliate belonging to the Kumho Group at the time, it didn't even come close to 10 trillion won. It was a situation where he couldn't even buy a single truck tire with his own money, yet he was taking on two giants entirely with borrowed funds. On the outside, he was wearing the shining armor of being the 7th largest business group, but on the inside, the sound of breaking bones began to emerge. Then, with what guts did Chairman Park Sam-koo bet such a massive stake that far exceeded his own capabilities? There was a dark contract that made that massive debt possible. However, under the very dining table where people were cheering and popping champagne, a 4 trillion won financial time bomb began to tick quietly, ready to cut the throat of the entire group. How on earth did Chairman Park Sam-koo manage to raise this massive acquisition fund? The secret lay in the cruel debt structure hidden behind the ledgers. Out of the 6.4 trillion won acquisition cost for Daewoo E&C, the actual money Kumho Group had on hand was a mere 2.9 trillion won. It was a level where even if they emptied every penny of the group's cash, they couldn't even cover half of it. Then, where did the remaining 3.5 trillion won come from? The answer was in external financial investors, such as banks and private equity funds. As soon as Chairman Park reached out his hand, financial companies arrived as if they had been waiting, carrying bundles of massive funds. But there is no such thing as a free lunch in this world. There was no way investors would readily hand over massive, trillion-won sums without expecting anything in return. It was here that Chairman Park Sam-koo signed a devilish contract, putting the entire fate of the group on the line. In the financial world, they called it a "put-back option," but once you strip away the packaging, the structure is quite simple. To put it simply, it’s like borrowing someone else’s money to buy an expensive apartment and signing a note. You’re promising that if the house price drops in three years, you’ll cover all the losses or buy the house back at the original high price. It was a contract that guaranteed investors a stock price of 32,000 won per share, along with a high annual compound interest rate of 9 %. If the stock price goes up, everyone makes money, so there’s no problem, but if it falls, it was a terrifying private debt contract where the group would have to shoulder all the losses alone. There was a person who, upon hearing about the details of this horrific contract, rushed over looking pale with dread. It was his younger brother, Park Chan-koo, then-Vice Chairman of the group and the founder’s fourth son, who was leading Kumho Petrochemical. The younger brother went to his brother’s office and pleaded with him in utter desperation. He cried out that buying a company with such a ticking time bomb, using other people’s money without any of his own, would lead to the destruction of the entire group. To his brother’s eyes, it was crystal clear that the put-back option was a time bomb that could explode at any moment. But to his brother, blinded by the dazzling light of becoming a top 10 conglomerate, the younger brother's screams sounded like nothing more than the complaints of a coward. Chairman Park Sam-koo ignored his brother’s painful warnings and pushed forward with the contract. And exactly two years later, the bells of catastrophe rang out like a lie. In the fall of 2008, the massive tsunami of the global financial crisis that swept the world hit the South Korean construction market head-on. As the real estate market froze, Daewoo E&C’s stock price began a plummet without wings. The stock promised at 32,000 won fell to the low 10,000 won range, effectively cut into a third in an instant. As the promised day of settlement approached, financial investors who smelled blood began exercising their put-back options en masse. The total amount written on the bills in the investors 'hands exceeded a staggering 4 trillion won. Can you even fathom how large a sum 4 trillion won is? It was a debt hole beyond imagination, one that couldn't be filled even if every single seat on every Asiana Airlines flight worldwide were sold out for decades. Under the suffocating pressure of such debt, on June 28, 2009, the Kumho Group finally raised the white flag. They threw up their hands, announcing they would put Daewoo E&C back on the market, less than three years after acquiring it. However, no one was willing to look at the bloodied asset at its original price. When a 4 trillion won debt bomb exploded, the fraternal bond that had lasted for 60 years turned into a bloody battlefield overnight. Unexpectedly, it was the younger brother, Chairman Park Chan-koo, who first drew his sword. Once the unmanageable 4 trillion won debt bomb went off, the massive ship called Kumho had already become a sinking wreck filling with seawater. Due to his brother’s reckless gamble, even innocent, profitable companies were in danger of being dragged down to the depths. The fear struck that if they just sat by, even the petrochemical division, built with the blood and sweat of their predecessors, would sink along with them. In June 2009, Chairman Park Chan-koo launched a quiet but meticulous escape operation. He sold off every share of the holding company, Kumho Industrial, held by himself and his family on the stock market. He then scraped all that money together and began to aggressively buy up shares of Kumho Petrochemical, which he was managing. He effectively built an independent defensive wall by surpassing a 10%stake in one fell swoop . Since he had essentially lowered a lifeboat to survive alone on a sinking ship, how did this look in the eyes of his older brother, Chairman Park Sam-koo? It must have looked like an unforgivable betrayal, with his own flesh and blood stabbing him in the back. He trembled with a sense of betrayal, furious that his brother would dare try to flee and abandon the captain, whether the ship was sinking or not. The older brother’s rage was immediate and cruel. Just a month later , on the morning of July 28, 2009, a surprise coup occurred that is rare in the history of the business world. Chairman Park Sam-koo urgently convened an emergency board meeting of Kumho Petrochemical in a flash. He mobilized the board members to abruptly remove his younger brother, Chairman Park Chan-koo, from his position as CEO. After chasing his brother out the door before he could even react, Chairman Park Sam-koo calmly stood before the reporters. He declared a sudden joint resignation, stating that he too would take responsibility for the entire situation, stepping down from the front lines of management to become honorary chairman. However, it was merely a flashy show to appease the angry public and the creditors. While pretending to step back on the surface, he was still holding onto control of the group from behind the scenes. But could the 4 trillion won debt possibly disappear because of the brothers' power struggle ? The creditors 'cold patience finally reached its limit. On December 30, 2009 , a tragic decision that would go down in South Korean financial history was made. The group’s root, the parent company Kumho Industrial, and its core manufacturer, Kumho Tire, could not avoid default and entered workout— that is, management by creditors. With Kumho Petrochemical and Asiana Airlines also applying for voluntary agreements with creditors, the Kumho empire, one of the top 10 conglomerates, was split in two overnight. From that moment on, the brothers, bound by blood, became enemies worse than strangers and waged a mudslinging war. The younger brother exposed internal documents claiming his older brother had created a slush fund, and the older brother retaliated by suing him for breach of trust, alleging he had secretly sold off Asiana Airlines’ in-flight meal business. On top of this, there were lawsuits over the trademark rights to the two characters "Kumho," leading to a chain of over eight lawsuits and criminal complaints over the span of seven years . The entire nation watched live as the siblings, who used to gather for ancestral rites on holidays, put on an ugly spectacle in court, pointing fingers and vowing to send each other to prison. It goes to show that greed and pride took precedence over their bond as blood relatives. This long-drawn-out war finally reached its end in December 2015. The Supreme Court officially recognized Kumho Petrochemical and its affiliates as an independent corporate group completely unrelated to the Kumho Asiana Group. The younger brother finally escaped his older brother’s grasp and paved his own way to survival. However, the real tragedy began after the separation of the groups. The older brother’s final gamble to win back the group while buried in debt ended up claiming the lives of innocent people. Did Chairman Park Sam-koo reflect on his actions as he watched his group fall into the hands of creditors? Not at all. Instead , he was seized by a twisted obsession to reclaim the empire he had lost at all costs. At the end of 2015, Chairman Park established a private holding company called Kumho Enterprise and bought back the management rights for the holding company, Kumho Industrial, from the creditors for around 720 billion won. But where did Chairman Park, who was already buried in debt, get such a massive amount of cash—720 billion won? If you follow the source of this money, a shocking path of plunder is revealed. To pay off the debts of the private company he controlled, Chairman Park began to suck the lifeblood out of Asiana Airlines, which had been turning a healthy profit . He handed over Kumho Terminal, a prime asset fully owned by Asiana Airlines, to his private company for a bargain price of just 270 billion won. But this was merely a teaser for the greater tragedy to come. Chairman Park’s eyes then turned to in-flight meals, the food served to Asiana Airlines passengers. There was a German partner that had been supplying perfect in-flight meals for 15 years, but Chairman Park blatantly demanded that this partner pay off the debts of his private company. When the partner flatly refused, calling it an unfair demand, Chairman Park threw 15 years of trust in the trash and unilaterally terminated the contract. He then signed an unbelievable deal, granting a Swiss catering company exclusive rights to supply Asiana Airlines' in-flight meals for a staggering 30 years. What was the price paid for selling off the passengers 'meals for the next 30 years ? The condition was that the Swiss firm would purchase 160 billion won worth of Chairman Park’s private company bonds interest-free. It was essentially handing over company assets as a bribe to fill the private coffers of the owner family. To put it simply, it was no different from tearing essential parts out of a plane you are flying in to install a fireplace in your own bedroom. It was an attitude of "as long as my room is warm, I don't care if the plane crashes." The price for that ugly greed exploded on July 1, 2018, in the in-flight meal crisis that shocked the entire nation. When the new factory meant to supply the meals caught fire, Park Sam-koo's management team forced a tiny vendor that could barely make 3,000 meals a day onto an airline that needed 30,000. Logically, how could a small factory produce ten times its capacity? At the airport, planes took off one after another without meals, and passengers were left hungry and furious on board. And the very next day , a tragic disaster occurred as the head of a subcontractor, suffering from murderous delivery pressure and exploitation, took his own life. The greedy management of a single owner took the life of an innocent vendor and brought national shame to the skies around the world. On top of that, Chairman Park Sam-koo was caught illegally lending over 130 billion won by mobilizing nine subsidiaries, including Asiana Airlines, to save a company where he held a large stake. Ultimately, antitrust authorities imposed a heavy fine of 32 billion won and reported Chairman Park to the prosecution. In August 2022, the first trial court sentenced Chairman Park to 10 years in prison and had him taken into custody in the courtroom. However, in September 2023, the appellate court acquitted him of embezzlement and breach of trust due to lack of evidence , finding him guilty only of violating fair trade laws, reducing his sentence to 2 years and 6 months in prison with a 4-year suspension, and released him. The judicial ruling went to the Supreme Court, but the true colors of an owner family that sent planes without meals and drove subcontractors to the edge remained an indelible record in history . So, how was the final report card recorded for the two brothers who left the group under a 10 trillion won mountain of debt? The results of the paths the two brothers chose were cruelly and starkly divided. First, the downfall of the older brother, Chairman Park Sam-koo, who tried to soar by taking on a massive 10 trillion won in debt, was utterly miserable. The Kumho Asiana Group, which survived through excessive privatization and debt recycling, eventually fell into a swamp of chain collapses. In July 2018, Kumho Tire, the group's reliable cash cow, could not withstand its financial woes and was sold to China's Doublestar for 640 billion won. Half a century of manufacturing history, built by the founder with blood and sweat, was helplessly handed over to a foreign company. Then, in April of the following year, 2019, the inevitable finally happened. With accounting fraud controversies and depleted liquidity leading to extreme pressure from creditors, they raised the white flag, announcing the sale of Asiana Airlines, the group's symbol and heart. Those beautiful colorful airplanes were officially transferred to a subsidiary of their competitor, Korean Air, following final approval in December 2024. What was left for the older brother who had even lost his airplanes ? On February 27, 2025, the antitrust authorities made a shocking announcement that shook the landscape of South Korea's business world. As the Asiana Airlines subsidiaries were entirely stripped away, the total assets of Kumho Asiana Group shrank from its peak of 26 trillion won to a mere 3.4 trillion won. It meant that a staggering 87%of their assets had vanished without a trace. Ultimately, 38 years after the large business group designation system was introduced in 1987, the group was officially expelled from the list of large conglomerates. All that remained in the older brother's hands were Kumho Express, sitting on a mountain of debt after even selling off the Gwangju bus terminal site, and the mid-sized construction firm Kumho E&C, worth only around 400 billion won in market cap. Then what happened to the younger brother, Chairman Park Chan-koo, who had been cast out by his brother and left alone with the chemical company? Surprisingly, the exact opposite miracle unfolded. Kumho Petrochemical, which had entered a voluntary agreement with creditors in 2010, made a successful early exit from creditor management in just 2 years and 10 months, by December 2012, through grueling cost-cutting and an export drive. It was the result of focusing solely on the technology of their core business, without being distracted by octopus-like expansion to swallow up real estate or other companies like his brother did. They bet everything on one single well: the synthetic rubber they knew best. In particular, they focused bold, preemptive investments on NB latex, the key raw material for the thin medical gloves worn by doctors in hospital operating rooms and emergency rooms. By establishing an overwhelming production system capable of churning out 950,000 tons annually, they stood tall as the global No. 1 champion, capturing nearly 30%of the world market . And in 2021, amidst the pandemic that swept the globe, the younger brother's perseverance hit a miraculous jackpot. As demand for medical gloves skyrocketed, Kumho Petrochemical set an unprecedented record in a single year with 8.4 trillion won in revenue and 2.4 trillion won in operating profit. The operating profit margin alone exceeded a whopping 28%. The 2.4 trillion won in pure operating profit the younger brother earned in just one year was an astronomical sum, enough to wipe out the put-back option debt hole the older brother had once bet the entire group’s fate on. The younger brother used this massive amount of money to pay off the debts completely and slashed the company’s debt-to-equity ratio to an elite level in the 30%range. He even fought off a management coup from his nephew, who had joined forces with an activist fund , with the overwhelming support of shareholders, firmly establishing his firm as a conglomerate with 10 trillion won in assets. The current stock market valuation of Kumho Petrochemical is approximately 2.8 trillion won, which is more than four times larger than Kumho Construction, which remained with the older brother. One person wore flashy wings and rode a trapeze only to crash to the ground, while the other silently forged steel on the ground to build an impregnable fortress. The extreme paths taken by these two brothers. But this astonishing parallel theory wasn't just a story unique to the Kumho family. Surprisingly, this cruel divergence of brothers was not the first time such an event occurred in the history of our conglomerates. Let’s look back at the Tongyang Group crisis that shook South Korea’s capital market about a decade ago. At the time, the chairman of Tongyang Group held a reckless debt party to save his failing cement and construction subsidiaries. Even as the entire group was sinking, he indiscriminately churned out 1.3 trillion won worth of fraudulent commercial paper and corporate bonds without any ability to repay them. In the end, over 40,000 ordinary citizens and retirees lost the retirement funds they had sweated a lifetime to save. Amidst the blood and tears of the victims, Tongyang Group disintegrated, and its head was sentenced to seven years in prison, heading off to a cold cell. However, there was one family that flatly rejected and turned away from the tearful request to bail out Tongyang Group’s trillion-won deficit . It was Orion Chairman Dam Chul-gon and Vice Chairwoman Lee Hwa-kyung, who had previously separated with the confectionery business. People around them poured criticism on them for being heartless, asking how they could abandon blood relatives, but Orion silently focused only on their core business of Choco Pies and snacks. What was the result of that cold-blooded decision? While Tongyang Group was completely ruined and vanished into history, Orion pioneered overseas markets and grew dazzlingly into a global confectionery giant with 3.3 trillion won in revenue and over 550 billion won in operating profit as of 2025. They perfectly proved the cold law of the market that only those who firmly refuse to guarantee the debts of relatives and protect the roots of their own core business survive. Now, shall we return to the two objects we encountered at the start of this video? The colorful-winged airplane that powerfully kicked off the airport runway. And recall the thin blue latex gloves tightly fitted on the hands of doctors in every hospital operating room. The older brother tried to fly high on wings of 10 trillion won in debt, but his wings melted under the scorching sun, and he plummeted to the ground. However, the younger brother staked the company's entire fate on thin surgical rubber gloves, defended his ground, and built a 10-trillion-won conglomerate. The man who was blinded by greed for expansion and swallowed the financial time bomb known as a put-back option lost even the sky. On the other hand, the man who did not covet what others had but held firmly to the solid soil of his main business finally survived. The giant trap known as the "winner's curse" is opening its jaws before countless companies even at this very moment. The moment you forget your own capacity and build a splendid castle with unbearable debt, the bill for that greed inevitably comes to take away what you hold most dear.

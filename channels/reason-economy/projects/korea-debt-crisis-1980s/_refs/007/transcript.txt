There was a time when Korea was poorer than India. It wasn't even that long ago. The year was 1965. That year, the money one Indian person earned in a year was 121 dollars. A Korean person in the same year earned 109 dollars. That is what is written in the World Bank ledgers. And 60 years have passed since then. Now, it takes 14 Indians to earn what one of us makes. It’s not because India lacks money. India is currently the country with the third-highest number of billionaires in the world. Money has been pouring in. But that money stopped dead at a certain floor. You can see where the money stopped just by looking at the numbers. India is the world's sixth-largest economy. If you line up countries by the total money they earn in a year, only five countries are ahead of India. Countries like the U.S. or China. That money isn't spent by the country, but divided among the people living there. So, let's try dividing it by the number of people. If you rank them again by how much goes to each person, India falls to 142nd place. That's lower than its neighbor, Bangladesh. Let's put it in terms of a household. If you combine all the money the family brings in, they are the sixth wealthiest house in the neighborhood. But when you divide it by the number of people, they are 140th. It’s the same house, but the numbers differ this much. There are more than enough people to work. So, what is the problem? For a poor country to rise to a wealthy one, there is a sequence to follow. Like stairs. Korea climbed up those four steps. India is missing two of them. It's not that they couldn't climb them; they were never placed there in the first place. What were the two missing steps? And where do people go when the stairs come to an end? In the middle of the city, there is a neighborhood called Dharavi. Its area on the map is smaller than Yeouido. It's land you can walk around in a single lap. No one knows exactly how many people live inside. Some say 700,000, others say a million. There are just too many people to count. In the alleys, your shoulders touch when two people pass each other. Overhead, wires are tangled like spiderwebs, and below, they tan leather, melt plastic, and run sewing machines. This happens on land with a population density over 20 times that of Seoul's average. However, just over one wall of this neighborhood lies India's largest financial district. Glass-covered high-rise buildings house bank headquarters and rows of brokerage firms. You don't even need a car. It’s a distance you can cross on foot . On one side, sewage flows through the middle of the alley, and on the other side of the wall, glass elevators rise silently. What lies between them? There is nothing there. Just one wall. In the same city, there is a house. It’s 27 stories tall. At 173 meters high, it’s as big as a typical corporate building. Only one family lives there. Inside that house, there is a room where it snows. Machines in the ceiling spray snowflakes. Mumbai is a city where it doesn't snow. So, they just turned one room into winter. It takes 600 staff members on-site to run this one house. It cost 2 billion dollars to build this house. And there is a plan to clear out that neighborhood, Dharavi , and rebuild it. The plan is to put everyone living there into new homes. The project cost is 3 billion dollars. So, it comes down to this. One house is worth two-thirds of the money needed to rebuild a neighborhood for a million people. If this were just a story about one peculiar house, we could brush it off. But the country's entire ledger looks like that. In India, the top 1% holds 40%of the nation's total wealth. Even in terms of annual income, 22% belongs to them. The important thing isn't just the number, but when these numbers emerged. Inequality in India is at its highest since they started keeping records. There was a time when Britain ruled India. Even in the year with the widest gap during that era, the gap today is even wider. It has been nearly 80 years since the country regained its independence. But there is something strange here. Poverty in India has decreased. And by a very large margin. 170 million people escaped extreme poverty in just 11 years. That is according to World Bank figures. More than three times the population of our country has escaped the worry of daily meals. The bottom has risen. During the same period, the gap reached an all-time high. Poverty has decreased. But no one has moved up. They haven't climbed a step from the bottom; the bottom just became a little less cold. They can eat now, but their standing position remains the same. Then why couldn't anyone climb up? To see where the staircase is broken, we have to look from the bottom step. Let’s stand in front of a small field in rural India. The rice is knee-high, and there is a person bent over in between. This person is not the owner of this land. His father must have farmed someone else's land, too. Who the owner is was decided by a piece of paper long before this person was born. It was 1793. A system was created that handed over land ownership to those tasked with collecting taxes. About one-third of British-ruled Indian land was settled that way. Families whose names were on the paper became landowners, and those who used to till the soil became tenant farmers. The system itself disappeared long ago. The country changed, and all the documents were renewed. But only the paper was erased. One study superimposed those old boundary lines onto a map of India today. The lines were still alive. Regions that had transferred ownership to landlords still have lower yields today. There are fewer schools and fewer hospitals. The soil and rainfall are the same as in the village right next door. Yet, it is still the case. Whose name was written on the document 200 years ago? That is what determines the number of schools a village child can attend today. After independence, they took action. They set a limit on how much land a single person could own . The problem was what happened next. Collecting the excess land and redistributing it barely happened at all. The law remained on paper, and the fields stayed exactly where they were. Even now, less than five out of every 100 Indian farmers own nearly a quarter of the farmland. Around the same time, the Korean Peninsula also rewrote its land records. As late as 1941, only four out of ten households farmed their own land. By 1955, that number became seven. In the meantime, people working on others 'land had almost disappeared. Nine out of ten tenant farmers received land deeds with their names on them in just a few years. Japan and Taiwan followed a similar path around that time. I am not saying that Korea did a better job. Those who received those documents back then probably didn't know what they were stepping on to climb up. It’s just that stairs are built this way. There are those who step on the bottom rung and those who don't, and the difference only becomes clear much later. That is why this happens in India. A father worked on that field. The son also works on that field. The grandson is there in that field now, too. All three stepped on the same soil, but none of them owned it. If land ownership doesn't change, people cannot move. You need to own your land to sell it and leave, and you need a deed to use as collateral to borrow money. That is the seed money needed to go to the city. Without a deed, one bad harvest leads to debt, and once in debt, you are even less able to leave. That is how feet get stuck to the soil. However, it’s not that there were no people who walked out of that soil. There were people who packed their bags and went up to the city. The place where those people were supposed to arrive was already set. It is the factory. But those factories are not there. Today, 46 out of every 100 working people in India are engaged in farming. Yet, the share they produce throughout the year doesn't even make up a fraction of the nation's total pie . Even if they wake up at the same hour and work the same day, what they take home at night is bound to be different. How empty that space is. Looking at the share manufacturing takes in the national economy, Korea is at 26 percent. China and Vietnam are also around 20 percent. India is at 13 percent. Its neighbor, Bangladesh, is at 22 percent. India manufactures fewer goods than Bangladesh. And this is a country that ranks among the world's top six in total wealth. Furthermore, this number isn't even going up. Ten years ago, it was over 15 percent. It has actually gone down from there. In 2014, the Indian government made a grand announcement. "Make in India." They said they would boost the manufacturing share to 25 percent and create a massive number of jobs. Since then, until now. That number remains stagnant. Does it matter if there are fewer factories? Did India go straight to offices instead? You might think so. This is the heart of today’s story. Factories accept anyone. They accept those who couldn't attend school for long. They accept someone who just arrived from the countryside by train yesterday. Even if you can barely read, if you are fast with your hands, you can work there. They accept women, too. And older people as well. Because you can just learn on the job. Call centers aren't like that. Software companies are even less so. There are things you must be equipped with before you open those doors. These aren't places that take people and develop them; they are places that select those who are already fully developed. Factories were places that built people, while offices are places that select people who are already built. This one difference wipes out an entire staircase for a nation. According to one study, the situation is different for developing countries these days. Factory jobs decline much earlier, while they are still much poorer than countries in the past. The stepping stones disappear before there’s even a chance to climb them. Regarding India, this kind of talk has even emerged. The industries India excels at cannot employ most of its people. Do you know where the missing staircase is most clearly visible? It’s women. Only three out of ten Indian women work. In Korea and China, it’s closer to six. It falls far short of the world average, too. In the days when Korea, China, and Vietnam were busy making and selling goods. The hands that filled those factories were women's hands. In 1970, the product Korea exported the most was textiles. Fourteen percent of exports. Third on that list were wigs. Those wigs, made by attaching human hair strand by strand. One in ten was a wig. The hands that made them received a paycheck with their own name on it for the first time in their lives. That salary paid for siblings' tuition and became a home of their own. But those factories were never built in India. So, those hands had no place to go outside the home. On top of that, the number of hands needing work grows every year. Of course, India has an IT sector. That is a fact. IT and its related industries directly employ 5.8 million people. That’s enough people to fill an entire city. However, India’s population is 1.46 billion. If you divide it, it’s one in 250 people. For everyone else, being an "IT powerhouse" is a story from beyond the wall. No matter how high those glass buildings rise, there’s no way to feel that height in these alleys. "Why not just be one of those people?" you might ask. The problem is that there’s only one way to get there. Can't they just study? Many people say so. Because if there’s no land and no factories, all that’s left is a desk. There are actually people who have climbed that path. But the story changes when you count how many have actually made it. Let’s step into a primary school classroom in rural India . Third-grade children are sitting there. The teacher asks them to open a textbook from a grade below and read just one line aloud. Only one child in four can read it. The rest scan the words and keep their mouths shut. The situation is similar when it comes to numbers. Only one in three children can solve a subtraction problem. This is the result of surveying hundreds of thousands of children across 600 rural districts. They are in the classroom, but they haven't stepped onto the first rung of the ladder. If they can't step on it at nine, they won't at twelve either. Eventually, that opportunity disappears entirely. Now, let's look at the other extreme. The Indian Institutes of Technology. If you get in here, your entire life changes. With one diploma, you land a job in Silicon Valley and lift up your whole family. This gate is the most certain path to escaping poverty. Last year, over 1.5 million people applied for this exam. But there are only about 18,000 seats available. Eighty-five people line up for one to get in. And you only get two chances in your entire life. Once you submit your second answer sheet, that door never opens again. It means the rest of your life is determined at eighteen. There is one more lock on that door. All those good jobs run entirely in English. Resumes in English , interviews in English. When you walk into a meeting room, the meeting is in English too. Yet, only one in ten people can actually use that language. The rest cannot even get to the front door. It’s not that they fail the exam; they can’t even read the notice explaining where the exam is held. The country could just build more schools. It’s been over 60 years since they set a goal for how much of the national budget to spend on education. They have yet to meet that goal even once. One more thing is added to this. What carries real weight in India is the thousands of social divisions called Jati. Where you are from and what your family has done for generations determines your first job. That doesn't mean it lasts a lifetime. There is a study that looked into the period between the early 1980s and the late 2000s. It says the inheritance of education and income has narrowed to other social levels. It’s a gap at the starting line. So, the door does exist. The people who opened it and entered are the face of India today. However, the whole country is lined up in front of it, and there are only a few keys. There are two steps missing, and there’s only one ladder to climb up. Couldn’t it have been fixed? There were certainly opportunities to fix it. The problem was whose hands those keys were held in. For 40 years starting in 1951, you needed a government permit to build a new factory in India. Increasing production also required a permit. Changing the product you make also required a permit . Deciding where to build the factory also required a permit. So, who received those permits? Those with connections in the capital received them. Large business groups already had those connections, while others didn't even have a chance to open a factory door. It was labeled as a system, but it only did one thing in reality. Locking the door for those who were already inside. In 1991, this permit system was removed. It had an effect. The growth rate jumped from 6%to the 7% range. However, according to one study, the effect varied drastically from state to state. Some states saw an increase in factories, while others saw them shrink. As soon as the gates were opened, only the ones ready to run surged forward. The door opened to narrow the gap only widened it further. One thing remains unsolved even after opening the door. Factories in India don't grow large. In the U.S., a 40- year-old factory employs seven times more people than a brand-new one. In India, they are barely twice as big. U.S. factories become adults after 40 years, but Indian factories are still middle-schoolers after 40 years. Why don't they grow? The moment you hire over 100 people, you need government permission to let them go later. So, it's better to stop at ninety-nine. They cut off growth themselves right at the threshold. Of course, scholars debate how significant this threshold actually was. Still, it is clear that an invisible line was drawn in front of the factory yard. People enter these workplaces that cannot grow any further . In India, ten people go to work without their names being written anywhere. They are not on rosters, contracts, or insurance documents. It is no different for salary earners. More than half of those who get paid every month don't even have a single contract. Can't we just collect from the top and send it down? That method has already been proposed. It's not about taxing only the very wealthy. Every time it comes up, it gets pushed back by the same argument. Growth comes first. Let's make the pie bigger, then share it. The pie has truly grown larger. The problem is that for the larger share to come down, there must be a path. That passage has not been paved yet. No matter how much water there is, not a single drop reaches the bottom without a pipe. It just pools at the top. Stairs are a truly strange thing. You don't notice them while you're climbing. Because the ground under your feet is solid, you just keep walking. The person who knows best that stairs exist is the one who stops before them. Because there is nothing to step on. Let's go back to 1965. That year, the money a Korean earned from working all year was 109 dollars. If you break it down by month, it's less than 10 dollars. People earning that money left the fields and entered factories. They packed lunch at dawn to catch the bus and returned at night with the smell of oil on their clothes. With the money earned that way, they sent their children to school, and those children sat at office desks. One generation climbed exactly one step. A grandfather stepped on the first rung, and a father stepped on the next. The spot we stand on now is right on those two people's footprints. Yet, we have never once thought of those stairs as remarkable. We thought they had been there from the start. That if you study you get a job, if you work you get a house, and your child climbs one step higher than you. We believed that's just how the world is made. Stairs aren't naturally there. Someone had to lay down the wood and drive in the nails, one step at a time. If you don't place them, that space remains empty for 60 years.

In November 2023, a massive ship entered a gas terminal off the eastern coast of Australia. It was supposed to be loaded with gas frozen at over 160 degrees below zero before departing. However, the ship remained stuck at the pier, unable to move. All four of the ship’s onboard generators had shut down at once. It was a ship built at a Chinese shipyard. China is the country that builds the most ships in the world . But to summarize what the shipyards in that country once went through, it would be this. The more ships they churned out, the more debt they piled up. What is even stranger is the situation now. Even though over seven out of ten global shipbuilding orders went to China this year, it is the Korean shipyards that are actually smiling. Why are Korean shipyards, which received fewer orders, the ones doing better? To find that answer, let’s trace the journey of that ship that stopped at the Australian pier. About a week after the ship stalled, the Australian company Origin, which was shipping gas from that terminal, made an announcement. They stated that the ship had lost power and could not leave the pier. They added that they had also begun to reduce gas production . That pier was the only one available. It usually handled one gas ship per month, but because the stalled ship was blocking the only pier, no other vessels could enter. I heard that three gas carriers that arrived to load cargo had to drop anchor offshore and wait for their turn. In the meantime, the gas shipments were delayed three times. Eventually, they finally managed to move the ship on December 1st. According to local newspapers, it took seven tugboats and three pilots to guide the ship, barely detaching it from the pier after eight hours. What was even more ironic was the identity of the ship’s owner. One of the companies that co-owned the ship was the very Chinese firm buying the gas from that terminal. So, their own ship was blocking the gas they were supposed to be buying. But this ship was not the first case. Five years ago, a twin ship from the same shipyard had its engines stop off the coast of Papua New Guinea while carrying gas from Australia to China. That ship eventually had to be towed by another vessel. It was a new ship that had been at sea for less than two years. The cargo these ships carry is no ordinary freight. Professor Lee Jang-hyun, who teaches marine engineering at Inha University, told a newspaper as follows. He stated that if this gas leaks, it has the explosive power to wipe out a city, which is why technical expertise and safety are paramount. Therefore, you cannot entrust such ships to just any shipyard . If a ship stops in the middle of the ocean, the concern for the gas onboard and the crew outweighs the cost of the ship itself. The name of the ship that blocked the Australian pier was the Qingdao. The twin ship that was towed off the coast of Papua New Guinea was the Gladstone. Both ships were built at the Hudong-Zhonghua shipyard in Shanghai, China. Hudong-Zhonghua was once the only place in China capable of building these gas carriers. It was essentially the face of China's shipbuilding industry. Even after leaving the dock, the Qingdao stayed anchored off the coast for quite a long time. Only after the engine company technicians watched them completely reassemble one of the broken generators could they finally leave Australia on Christmas Eve. Even then, it ultimately had to be towed back to China for repairs. With sibling ships from that same "face" of the industry breaking down at sea in succession, the news must have reached those looking to commission ships. Neither the sailors who manned those ships nor the gas company officials waiting anxiously at the docks would have easily forgotten that shipyard's name. Hearing this, you might think the following. That shipyard must have run out of customers and closed its doors. With such rumors spreading across the seas, who would ever commission a ship there again? But that wasn't the case. In 2024, Qatar in the Middle East commissioned two ultra-large gas carriers from that very same Hudong-Zhonghua. In monetary terms , it was worth about 8 billion dollars. They say delivery of the completed ships will begin in 2028. The shipyard that built the broken ships ended up receiving an even larger order later on . Qatar couldn't have been unaware of the news about the Qingdao. The fact that they still placed the order means they believed the shipyard's capabilities had improved significantly in the meantime. The picture is the same when looking at the Chinese shipbuilding industry as a whole. From the start of this year through September, China took 76%of the global ship orders. South Korea took 15%. Moreover, the orders China received nearly doubled compared to the same period a year earlier. So, is the claim that China's shipbuilding is collapsing all a lie? It is half right and half wrong. Looking at this very moment, China is far from collapsing; it is firmly holding the top spot in the world. If you have heard that China's shipbuilding industry is ruined, that doesn't align with the current numbers. However, there is the other half. The price Chinese shipyards paid to reach that number one spot was not small. To understand what that journey was like, you have to look at the Chinese coast over a decade ago. In the winter of 2013, there was a village called Changqingchun on the eastern coast of China. It was a village built for shipyard workers right in front of the main gate of what was then China’s largest private shipyard, Rongsheng Heavy Industries. But that winter, what caught the eye of a foreign news agency reporter visiting the village were shops boarded up with wooden planks. They say that was the case for nine out of every ten shops. They said the police station and hospital doors were locked, and in the empty apartments, only a few discarded clothes and name tags remained. Mr. Xu, who ran a shop, was playing a game while waiting for customers who never came, and told the reporter this. "I only sell to the shipyard workers here.""If those people are gone, who am I supposed to sell to? " A worker in grease-stained coveralls said he hadn't received his September paycheck yet. Men stood in front of a lottery shop, smoking cigarettes and waiting to win. Even a shipyard executive confessed that the office atmosphere was very grim because no one knew what the company's plans were. That worker probably made it through each day thinking, "Maybe it will come in today, maybe tomorrow." Those of you who have experienced the hopelessness of not hearing anything after payday know what it's like, right? A few months earlier that summer, there were reports that hundreds of workers had blocked the shipyard’s main gate, demanding their overdue wages. Two years later, in December 2015, another shipyard went bankrupt, this time in Zhejiang Province. It was called Wuzhou Shipyard, and it wasn't just some local neighborhood shipyard. It was a state-owned shipyard with the government as its owner. An employee there reportedly told Chinese media that the shipyard had stopped work and had already entered bankruptcy proceedings three or four months prior. At the very top of the list of remaining debts were the wages of migrant workers. The laborers who had come from the countryside to sweat in the docks. Following that were unpaid debts to parts suppliers and taxes, and they said four unfinished ships were left sitting in the docks. The debt was about 170 billion won in our currency at the time, which was about 1.7 times their assets. It had become a hollow shipyard that couldn't pay its debts even if it sold everything. Secretary-General Chen of the China Association of the National Shipbuilding Industry called this the first bankruptcy of a state-owned shipyard in recent times. Have you ever thought that even a shipyard backed by the state could shut its doors? We also saw something similar in the winter of 1997. Watching big companies that we thought would never go under collapse one by one, we learned back then that having a big name doesn't guarantee survival. This wasn't just a story about one or two places. Around 2010, shipyards had sprung up along the coast like bamboo shoots after the rain. A foreign economic media outlet once counted their number at 1,647. But after a period of restructuring, Chairman Ren of Yangzijiang Shipbuilding said that in the past year , only about 100 shipyards had delivered ships or received orders. On a coastline where over a thousand places had set up cranes, only that many were actually still building ships and surviving. Even state-backed shipyards couldn't hold on just by receiving a lot of ships. But it wasn't just a lack of work that brought the shipyards down. There was a strange business model where even if you had work—no, the more work you took, the more you lost. In the spring of 2021, a man named He Xu, who was in charge of ship sales at the state-owned Dalian Shipbuilding Industry in the northern port city of Dalian, confided the inside story to a Chinese newspaper. He revealed that the price of the thick steel plates used to build ships had risen by 35%within the country. He reportedly added one more thing. He said that Chinese steel plates were more expensive than Korean ones, which was hurting their competitiveness. In China, a country famous for mass-producing goods cheaply, the steel plates for shipbuilding were actually more expensive than in Korea. In the same article, an executive at a state-owned shipyard in Wuhan stated that the speed at which steel prices were rising was more problematic than the amount, making it impossible to keep up. That spring, He Xu must have pulled out the contracts signed several years prior to review them. The price of the ships in the contracts remained the same, but the price of steel was jumping day by day. The more he tapped on his calculator, the more the losses must have mounted. In fact, Chinese shipyards that year suffered losses even after building and delivering the ships. Doesn't it seem strange to lose money after selling a ship? The reason lies in the way ships are bought and sold. The price of a ship is set on the day of the contract, but the vessel is not delivered until two or three years later. Even if steel prices spike in the meantime, the ship price remains fixed, so the shipyard bears the brunt of the increased costs. Since steel plates account for about 20%of the cost of a ship, it is not a small sum of money. It's easy to understand if you think of a caterer hired for a banquet. They agreed on a price in advance for a banquet happening months later in July, but when they arrived at the market, the price of meat had skyrocketed. They couldn't just go back to the banquet host and ask for more money. That difference comes straight out of the caterer’s own pocket. It would be even more painful for a caterer who had quoted a low price from the start. That’s because there wasn't much margin to begin with. Then why did Chinese shipyards quote such low prices ? If the docks are empty, both the workers and the machinery just sit idle . They figured that securing work, even at a slight loss, was better than shutting down. If the work stops, an entire shipyard town like Changqingchun goes dark, which probably made it even more desperate. But the even more painful part comes next. The buyer pays a portion of the ship's price to the shipyard before construction begins. This is so the shipyard can buy steel and hire workers. The problem is that they don't just hand over that large amount of money. There must be a promise that if the shipyard fails to build the ship, the bank will refund the money received in advance. This is what is known as a bank guarantee. Without this guarantee, it is difficult even to sign a contract. Back in the day, if you wanted to receive a large sum of money in advance in the neighborhood, someone senior had to vouch for you. But what if it's a household that is piled high with debt? No one will sign for them. The work is right in front of them, but because they lack one stamp, they can't take it . Shipyards are exactly the same. Banks are hesitant to provide guarantees to a shipyard with mounting losses. For Wuzhou Shipyard, where debts far exceeded assets, the threshold for bank loans would have been even higher. So, even if there is work, they can't take it, and because they can't, the debt only piles up. This is exactly what the saying "the more you pull, the deeper you get into debt" means. And the most painful part comes from the sea. There’s an old saying: "You get what you pay for." When the Qingdao ended up blocking a foreign port, or when the Gladstone had to be towed in the middle of the sea, those looking to commission ships probably thought of something similar. Of course, you can't blame the stopping of those two ships on just the price. Still, those two names likely stayed in the minds of the people who commission ships for a long time. But there is one more calculation left. Not all ships are the same. It becomes clear when you compare a noodle shop to a banquet feast. If you count by the bowl, no one can beat the noodle shop. Because they’re churning out noodles all day long without a break. But if you count by the price of a full table, the story changes. A banquet feast, which requires more labor and expensive ingredients, far exceeds the price of several bowls of noodles. Moreover, you can't prepare a banquet feast in just any kitchen. You need skilled hands, and the people who have tasted it once must return. China served a lot of noodles, while Korea prepared a banquet feast. It’s even clearer when you look at the numbers. If you count the ships ordered last year, China had nearly six times as many as Korea. However, when measured by the workload required to build one ship, a Korean ship was nearly twice as large as a Chinese one. The noodle shop won on the number of bowls, but the banquet feast won on the size of the table. That’s not to say Korea was unscathed by the 2021 steel plate price turmoil. In the first half of that year, the three major Korean shipyards also saw a combined loss of about 3 trillion won by preemptively accounting for the rising cost of steel plates. It was a year when both countries were hit equally hard by steel prices. It was after that that they diverged. The choice of which ships to accept afterward is what set the two countries apart. So, you might ask, why don't Chinese shipyards just raise their prices? It’s not as easy as it sounds . Customers who only come for the low price will leave the moment you raise it to find a cheaper place. A noodle shop can't just raise its prices to match a banquet feast overnight. Now that you understand the calculation, this is where the real story begins. It was October 2014, at an airport in Moscow covered in thick fog. A private jet taking off late at night collided with a snowplow. On that plane was Christophe de Margerie, CEO of the French energy company Total. The CEO passed away along with three crew members. He was the one who drew the blueprint for the project to extract gas from the Yamal Peninsula in the Russian Arctic. It was a project that required transporting gas extracted from ice-covered land across frozen seas. That sea was not a place where ordinary ships could travel. It had to be a gas carrier capable of breaking through 1-meter-thick ice on its own. A single Korean shipyard took on the responsibility for all 15 of those ships. That was Daewoo Shipbuilding& Marine Engineering, now known as Hanwha Ocean. The name given to the first ship was Christophe de Margerie. It was built at the Geoje Okpo Shipyard, bearing the name of the man who sketched the project but left us too soon. They said the front and rear of the ship, which collide with the ice, were wrapped in special steel plates three times thicker than normal. The price was also different. About 320 million dollars per ship. That was about 1.6 times the cost of a standard gas carrier. It was the biggest and most demanding order among all the projects. The ship, completed in Okpo in the winter of 2016, was handed over to its owner only after finishing sea trials in the Arctic. At the naming ceremony in June 2017, Daewoo Shipbuilding&Marine Engineering CEO Jung Sung-leep said this. He stated that although polar development was difficult due to the lack of transport routes, Daewoo’s technology opened the era of full-scale Arctic sea navigation. That claim was confirmed on the sea that summer. The ship departed Norway and reached Boryeong, South Chungcheong Province, in just 22 days after crossing the ice-covered Arctic Ocean. It is said to be the first merchant ship to cross the Arctic Ocean without the help of an icebreaker. The Russian company operating the ship said that, unlike before, the route became much shorter because they no longer needed an icebreaker. If a ship stops in the middle of a frozen sea, it is not easy to call for a tugboat. So, the client likely prioritized finding a ship that wouldn't break down over the price. You don't entrust a massive project to just anyone. Especially when it comes to a ship that has to break through ice. In November 2024, another visitor came to Geoje. It was Saad al-Kaabi, the Minister of Energy of Qatar, a gas-rich country in the Middle East. The minister personally came to Geoje to name each of the four gas carriers built by the Korean shipyard. He reportedly thanked Samsung Heavy Industries for their excellent craftsmanship and quality, and Hanwha Ocean for their dedication. There was a reason the minister traveled all the way to Geoje. Even when air travel was blocked due to COVID-19 in the summer of 2020, Qatar had already secured ship construction slots with three Korean shipyards via video conference. Qatar decided to commission over 100 new gas carriers, and out of the first 65 vessels ordered, Korean shipyards secured 54. In the next round, they secured 44 more. Of course, Qatar also placed some orders with China. Even so, the number of ships won by Korean shipyards was nearly 100. You only entrust your next banquet to someone after you've tried them once and been satisfied. The results were clearly reflected in the ledgers. While global shipbuilding orders fell by 27%last year, Korea’s actually grew by 8%. In a year when the world shrank, Korea moved in the opposite direction and increased its workload. The profit margins changed as well. In the second quarter of this year, HD Korea Shipbuilding&Offshore Engineering made a profit of over 18 won for every 100 won worth of ships sold. Can you imagine the difference compared to 2021 , when they were losing money even after delivering ships due to the price of steel plates? One more thing to look at is the price. The price of a gas carrier from China is said to be about 8%cheaper than one from Korea. Since these are deals involving hundreds of billions of won per ship, that’s not a small difference. Yet, shipowners still line up in front of the more expensive Korean shipyards. Because if they try to save a few pennies and end up stranded like the Qingdao-ho, the losses would far exceed the savings. Moreover, most of the parts that go into Korean ships are made by our own hands. It’s like using our own dishes and cutlery for the entire banquet. Of course, it would be an exaggeration to say that Korea has taken all the world's ship orders. China still has a much higher number of total vessels. However, the fact that the most expensive and demanding banquet orders come to Korea is proven by the clients who visit Geoje themselves. At this point, it seems Korean shipyards have nothing left to worry about. If the story ended here, it would just be a simple tale of how well the Korean shipbuilding industry is doing lately. The kind of story you might have heard once or twice on the evening news. You've heard about ships breaking through Arctic ice and the Qatari minister visiting Geoje, so you might think you've heard it all. But that is not the end of the story. If you look at one figure that came out this year, the true strength of Korean shipyards actually appears in reverse. On the surface, it’s a number that makes it look like Korea lost, but when you look deeper, the reality was the opposite. The real story starts now. In the first half of this year, 90 gas carriers were ordered worldwide. Out of those, China took 52, and Korea took 37. It is said that this is the first time China has surpassed Korea in this sector. When this news was reported last August, the word "emergency" even appeared in the news headlines. Thinking that even the most expensive and demanding banquet orders are now going to China. It's understandable to have such concerns. But when you look at the reasons, the story flips completely. South Korean shipyards already had most of their docks filled with orders for delivery through 2029. Even if they wanted to take new ship orders, they simply didn't have the space to build them. It wasn't that they were defeated. It was that they couldn't take orders because they were out of space. What would you do if a restaurant was fully booked until 2029 and more customers showed up ? You would have no choice but to apologize and tell them to try another place. Customers in a rush can't wait years for their turn. The "other place" those customers went to was a Chinese shipyard. With Chinese shipyards now reportedly selling delivery slots for 2030, it’s a time of a global shortage of shipbuilding capacity. Moreover, the Korean shipbuilding industry reportedly saw China’s increasing volume as an opportunity to demand better prices since the beginning of this year. After all, a restaurant overflowing with customers has no reason to offer discounts. Here, we can see three reasons why the Korean shipbuilding industry is formidable. First, Korea is now picking and choosing. Since slots are filled through 2029, they can choose ships that offer the right price and better profit margins. HD Korea Shipbuilding& Offshore Engineering reported a profit margin of over 18%on sales. In the same second quarter, Hanwha Ocean and Samsung Heavy Industries also exceeded a 10%profit margin. All three major shipbuilders have become companies with double-digit profit margins. This is the first reason they are formidable. Second, the United States has joined hands with Korea. There is a shipyard in Philadelphia, USA, that is owned by Hanwha. Last spring, a U.S. newspaper visited the site and found that one of the welding apprentices, Mr. Gillo, was originally a hairdresser. Mr. Gillo revealed that his father said this to him. "You went from using a razor to using a welding gun." Mr. Zanolli, who worked as a hotel waiter, said the work here feels more stable with less fear of being laid off. There was also Mr. Dono, who quit his job at an Indiana car factory to return to his hometown of Philadelphia. Americans have started to look at shipyards as a source of jobs again. Dozens of technicians from Korea are working at that shipyard, teaching over 170 apprentices. It means the expertise of Korean shipyards has crossed over to U.S. coastal shipyards. Last August, President Trump said at the White House that Korea is very good at building ships. He stated they would buy ships from Korea and also have them built by American hands in the U.S. Around that time, they promised to use $ 150 billion of the planned investment in the U.S. for shipbuilding cooperation. And this July, the Korea-U.S. Shipbuilding Cooperation Center opened in Washington. At that event, where the executives of the three Korean shipyards stood side by side, U.S. Secretary of Commerce Lutnick said this. They won't evaluate based on how many meetings were held or how many agreements were signed. You mean they'll evaluate based on the number of ships built in the U.S.? It means they want to see results in ships rather than just words. It also means the U.S. knows exactly who has the skill to build those ships. Conversely, the U.S. government started charging fees on ships built in China when they enter U.S. ports starting last October. In less than a month, the leaders of the two countries met and delayed these fees for one year. That grace period ends next month, and the U.S. government has now proposed pushing it back for another two months. It hasn't been finalized yet. In the meantime, something happened to a Korean shipyard right in the middle of the two nations. Last October, China placed five U.S. subsidiaries of Hanwha Ocean on a sanctions list. That included the Philadelphia shipyard. These sanctions were also delayed for one year, starting on the same day as the fees. It also means that Korean shipyards have gained such weight in U.S. waters that another country felt the need to single them out. Third, the size of each individual ship is still different . If you count the ships ordered this year by number, China has more than seven times as many as Korea. However, if measured by the volume of work per ship, one Korean ship is still nearly 1.5 times larger than a Chinese ship. China may be serving more bowls of noodles, but Korea is still the one setting the banquet table. But aren't we eventually falling behind if we give up all the volume? Absolutely not. When the banquet table is fully booked until 2029, you don't really count the number of noodle bowls. It's not about anxiously waiting for customers, but being in a position to choose which customers to accept. Of course, it's not all rosy. In the past, Hudong-Zhonghua was the only shipyard in China that could build these gas carriers. But now, that number has increased to five or six. China is also learning how to set the banquet table one step at a time. However, knowing how to set a table and having customers trust you enough to entrust it to you are two different things. That trust is only built when each and every ship doesn't stop in the middle of the ocean . Twelve years after that winter when the shipyard waited for customers who didn't come, a movement to relight the fires at the shipyard in Changqingcun village began this January. That shipyard, which once had 40,000 employees, closed down under the weight of debt and failed several attempts at revival. However, according to reports by Chinese economic media, a shipbuilding company in Anhui province has decided to lease the facility to build large ships. It means they will take orders even by renting empty facilities because the large Chinese shipyards are packed with work. It's just that the price paid when steel prices, bank guarantees, and the trust of those entrusting the ships were shaken was high. Even China is being cautious. The China Association of the National Shipbuilding Industry warned that expanding facilities too quickly increases risk. As shipyards that had closed are reopening one after another, there are growing concerns that ships might end up in oversupply again. That is why being selective about orders is even more important. When there is a surplus of ships, the ones that see their prices cut first are inevitably those that anyone can build. There are concerns on the ground in Korea as well . Last summer, a newspaper reporter met Mr. Hong, a subcontractor and painter in Geoje, who said that at 43, he was still the youngest member of his team for the fourth year in a row. Mr. Hong reportedly said this. "Young people don't come to Geoje anymore.""It's the same line of work, but doesn't other work pay better?" Yet, it is these people who are currently setting the table for the feast. Whether it's an icebreaker for the Arctic or a ship heading to Qatar, it is ultimately those people who weld the steel plates and paint the hulls before sending them out to sea. Still, one thing is clear. Customers will always seek out the builders who create reliable ships that don't break down at sea, at a fair price. Whether that holds true can be seen by looking back at that Australian pier. I'm talking about the "Qingdao" that was blocking the only pier in November 2023. Australia's maritime safety agency banned the ship from entering for 180 days. They said it was the ninth such ban issued that year alone. They even took issue with how the captain and the ship management company responded during the repairs. The saying "you get what you pay for" applies even to ships worth hundreds of billions of won. Anyone commissioning a ship would have a question they want to ask even before the price. It is the question of whether this ship will stop working in the middle of the ocean. You have already seen the answer Korean shipyards provided to that question in the Arctic ice and at the naming ceremonies in Geoje. So, it is no surprise that Korean shipyards, despite receiving fewer orders, are the ones smiling. Korea didn't receive fewer ships; it was being selective. But this position wasn't achieved for free. Mr. Kim, who runs a snack shop in Geoje and was 72 last summer, recalled to a newspaper that back in the 2000s, the streets were packed with young people even at 5 a.m. The long winter came to those streets in the mid-2010s. That was when China pushed in with low prices, orders at Korean shipyards dried up, and Daewoo Shipbuilding& Marine Engineering faced multi-trillion won losses. On April 10, 2016, one of the partner companies working inside Daewoo Shipbuilding&Marine Engineering closed its doors. Mr. Lee, a 32- year-old who had worked there for over five years, said he lost his job that very day. That spring, people flocked to the Geoje Employment Center to inquire about unemployment benefits, and the word "restructuring" was mentioned in every evening news broadcast. Are there some of you who listened to that news and worried about your children or relatives living in Geoje and Ulsan? There were people who stood by the docks even through that winter. Master Ko Yun-yeol of Hyundai Heavy Industries in Ulsan joined in 1978 and worked with steel for 40 years . And last year, his son, technician Ko Min-chul, became a Republic of Korea Master in the field of cutting and joining steel plates. They say it was the first time a father and son have both become masters in the shipbuilding industry. There are also those who have come from afar. It was the price for enduring that long winter. In the future, when you see shipbuilding news, look at how much they received per ship rather than how many ships they secured. If you look at the price of the feast rather than the number of noodle bowls, you will quickly see which country’s shipyards are smiling. And please also remember that behind that feast are the people of Geoje and Ulsan who endured the long winter.

This means there are that many neighborhoods where there are simply no stores to buy newly released manga books. Hearing only this much, it seems true that the nation of manga is in decline. Even amidst the trend of shrinking paper, the overall market has continued to grow. The weight that manga holds in the publishing industry has not diminished either. So, who was the first to take a seat in front of those three counters? Was it the major Japanese publisher that has produced the most manga? A Korean company quietly stepped into that line. How did that company start? In April 2016, a manga-themed store opened its doors in Japan. Its name was Piccoma. It was created by Kakao Japan, the Japanese subsidiary of Kakao located in Minato Ward, Tokyo. It was the current Kakao Piccoma. The start was humble. Three Japanese publishers submitted their works. The number of titles uploaded was only eight. It wasn't even enough to fill a single shelf. What could one accomplish with eight titles in the home of manga? It was far too light for the load they had brought. Sales were even smaller. According to CEO Kim Jae-yong, there were days when daily transaction volume for the iPhone section was as low as 200 yen. The one-day revenue I mentioned earlier was around this time. On the Android side, there were days when we didn't earn a single penny. I am telling you that there were days when we set up a single stall in the middle of a major bookstore in a foreign country and no one opened their wallet all day long. However, this app was not the first Korean comic app to enter Japan. There was an app that arrived about a few weeks earlier. It was Line Manga, a Naver affiliate, launched in 2013. Naver's Japanese comic business subsequently consolidated into Line Manga. The path Line Manga chose was one that respected Japanese publishing culture. Japanese readers have long purchased comics by the volume. Therefore, Line Manga centered on single volumes and maintained that method for quite some time. Since adopting the publishers 'familiar methods made the process of acquiring works less unfamiliar, there was a reason for that choice. The mainstream of Japanese digital comics was not much different. It was a method where paper books were transferred directly onto the screen, and users purchased volumes one by one by flipping through the pages. Since multiple chapters are bundled into a single volume, readers were essentially buying several chapters at once. That habit is quite deeply ingrained. In one survey asking Japanese manga readers which format they read at least once a week, 64.5% read side-scrolling comics. 35.7%read vertically, and 7%read vertically. Side-scrolling was nearly twice as popular. Picoma took the exact opposite path. From the start, they sold chapters individually rather than by volume. On top of that, they added a system where the next chapter was unlocked for free if the reader waited one day. In a country where buying by volume is the norm, they opened a store that sold books broken down into individual chapters. The calculation changes for the reader as well. If you buy by volume, you pay upfront for later chapters you aren't interested in yet, but with chapters sold individually, you only need to buy the exact chapter you are curious about right now. From the seller's perspective, it is also a structure where the opportunity to sell a volume, which used to be once, becomes available for every single purchase. There was the side that entered first and adapted to the Japanese model, and the side that entered late with a completely different approach. One bet on familiarity, while the other bet on novelty. Which one was right? At that time, no one could answer that question . However, a small signal began to appear starting that summer. By the end of July, Picoma's daily users exceeded 10,000. This happened just over three months after its launch. Every day, 10,000 people stopped in front of that unfamiliar platform. By the end of that year, the number of users surpassed 160,000. It was a figure achieved before the app, which had launched in the spring, even passed the end of the year. But users don't equal money, do they? In a country where content was sold by the volume, how could an app that released chapters one by one—and even then, for free if you waited— make money? It seems like releasing content for free wouldn't generate revenue. However, there is quite a sophisticated calculation hidden within this strategy. The rule is simple: if you watch a chapter and wait 23 hours, the next chapter unlocks for free. This means you can follow along one chapter a day without paying a single penny. Making you open the app again at a similar time tomorrow—that is the habit this rule cultivates. The problem is that the story cuts off at the moment you are most curious. If the next chapter is locked from the previous one, where the protagonist is in crisis, can you wait until tomorrow? Only those who don't want to wait unlock it using coins. One coin is worth 1 yen, which is about 9.5 won in our currency. The number of coins required per unit varies by work. The system involves buying coins in advance to stock up and taking them out one by one whenever you are curious. It is similar to the difference between buying a month's worth of newspapers at once versus buying one copy every morning at a newsstand. Even for the same newspaper, the form of the money coming in is completely different. This difference becomes even clearer when you look at how the money is received. In the past, it was money paid all at once on the day you went to the bookstore. Now, it has become money that comes in a few coins every day you open your phone. The days when revenue is generated have shifted from occasionally to every day. That is why it is evaluated as a model that has transformed the habit of buying and reading individual volumes into a habit of reading a little bit every day. The vertical comic format, where scenes scroll down one panel at a time, suited the short pace well. However, vertical comics themselves were only at a level that could be considered the sole focus of the Japanese digital comic market in a survey conducted a few years ago. In essence, the weapon this app brought was closer to a method of generating revenue than to the new screen itself. At this point, you might be thinking: Did Japanese publishers fail to create such apps because they lacked the technology? That is not the case. Japanese publishers were already operating their own apps. The "store" known as an app existed, but the way it sold items resembled paper books. What was different was not the technology, but the method of selling. The payment system, which unlocked the next chapter after waiting a day, was something that did not exist in Japan, and Picoma was introduced as the first model of its kind. However, around this time, something happened in an unexpected place. The stage was a pirate site. There was an illegal comic site called "Manga Village" with 98.92 million monthly users. This meant that nearly 200 million people were reading comics without paying. That site closed down in 2018. Where did all those readers who used to read for free go? There are no statistics to track exactly where those readers went. However, the year after the site closed, Japanese electronic publishing grew by 24%. It is equivalent to earning 124 yen where they used to earn 100 yen. It was also at that time that digital comics surpassed paper comics for the first time. Digital comic sales exceeded half of the 259.3 billion yen manga market for the first time. From that point on, the scales tipped toward the screen.

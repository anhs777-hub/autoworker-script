At a Comic-Con event in Arizona, USA. At this massive comic book convention, something incredible happened. An unknown author sold out his entire stock of books. A staggering 810 copies . The author's booth was completely empty before the event even ended. And that was for three years in a row. But there is something even more surprising . The protagonist of this hero comic isn't a fictional character with superpowers, but a real figure from 400 years ago. It’s Admiral Yi Sun-sin of the Joseon Dynasty. However. When he first brought this book around, every American publisher turned him away. They said stories from Asia would never sell, so not a single one would accept it. Yet that very book went on to sell 250,000 copies. Marvel and DC. Right in the heart of the U.S. market, where these two companies dominate the revenue. Why on earth are Americans so enthusiastic about the story of a Korean admiral they didn't even know? If you watch until the end, you'll learn how Admiral Yi Sun-sin, who couldn't even get past a publisher's doorstep, sold 250,000 copies in the home turf of Marvel and DC. I mentioned 810 copies in three days, right? That was just for one single event. He has stood in front of his booth like that at over 120 events for 16 years. At times, he sells as many as 1,500 copies at a single event. According to the author, 250,000 copies have passed from hand to hand in that way. But what makes this number truly terrifying is where they were sold. When $ 100 worth of comics are sold at an American comic shop, Marvel takes $ 37 and DC takes $ 26. Together, the two companies account for 63%. Spider-Man, Batman, and Superman all belong to these two houses . Think of a convenience store beverage fridge. There are ten shelves, and six and a half of them are packed with drinks from those two companies. Hundreds of other companies are fighting for the remaining three and a half shelves. Customers always reach for what they are used to in the shelves they always look at. But this man? He couldn't even make it onto those three and a half shelves. He couldn't even open the fridge door once . In the end, he stood outside the fridge with a cooler box and sold them himself. And he sold 250,000 copies that way. There was no company to run ads for him, and no publisher to back him up. And the name of this book's protagonist is actually inside your wallet, too. Did it sell this well from the very beginning? It was the exact opposite. There wasn't a single publisher across the United States willing to take the book. The man who created this book is named Onrie Kompan . He is an American comic book artist living in the Chicago suburbs. He is neither of Korean descent nor an expatriate. Every time he knocked on a publisher's door with his manuscript, the answer he received was exactly the same. It won't sell because it's an Asian story. It wasn't just one or two places. Every door I knocked on said the same thing. The reason for rejection wasn't the book's content, but that the protagonist was Asian. Not a single person was even curious about who Yi Sun-sin was. Years later, Onrie Kompan shared those days like this. People told me I was crazy. An American trying to publish a comic about an Oriental general from 400 years ago— it’s no wonder he heard that. You might think, if publishers won't take it, why not just put it in shops? It’s not as easy as it sounds. The U.S. comic market structure is just like a department store. Imagine this. You want to put your homemade kimchi into a department store's food section. You're confident in the taste. But the manager won't even open the lid and says, "Our customers don't look for this." That's one word. And that's the end of it. No matter how delicious it is, it can't even get near the shelf. In the U.S. comic market, distributors play that exact role. Distributors have to pick it for a book to land on shelves in comic shops nationwide. Kompan's book even made it onto the distributor's list once. But shops wouldn't order it, saying it was about foreign subject matter. With no orders, the distributor eventually gave up on it. Blocked once at the publisher's door, and again at the shop's door. Most retailers won't stock my book. These are Kompan's own words. He had the book, but nowhere to put it. So where did he get the money to print the books? To cover production costs, Kompan worked for 10 years at a financial firm he hated. By day he calculated other people's money, and by night he held onto his manuscript of the 400-year-old Joseon navy. He was basically funding his dream with the job he detested most. He set aside his monthly salary for printing costs, pouring enough money into the book to shake his family's finances. Even after pouring all that in, not a single penny had gone into his own pocket from the project. Publishers reject him, shops won't take it, and money only flows out . An average person would have packed their bags already. Anyone could see it was a fight he should have folded. But this man didn't quit. Why on earth was an American so obsessed with a Joseon general from 400 years ago? It started with a single drama DVD. Around 2005, a college roommate handed him a bundle of drama DVDs. He said it was a Korean historical drama. The title was " Immortal Admiral Yi Sun-sin," a 104- episode series aired on KBS. Most people would watch a few episodes and turn it off. At 104 episodes, it would take over three months to finish watching one a day. But this man watched it to the end. And after that, he couldn't find his way out. A general from 400 years ago in a far-off land completely turned the life path of a Chicago man around. In fact, Onrie Kompan was a Marvel kid through and through. He says he dreamed of becoming a comic artist after seeing a Spider-Man cover at age four. In college, he fearlessly started his own publishing company. What was the result ? He put out one terrible book, by his own admission, and closed down right after. His dream of being a comic artist was crushed, in a way. Yet, such a man became genuinely and deeply hooked on a Korean historical drama. All one hundred episodes of it, no less . Kompan describes his feelings at the time like this. "The superhero market is already saturated." He wanted to draw a real-life hero anyone in the world could relate to, and that was Yi Sun-sin. The world is full of fake heroes in capes, but the spot for a real-life hero was empty. He even had a blueprint. It was Frank Miller's comic, 300. It’s a story about 300 Spartan warriors blocking a massive Persian army. Kompan’s calculation was simple . A story of a few holding off a massive army transcends nationality. So , what about the story of a Korean general who held off 133 ships with just 13? He figured if he told it that way, the world would recognize it. From then on, it was almost the life of a scholar. For two years, he dove into the Nanjung Ilgi, the Imjin Jangcho, and the Jingbirok. In 2008, he flew all the way to Korea. He walked Asan and Jinhae himself and sought advice from Professor Jeong Byeong-hoon of Sunchon National University and military officials. It's rare for someone to go this far just from watching a drama. And finally, in December 2009, the 24- page first issue was released. It took over four full years since he first watched the drama. Among the superheroes in tights, a Korean general in armor set sail for the first time. He didn’t make the book alone. The story was co-written by David Anthony Kraft. He’s a veteran who wrote The Defenders and Savage She-Hulk for Marvel in the 70s and 80s. Italian artist Giovanni Timpano handled the art , while Argentinian artists did the colors and lettering. It’s a Korean general planned by an American, written by a Marvel veteran, drawn by an Italian, and colored by Argentinians. That’s practically a multinational allied fleet. Even though there wasn’t a single Korean person among those making it. His plan was ambitious , too. Three trilogies of four volumes each—12 books in total. And he was going to print every single one with his own money. Here is why that is so daunting. The moment you print, you’re already losing money. Whether it sells or not, the printing house bill doesn't wait for you. If it doesn't sell, what piles up in the warehouse isn't paper, but cash. If you have a publisher, the company shares that risk. Self-publishing is holding the key to that warehouse all by yourself. So, where should you sell the books you’ve printed? If shops won't carry them, just go to the marketplace. Try to imagine a traditional five-day market. Do you remember the scene where snack vendors and medicine peddlers would raise their voices on market day? Peddlers without shops would travel from village to village following the market dates. There are marketplaces like that in the U.S. too. It’s exactly Comic-Con. It’s a place where tens of thousands of people gather with wallets in hand whenever it opens. You pay for your spot, set up your booth, and when the market closes, you pack up and drive to the next one. You stand at the booth for three whole days, repeating the same story hundreds of times, and restock the boxes at night. Onrie Kompan attended 15 to 20 such events every year. That’s how he sold 15,000 copies by hand. But there was one problem. Americans don’t know who Yi Sun-sin is. Nobody buys a snack they’ve never heard of. That’s why grocery stores have sampling corners. What Kompan set up was essentially an Yi Sun-sin sampling corner. "400 years ago, there was a general who held off 133 ships with just 13." This one sentence is the sample piece for them to taste. It’s exactly that one piece on a toothpick they hand you at a tasting booth. He says this line to every passerby, all day long, for three days straight. People who get a taste pick up the book, and those people bring others along. In 2013 alone, thousands of copies were sold. It was a sell-out at 14 consecutive events held across the U.S. and Canada. When the shelves are empty, he reorders from the printer, loads new boxes, and leaves for the next market. In 2014, cumulative sales surpassed 32,000 copies. Yet, the name was nowhere to be found on the U.S. comic industry map. It was a book that sold out at events but never appeared on any industry sales charts. That’s because it bypassed stores and was sold only from hand to hand. It was, in a sense, a legend of the marketplace known only to those in the know. In 2015, there came a moment when he really faltered. The Kickstarter campaign launched to fund the next book failed to reach its goal. What is Kickstarter, you ask? It’s a way to raise production costs by receiving advance funding via the internet. If the funding source dries up, there is no next book. It was a situation where the dream he had held onto for 10 years while working a job he hated could come to a sudden halt.

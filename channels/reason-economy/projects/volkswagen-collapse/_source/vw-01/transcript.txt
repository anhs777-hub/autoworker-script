On the 3rd, the Volkswagen board unanimously approved a massive restructuring plan. Titled "Future Plan 2030," it aims to cut 50,000 more jobs, halve the number of models by 2035, and reduce specification and option combinations by 75%. However, large-scale cuts at Volkswagen are hardly newsworthy at this point. It’s just now confirmed, but we’ve covered this multiple times, and with the media highlighting it as well, many of you are likely already aware of the crisis Volkswagen is facing. So, is Volkswagen just going to fail without any countermeasures? In reality, that’s unlikely. The reason Volkswagen is going to such lengths with this massive restructuring is, in a word, a "step back to take two steps forward." They plan to squeeze out every bit of capital from this restructuring to bet on two major future plans. The press release states that the Volkswagen Group is systematically customizing platforms, electrical/electronic architecture, driver assistance systems , and software to meet the specific needs of the Western and Eastern hemispheres. In short, they are placing one bet on China and another on the US. Volkswagen’s survival depends on hitting the jackpot with these bets. Whether this will succeed is what we’ll summarize for you today, focusing on the core points. First, Volkswagen has placed its first bet on China. This is because China is an incredibly important market for Volkswagen. Looking at data from the late 2010s to 2020, it’s clear how vital the Chinese market was; in 2018, the Volkswagen Group sold 10.83 million vehicles globally. Of that total, 4.21 million, or 38.9%, were sold in China. In 2019, while Volkswagen sold 10.97 million vehicles worldwide, China accounted for 4.23 million, maintaining a 38.6%share. In 2020, even as total global sales dropped to 9.3 million due to COVID-19, China still sold 3.85 million units, pushing its share up to 41.3%. So, during that period, Volkswagen was a company that sold four out of every ten cars globally in China . It is an undeniable fact that their dependence on China was extremely high. But how has that changed in five years? Looking at last year’s figures, out of 8.98 million global sales, China accounted for 2.69 million—a drop of over 10%to about 29%—representing a loss of 1.54 million units compared to five years ago. 1.54 million units is equivalent to the entire annual sales volume of the Korean automobile market simply vanishing. Moreover, this evaporation of sales is ongoing, as Volkswagen's China sales in the first half of this year reportedly dropped by 25.9%compared to the same period last year. It is said to be the lowest figure since 2010, and while the stagnation of the Chinese domestic auto market, which saw passenger car sales fall by over 20%year-on-year, certainly played a part. However, Volkswagen still performed roughly 6%worse than the market, so it's fair to say that while everyone in China is struggling, Volkswagen is struggling a bit more. In any case, the Chinese market was the most important in the world to Volkswagen, and its reliance on this market was extremely high, yet it is a clear fact that sales there are dropping by hundreds of thousands of units every year. The Volkswagen Group can only regain its momentum if it finds a breakthrough in China by any means necessary. So, Volkswagen has started to bet heavily on China, and the first of those bets is the establishment of a Chinese research and development center. They founded a company called Volkswagen China Technology Company (VCTC) in Hefei, Anhui Province, and the scale of the investment is truly massive. It started with 1 billion euros in 2023, and they invested an additional 2.5 billion euros in April of the year before last. Combined, that's 3.5 billion euros, which is over 5.46 trillion won in our money invested into a single research center in China. It has become Volkswagen Group's largest development center outside of Germany, so what is the core purpose of this VCTC? It’s that they no longer have to ask the German headquarters about every little thing. The traditional Volkswagen method was that when the China team planned a car, they had to report it to the German headquarters, wait for approval, proceed with planning in China, and go through the German purchasing department to buy parts; due to time zone differences and bureaucracy, a single decision could take weeks. In the meantime, Chinese companies like Xpeng, Li Auto, and NIO operate at "China Speed," where if they decide to make a car today, work begins tomorrow. This is exactly why new cars were popping out every 18 to 24 months. So, Volkswagen created VCTC with a strategy to handle everything from decision-making to development right there, without needing to go back and forth to Germany for cars made and sold in China. They even built a supplier park in Hefei spanning 450,000 square meters—roughly 63 soccer fields— where they brought in 1,000 local Chinese parts suppliers to consolidate everything from quality inspection to supply in one single location. As a result, the overall vehicle development cycle was shortened by 30%, and development costs for certain new cars were reduced by up to 50%. It was a bet to match the speed and cost reduction of Chinese cars through an R&D center dedicated to China. Next, the second bet Volkswagen made on China is a platform dedicated to the Chinese market. It’s called the CMP platform; it was originally named "China Main Platform," hence CMP, but they likely thought the name was too blunt, so they quietly changed it to "Compact Main Platform." Anyway, this is the platform developed by the VCTC mentioned earlier . It is a platform that can accommodate everything from pure electric vehicles to plug-in hybrids and EREVs; the key is that it was made to equip the powertrains Chinese consumers prefer most, rather than the pure internal combustion engines Volkswagen used to offer in the Chinese market. The goal is for 15 out of the 18 models that SAIC Volkswagen plans to release by 2030 to be based on this CMP platform. An EREV called the ID.UNYX was already unveiled this past January. However, more important than this China-exclusive platform, the third bet Volkswagen made in China is on their electrical/electronic architecture. You see, the most important thing in cars these days is how well they can be updated. Infotainment updates are now standard, and cars that didn't drive well suddenly performing better after an update have become one of the most important factors determining a car's marketability. In our market, such cars are still rare, except for Tesla, Mercedes, BMW, and a few other models, but in the Chinese market, these things are now basic. However, traditional automakers, including Volkswagen, used a distributed architecture structure where dozens to over a hundred small ECUs were scattered throughout the car. For instance, the wiper controller was separate, the window controller was separate, the powertrain controller was separate, and so on, all supplied by different parts manufacturers. So, even if Volkswagen wanted to perform over-the-air updates, they had to coordinate by getting software one by one from the manufacturers of over 100 ECUs. They could potentially do it, but since it costs an enormous amount of effort, it’s honestly nearly impossible. Therefore, for these updates to be smooth, a zonal architecture—where four or five high-performance computers control all of the car's dozens or hundreds of functions—needs to be applied, but Volkswagen failed when they tried to build it themselves through their subsidiary, Cariad. So, in China, VCTC, Cariad China, and Xpeng, known for being the best at software in China, cooperated to develop a China-exclusive zonal architecture. This is what they call the China Electronic Architecture, or CEA. Volkswagen spent over 23 trillion won and several years in Germany trying to develop this through Cariad, but they say it was completed in just 18 months after collaborating with Xpeng. So, they’ve established a China-exclusive research center, a China-exclusive hardware platform, and a China-exclusive electrical and electronic architecture—now there’s only one thing left. How did Volkswagen solve the autonomous driving requirement? This is where Volkswagen’s fourth bet comes in; first, they created something called Carizon. It’s a joint venture between Cariad and China’s Horizon Robotics, and it feels like Cariad just won't die . Horizon Robotics is a Chinese autonomous driving semiconductor company. They make the Journey series chips, and the Zeekr cars entering the Korean market also come equipped with these chips. Anyway, Cariad holds a 60% stake and Horizon Robotics holds a 40% stake in this venture. Volkswagen decided to invest 2.4 billion dollars into this, which is over 3.7 trillion won. So, results from this Carizon venture began to emerge late last year. The first is the Level 2+ system. China tends to play with terminology, so Level 2+ refers to hands-free driving only on highways, but this Level 2+ has been developed and is already being used in the ID.UNYX 06 and 07. They say that a Level 2 ++ system, which drives to your destination in the city once you input it, is scheduled to be updated within this month. So, they say this Level 2 ++ system will be applied to all vehicles using the CEA architecture mentioned earlier. Their future goal is to mass-produce Level 3 vehicles in China where the driver doesn't need to supervise at all and the manufacturer takes responsibility, but Chinese authorities have recently implemented a policy stating you can't call it Level 3 unless you're prepared to take full responsibility for accidents, so it's unclear if it will actually be released as Level 3 or if they'll use typical Chinese marketing terms like Level 2 +++ or Level 2.99. Now, there’s also talk that Volkswagen is collaborating with Xpeng on autonomous driving in addition to Carizon, but this hasn't been confirmed . Back in February, a report by the Chinese media outlet China EV Post mentioned an internal memo from Xpeng's CEO stating that Volkswagen became the first commercial customer for Xpeng's next-gen autonomous driving system, which led many media outlets to report that Volkswagen and Xpeng are collaborating on autonomous driving; however, since this hasn't been officially announced, it's fair to say that Volkswagen's main bet on Chinese autonomous driving is currently Carizon , and we’ll have to wait and see if their partnership with Xpeng expands from electrical and electronic architecture to autonomous driving as well. So, are all these bets Volkswagen is making in China actually yielding results, and are they turning into real cars? They are. They’re being released very quickly, just as planned. At the Beijing Motor Show last April, the Volkswagen Group even held four world premieres in a single night. They introduced the ID.UNYX 09, developed in just 24 months through a joint venture with XPeng. They also revealed the ID.AURA T6, built by FAW-Volkswagen, which features the CA architecture and a CarPlay-level 2+ system. SAIC Volkswagen also unveiled the ID.ERA 9X. And the second mass-production model for Audi’s China-exclusive brand, the E7X, was also released. Including these four cars, they say over 20 new models will be launched in China this year alone. So, while the results seem to be coming out quickly as planned, whether these products are actually delivering the intended outcome—reviving Volkswagen as planned—is a different matter. To start with the conclusion, they haven't been able to revive Volkswagen yet. Looking at the sales of the ID.UNYX series, which is built by Volkswagen Anhui for the Chinese market , they sold about 2,000 units in May, June, and July, and around 1,300 units last month. Hmm. Well, compared to local Chinese companies like Zeekr, NIO , and Li Auto, which sell over 30,000 units a month, the comparison is essentially meaningless. For now, only the cars themselves are appearing, but the results in terms of rising sales have yet to materialize. Of course, there is still a chance for a turnaround. As mentioned earlier, the urban Level 2 ++ driver-assistance system starts updating this month, so we’ll have to wait and see if these features—which Chinese consumers consider essential—can lead to a rebound in sales. Now, moving on, Volkswagen has made a second bet in the United States. You might wonder why they would bet on the U.S. after investing so much in China, but in most global markets outside of China, it's difficult to use the same electric/ electronic architecture or software developed for China. Since global markets require passing much stricter safety certifications and meeting various regulations, it’s fair to view the investment in the U.S. as a separate bet to gain competitiveness on a global scale. Why go to another country like the U.S. to invest? I don’t think there are many people left who think it would be better to just invest in local subsidiaries, the way Hyundai does with their $ 4 billion investment. Volkswagen had already created Cariad only to suffer over 11 trillion won in operating losses over three years, leading to the disaster of delaying the Porsche Macan Electric and Audi Q6 e-tron launches by two years each. We covered this story in detail in our last video, so we would appreciate it if you could watch that video if you are curious. Anyway, after getting a taste of failure with Cariad, Volkswagen realized that developing software alone was impossible, so they took their money to the US to place a bet on Rivian. Rivian is a company in the US that specializes exclusively in electric SUVs and electric pickup trucks. They are the company making vehicles like the R1S, R1T, and R2, and they are actually quite attractive when you see them in the US. The cars themselves have both an outdoor feel and a high-tech vibe, making you want to spend your own money to buy one. However, until now, cars like the R1S and R1T have been priced between $ 70,000 and $ 100,000 in the US, which is nearly 100 million won, so sales volume hasn't been that high. As of last year, they were at the 60,000-unit annual level. Since Volkswagen, the company we are talking about today, sells around 9 million units annually, they really cannot be compared in terms of volume. It's a 150-fold difference. Yet, Volkswagen took their money to this company to propose forming a joint venture. How much did they bring with them? Up to 5.8 billion dollars. It seems they brought almost 8 trillion won in our currency. It's a massive amount of money, but it's not like they're just dumping it into Rivian's bank account all at once; it's structured to be paid out in stages as milestones are met. First, 1 billion dollars is provided as convertible notes, 1.3 billion dollars is for intellectual property licensing and joint venture equity, and the remaining 3.5 billion dollars is money that will be deposited as goals are met step by step. Volkswagen definitely put safety measures in place after getting hit so hard by the Cariad situation. So, the name of the joint venture between Volkswagen and Rivian is RV Tech. It stands for Rivian and Volkswagen Group Technologies. It was established in November of the year before last, and the equity is split 50/50 between Rivian and Volkswagen. So, what exactly is Volkswagen buying from Rivian by setting up this joint venture? It is the very "zonal architecture" I mentioned earlier when explaining the CA. In China, they created one through a joint venture with Xpeng, but the zonal architecture for the global market is being developed with Rivian. This is because Rivian is a brand that already mass-produced zonal architecture early on. Rivian applied this zonal architecture to actual mass-produced vehicles when they released the second-generation R1T and R1S the year before last. The number of ECUs in a single car was reduced from 17 to 7. They say this reduced the wiring by 1.6 miles, or 2.6 km, per car . The fact that 2.6 km of wiring disappeared is incredible; since the 63 Building is about 250m tall, that’s like removing the length of five round trips up and down that building from a single car. In the process, the car became 20kg lighter and material costs were reportedly cut by 20%. And the really important thing here is that it became easier to repair. There’s a case highlighted by the US media outlet InsideEVs where a first-gen Rivian R1T owner had a minor collision and broke one of the side cameras. To replace that single camera, the repair shop had to pull out the entire wiring harness, disassemble the dashboard, and even remove the seats. As a result, the car ended up stuck at the repair shop for eight months. All because one camera broke. This is the limitation of the existing architecture. So, the zonal architecture isn't just for over-the-air updates; it's a structure that simultaneously improves costs, weight, and serviceability. For Volkswagen, they are a company where cost reduction is currently the most urgent priority. So, with this one move , they are killing two birds with one stone: cost reduction and the implementation of a next-gen software-defined vehicle architecture. Now, how far has this Rivian technology come? Fortunately, unlike Cariad, progress is moving along quite well. It is said that the Volkswagen and Rivian teams created a working demo car just 12 weeks after they first met. They took a Volkswagen vehicle and swapped in Rivian's zonal architecture, and seeing them do in three months what Cariad had been struggling with for three years convinced Volkswagen that Rivian was the real deal, which is why they signed the deal. Anyway, they announced that they successfully completed winter testing this past March. First, engineers from Volkswagen , Audi, Scout, and Rivian gathered in Phoenix, Arizona, to finalize the software functions and prepare the vehicles. Then, they sent these cars to Arjeplog, Sweden. This is where automakers go for extreme cold-weather testing. They verified whether the hardware and software could run well together under extreme conditions and if over-the-air updates worked properly . Having passed these tests, Volkswagen invested an additional $ 1 billion into Rivian. So, the next step is to apply this zonal architecture to Volkswagen’s mass-produced vehicles, which is happening next year. They plan to first apply this joint zonal architecture with Rivian to the ID.1, an ultra-low-cost electric vehicle with a target price of 20,000 euros, or just around 30 million won. It’s a state-of-the-art architecture that cost trillions of won, but they’re putting it into their cheapest car first, not their most expensive one. Because cheaper cars are more sensitive to production costs, reducing wiring and ECUs is actually much more critical for a 30-million-won vehicle. And this joint architecture is reportedly recognized as Volkswagen's next-generation integrated platform, the SSP. This means that in the future, Volkswagen Group brands like Volkswagen , Audi, and Porsche will all be released using this platform and the latest architecture. It really feels like Volkswagen has finally found a way to survive. So, looking only at this, it seems Volkswagen's bet on the U.S. is proceeding successfully, right? However, there is still one crucial piece missing here. It's autonomous driving. What Volkswagen is building now in collaboration with Rivian is just a next-generation electrical/ electronic architecture and a vehicle operating system; it does not include autonomous driving software. Then, who will be in charge of the autonomous driving that will be deployed in their global fleet? Originally, it was Bosch. Volkswagen and its subsidiary Cariad formed an autonomous driving alliance called ADA with Bosch in 2022 to create a system covering levels 2 to 3, with an investment of about 2.3 trillion won , but as we reported in a previous video, this ADA alliance was completely dissolved two months ago. Even after spending 2.3 trillion won, internal evaluations concluded that its performance was far behind that of competitors. Of course, it wasn't that there were no results at all, as ADA did develop a Level 2+ system that allows for highway hands-free driving, and while that software could be applied to cars like the upcoming Audi Q9 or next year's ID.1, the problem is there's nothing beyond that. So what do they do now? How will they support highway hands-free driving that meets the latest international DCAS standards , or urban hands-on autonomous driving? For this, Volkswagen has decided to purchase it from a new partner. Who is that partner? Although no official announcement has been made yet, according to the German publication Automobilwoche, Volkswagen plans to finalize and announce the contract by the end of this month. As of now, two companies are being mentioned as strong candidates. The first is Mobileye, which is already collaborating with Volkswagen on various projects. The ID. Buzz robotaxi is also based on Mobileye . Also, in March the year before last, they signed a contract to introduce Mobileye SuperVision and Chauffeur as software for consumer vehicles, a method that allows for immediate implementation of a Level 2+ system by equipping the Mobileye IQ6 chip and software developed by Mobileye. It is the easiest approach and currently the most likely, but we will have to wait and see if it is ultimately adopted. The second candidate being discussed is the British autonomous driving startup, Wayve. It is a company that Nvidia has invested in, and it creates software based on end-to-end AI. However, I think the possibility of Volkswagen choosing them is lower than Mobileye because they still lack a proven track record. We will have to wait a little longer to see which company ultimately becomes their autonomous driving partner. It could be Mobileye or Wayve, as mentioned before, or they could suddenly team up with companies like Nvidia or Qualcomm, so we'll wait a few weeks and let you know as soon as news breaks. When we see Volkswagen news these days, we usually only notice things like 50,000 layoffs and plant closures, but as you saw today, Volkswagen isn't just downsizing. They are investing 8 trillion won in China and another 8 trillion won in the U.S. company Rivian. They also announced that they will invest 210 trillion won in facility investment and R&D over the next five years. The reason they are cutting staff and closing factories is to raise this capital. The surprising part is that all the money raised this way is currently going to Chinese and American companies. The world's second-largest automaker has admitted that it cannot do it on its own and has decided to put out the fire by purchasing the technology from those who do it best. However, I think this is, conversely, proof that Volkswagen is still very much alive. It also seems to serve as a lesson that the belief held by current hardware-based car companies that they can develop software in-house might be a mistake. What do you think?

Jolly Pong that was always in our school bags as kids, and Butter Waffles we’d snack on after exams. The man who created them lived his entire life unable to swallow food properly due to a stomach illness. Unable to eat properly, he became obsessed with taste instead. If there wasn't a machine for it, he would build one himself to produce new snacks. That is how Jolly Pong, Crown Sando, and Butter Waffles were brought into the world. Yet, this man’s company once went bankrupt, unable to cover even a small payment. Now, let’s follow the journey of how this company, which nearly vanished from bankruptcy, ended up swallowing Haitai, a rival twice its size. In a corner of the factory. A man was struggling with a machine. If a store-bought confectionery machine couldn't produce the taste he envisioned, instead of blaming the machine and giving up, he would grind metal and fix parts to remake it himself. He would adjust the dough mixture countless times and check the baking temperature with his fingertips until he achieved the desired texture. Among the employees, people said the boss was crazy about snacks. That man was Yoon Tae-hyun. What’s strange is that he himself was a man who couldn't properly enjoy food. Yoon Tae-hyun lived his whole life with a stomach illness. Because his stomach was weak, he couldn't swallow just anything, so his palate naturally became picky. Usually, this would be a weakness. But for him, it worked in the opposite way. He could never just let a taste that bothered him slide. Even snacks others found acceptable were lacking to his tongue, and he even worked on the machines himself to bridge that minute gap. It turns out that a man who couldn't eat properly became all the more obsessed with taste. Let’s go back to see where this obsession began. Yoon Tae-hyun was originally a tailor who made suits. But the stomach illness that followed him for life held him back here too. As his health declined, sitting in one place for long periods to sew became difficult, and he eventually had to close his tailor shop . He opened a dumpling shop and survived by doing suit alterations, all while searching for a new line of work. Then, in 1947, he opened a small confectionery shop in Jungnim-dong, near Seoul Station. The name was Yeongildang. This was the beginning of the company we know today. It was a small shop where he used one electric oven to make and sell bread using donut flour he bought from U.S. military bases. And Yeongildang, established in 1947, changed its signboard in 1956. The new name, Crown, meant a royal crown. He chose the crown, a symbol of the highest authority, to reflect his determination to make premium snacks he could safely feed to his own children. He gave a small snack shop the name " Crown." His first masterpiece to live up to that name arrived in 1961. It was Crown Sando. Back then in Korea, the only sandwich biscuits available were American-made ones that leaked out of U.S. military bases. They were expensive and rare. Yoon Tae-hyun created a domestic sandwich biscuit to replace those American imports. It was the familiar shape we know today, with cream filled between two biscuits. The response was beyond his expectations. Wholesalers lined up in front of the factory to get their hands on the product. Everything produced was sold immediately. The fussy palate of a man who could barely swallow food had created a snack that people stood in line to buy. And this obsession led his son to create something entirely new. The person who created that new product was the eldest son, Yoon Young-dal, who returned after studying in the U.S. It began when he joined the family company in the 1960s. He made two major changes . One was switching from a distribution system controlled by wholesalers to a direct sales system managed by the company. The other was a new snack released in 1972. It was Jolly Pong, a light bag of puffed rice and barley with a sweet coating. However, Jolly Pong didn't sell well at first. Puffed grain snacks were unfamiliar, and people didn't know how they were supposed to be eaten. For a while, it just piled up in the warehouse. The tide turned in an unexpected place. At the time, U.S. military families lived in the Hangang Mansion apartments, and they started pouring milk over Jolly Pong like cereal, which helped spread the word. They said it tasted just like the cereal they had back home. As that word of mouth spread through the neighborhood, the snack finally started to sell. The taste of pouring a bag into your mouth from your school bag. That is how Jolly Pong established itself as the original K-cereal that Koreans eat with milk. Once the path was cleared, a string of hits followed. In 1986, Cuku Das was released, featuring soft cream wrapped in a thin, delicate biscuit. Many will remember how we carefully took small bites for fear it would crumble. Then, in 1991, Choco Heim appeared. A box of Choco Heim cost 1,700 won. Since other companies 'boxed snacks cost 1,000 to 1,200 won at the time, it was intentionally priced as a premium snack . It was even rumored that only children from wealthy families ate it. The snacks we grew up eating without even knowing the names all came from this one company, and that's how Crown Confectionery entered its heyday. Unexpectedly, the peak of that golden age wasn't a snack, but a drama. In 1999, MBC aired a drama titled "Kuk-hee ." The story followed a protagonist played by Kim Hye-soo who jumps into the confectionery business during the Japanese colonial period, and the character was modeled after the founder , Yoon Tae-hyun. The writer was inspired by his early days in business, and Crown Confectionery stepped in to sponsor it. The reaction was huge. It was a national drama watched by one in every two households. The peanut sandwich created by the main character in the drama became a real product named "Gukhee Peanut Sandwich," bringing a second heyday to the company's long-standing sandwich biscuits. However, a lonely scene overlaps in the midst of this buzz. In September 1999, while the drama "Gukhee " was airing, Yoon Tae-hyun passed away . Just when the story based on his life was becoming the talk of the nation, the man who served as the inspiration closed his eyes without seeing the full outcome. At the peak of its glorious heyday, the company fell into its deepest quagmire. In 1997, Crown Confectionery celebrated its 50th anniversary. It was the year a company that started in a small bakery near Seoul Station had endured for half a century. Jolly Pong, Cuckoo Das, and Choco Heim were dominating the top spots in the boxed snack market. It was a year where it wouldn't have been strange to hold a grand celebration. But that very winter, the entire nation was shaken. It was the IMF financial crisis. In a time when perfectly fine companies were collapsing one after another, Crown could not avoid that wave. The problem wasn't just the external pressure. The company had already been expanding outside its core business well before that. A company that made snacks and bread had branched out into Crown Engineering for machinery, and even into dairy products through its subsidiary, Woori Dairy. Diversification, branching out into various businesses beyond the core. When things were going well, these looked like wings helping the company grow, but when the economy froze, these branches started holding the company back one by one. Debts had piled up, and the flow of money had dried up. And in January 1998, Crown Confectionery ultimately failed to cover a promissory note. It was a small note that could hardly be called large compared to the company's size. A company that had built itself up for 50 years went bankrupt in the face of that one small piece of paper. The company then filed for a court-mandated composition. It was a procedure to save the company by restructuring its debt under court supervision. They chose rehabilitation over liquidation. It was the moment the company that made Jolly Pong and Crown Sando knocked on the court's door to survive. What followed was a time of agony. They sold factories and merged production lines. People who had worked together had to leave the company as well. No feast was held for the 50th anniversary; instead, it became a year where they had to shed the most. Now, this is where the real story begins. Inside the collapsing company, there was one product that became the key to recovery. It was the Butter Waffle, the youngest product launched in 1997, just before the bankruptcy. Released under the title "The Aristocrat of Snacks," this product began to prop up the company during the darkest period right after the bankruptcy. Even in those days when people kept their wallets shut, butter waffles continued to sell steadily, providing the struggling company with small but vital cash flow. The conclusion the company reached was simple. The answer, in the end, was snacks. Since the branches they had spread outside their core business had brought the company down, the path to survival was to return to snacks. They focused on what they knew how to make and concentrated their efforts on top-selling products. By paying off their debts one step at a time, Crown Confectionery graduated from court receivership in 2003, sooner than expected. They were freed from court supervision and stood on their own two feet again. It had taken five years from bankruptcy to revival. For an ordinary company, the story would have ended there as one that barely survived . But this revived company soon did something hard to believe. It swallowed a company much larger than itself whole . That big company was none other than Haitai Confectionery. To understand this reversal, we must first know what kind of company the opponent was. Haitai began with one boy. In 1939, 15- year-old Park Byung-gyu joined a confectionery company run by the Japanese as an accountant. He was the only Korean employee in the entire company. Then, liberation arrived. Park Byung-gyu and his partners took over the factory left behind by the departing Japanese. That is how Haitai Confectionery was established in 1945. Starting from an empty factory with nothing but their bare hands, it was a beginning similar to that of Yoon Tae-hyun, who opened Young-il Dang during the same period. The first product Haitai introduced to the world was Yeonyanggaeng. It was Korea's first mass-produced snack. In other words, it was the first snack churned out in large quantities from a factory. Even when the war broke out, they took their cauldrons and boilers to Busan as refugees and never stopped production, even amidst the falling shells. Afterward, Haitai hit back-to-back successes with Bravo Cone and Matdongsan, rising to the number one confectionery company in the country in the 1970s. At one time, they were the biggest snack company in the nation. How did such a Haitai end up in someone else's hands? After the founder, Park Byung-gyu, passed away in 1977, the company expanded beyond its core business. They dabbled in electronics, trade, and heavy industry. That reckless expansion became their undoing . In November 1997, the Haitai Group finally met its bankruptcy. And in 2001 , the heart of Haitai, the confectionery division, was sold to a UBS consortium of foreign investment firms. Several investors pooled their funds to buy it together. It was the moment the number one snack company fell into the hands of foreign capital. But this is where the story takes a turn. Four years later, in 2005, it was none other than Crown that stepped up to buy that Haitai back. Looking at the size of the two companies, you can see just how extraordinary this picture was . If you look at the numbers, it looks like this. At the time, Crown was ranked fourth in the industry, while its target, Haitai, was second, with more than double the revenue. The fourth-ranked company set out to swallow the second-ranked one, a company more than twice its size. A company that once barely managed to make a domestic biscuit to replace imported American snacks was now declaring it would buy out a confectionary giant that was once the nation's leader. It was like David swallowing Goliath. Naturally, it wasn't a sum of money that Crown could handle on its own. So, Crown chose a leveraged buyout (LBO) strategy, taking on debt secured by the company's own assets and gathering investors. The acquisition price was settled at roughly 500 billion won. The foreign capital that had bought it for a pittance cashed out with a profit after four years, and it was a domestic company that bought it back at a higher price. Once the acquisition was complete, the landscape changed instantly. Combining the revenue of Crown and Haitai instantly propelled them to the second-place spot in the industry. While still not reaching Lotte’s trillion-won-plus revenue, the company that had been fourth became one of the "Big Two" overnight. A company that had collapsed because it couldn't cover a small bill had, in seven years, taken control of a former industry leader larger than itself. It was a thrilling turnaround. However, this massive acquisition contained both light and shadow that would soon be revealed. Was the acquisition truly a success? It took another nine years to get the answer. In 2014, a snack quietly emerged from Haitai. It was Honey Butter Chip. To be precise, it was a potato chip made by Haitai Calbee , a joint venture established with equal investment between Haitai Confectionery and Japan's Calbee. The unfamiliar combination of sweet-tasting potato chips cleared out convenience store shelves nationwide within two months of launch. A peculiar scene unfolded where people went from store to store to find a bag, and even paid premiums on the secondhand market. It didn't end as a passing fad, either. Honey Butter Chip established itself as a flagship product, consistently selling tens of billions of won worth every year. The light did not stop there. In February 2024, the song "Bam Yang Gang" by singer BIBI climbed to number one on the music charts. Then, the "Yanggang" mentioned in the lyrics actually started to fly off the shelves . During the few weeks that BIBI's song was a hot topic, sales of Yanggang at convenience stores and hypermarkets increased noticeably. It was as if that sweet red bean jelly, which debuted as the nation's first mass-produced snack in 1945, was being sold again nearly 80 years later because of a single song. The numbers eventually gathered into a single line. In 2023, the consolidated revenue of the group's holding company, Crown Haitai Holdings, exceeded 1 trillion won for the first time. The gamble taken by a bankrupt company to swallow a firm larger than itself finally paid off. What was once called a reckless bet was re-evaluated as a successful gamble. However, there was another business within the same company that faced the exact opposite fate. It was Crown Bakery. At one time, it was a pioneer of franchise bakeries with hundreds of stores nationwide. Yet , that very bakery disappeared without a trace while its snack business was heading toward the 1-trillion-won mark. The start of the downfall came from the outside. When the parent company reeled from the IMF crisis, the momentum to invest in the bakery dried up first. Meanwhile, competitors like Paris Baguette were rising fast. They were ahead in capital and logistics, and they also used the weapon of baking fresh bread daily from dough delivered to the stores. Customers gradually moved toward the freshly baked bread. However, the deepest wounds came from within. The head office pushed its franchisees too hard. They raised supply prices, refused to accept returns of unsold products, and denied contract renewals upon expiration. Franchisees faced high switching costs to change to other brands, but the compensation offered by the head office fell far short. The store owners resisted strongly. And the number of stores began to shrink. Finally, in 2013, Crown Bakery completely folded its franchise business. During the same period, the growth curve of its competitor, Paris Baguette, was the exact opposite. While Crown's bakeries collapsed from hundreds to zero, Paris Baguette's stores grew to thousands. In the same market, one side vanished while the other exploded in size. That is how two different fates diverged within the same company. The snack business they swallowed hit 1 trillion won, while the bakery they raised themselves disappeared. The company has now transitioned to a leadership system under CEO Yoon Seok-bin, the grandson of the founder and a third-generation owner. Although the succession is complete, the lack of a hit product since Honey Butter Chip and sluggish overseas growth remain as tasks ahead. There was once a man who could not even swallow food properly. Living with a picky palate his whole life due to stomach problems, he became obsessed with making food instead of eating it. That obsession gave birth to Jolly Pong and Butter Waffle. The company he founded collapsed over a single unpaid promissory note, but it rose from the bottom, swallowed a larger company called Haitai, and eventually surpassed 1 trillion won in revenue. However, the bakery raised by the same company disappeared without a trace. It goes to show that success and failure were always two sides of the same coin. The story that began with a man who couldn't swallow food but was obsessed with snacks has come this far. For some , it was a bag of Jolly Pong in their school bag, and for others, a cake for their birthday—that was the company. It turns out that such rise and fall were layered behind that familiar name.

On May 15, 1997, the company that produced the most bread in South Korea shut its doors. Samlip Foods. It is the very company that made the Samlip bread , cream bread, and Boreumdal moon cakes you pick up at convenience stores even at this very moment. Annual revenue was 190 billion won. With over 2,000 employees and nearly four out of every ten people in the market eating this company's bread, it was undoubtedly the number one company in the country. Do you know what brought such a company down? It was a single unpaid promissory note of 300 million won. A company earning 190 billion won went bankrupt because it couldn't come up with a mere 300 million. Doesn't that seem strange to you? Does it make any sense for a company earning 190 billion won to not have 300 million? There is something even stranger here. The amount of debt guarantees this company signed, promising to pay off others 'debts, was a staggering 142 billion won. That is 20 times its own capital. It means a company that couldn't cover 300 million was casually signing off on 142 billion won worth of guarantees. So, Samlip Foods did not go bankrupt because it lacked 300 million won. The 300 million was just the trigger pulled at the end, and the real bullet was elsewhere. If you listen to this story to the end today, you will find out everything that happened behind the bag of bread you pick up thoughtlessly. How a father divided the company between his two sons, how the successful older brother ended up collapsing the company, and how the younger brother who inherited a small peripheral company reclaimed this fallen kingdom to expand into 15 countries worldwide—it is a story that feels more like a drama because it is true. The story goes back 80 years to the year of liberation. In 1945, a 25-year-old man opens a small bakery in a place called Ongjin, Hwanghae Province. His name is Hur Chang-sung. He would later completely change the history of bread in this country, but at the time, he was just a diligent young man who gave up schooling due to financial hardship to learn baking skills. The name of the shop was Sangmidang. It meant "a house that praises taste." But what was the situation of this country right after liberation? It was a time when the Korean War broke out, the entire country was in ruins, and everyone was going hungry day by day. Hur Chang-sung moved south and reopened Sangmidang near Bangsan Market in Jung-gu, Seoul, in 1948. But what made this young man different was this. He wasn't just someone who made good bread; he was a businessman with a calculator always running in his head. At the time, bread was an expensive food that ordinary people could not easily afford to buy. Why was that? It was because the cost of the coal briquettes used in the ovens to bake the bread was high. Most people would have just accepted that expensive coal prices meant expensive bread, but Heo Chang-seong thought about things differently. If I cut fuel costs, I can lower bread prices, and if bread is cheaper, wouldn't people buy more of it? So, he developed his own anthracite kiln that used powdered coal instead of regular briquettes. This powdered coal was a whopping 90%cheaper than standard briquettes. Since the cost dropped so drastically, Heo Chang-seong lowered the price of his bread accordingly. So, what happened next? Sangmidang's products started selling like hotcakes. "That shop's bread is tasty and cheap." As this rumor spread, the business began to thrive. Now, here is where the first genetic trait of this family emerges. It is the instinct for mass production: cheaper and more abundant than anyone else. This instinct would later propel the company to the very top. But Heo Chang-seong didn't stop there. He kept a close eye on their neighbor, Japan. In Japan, the market for mass-produced bread, where factories churned out bread on a large scale, was already growing rapidly. What is mass production? It refers to the bread we buy today at supermarkets or convenience stores—the kind made in factories, packaged in bags, and sold. Heo Chang-seong had a lightbulb moment. Korea will soon be like this, too. The era of baking one by one by hand is ending, and the era of factory-made production is coming. So, in 1959, he founded a company called Samlip Confectionery. This was the true beginning of the Samlip Foods we know today. He even brought in baking technicians from Japan to begin serious research. He had one single goal. To create a "national bread" that was affordable, delicious, and could be mass-produced in a factory. And so, in 1964, a product that would mark a milestone in the history of Korean bread was released. It was the Samlip Cream Bread. Why was this such a big deal? While making this cream bread, Samlip installed the first automated bread production line in the country. Since machines were churning out what used to be handmade one by one, they were able to slash the prices. Back then, a cream bread cost only 10 won. It might not sound like much now, but in the 1960s, when even rice was scarce and people were going hungry, a 10-won cream bread was truly a revolution. Try to imagine it. When you're hungry and rice is scarce, imagine how much joy it must have brought people back then to be able to buy a bread filled with sweet cream for just 10 won. It is said that people lined up early in the morning in front of the Samlip Daebang-dong factory just to buy the cream bread. Stories even go that people would flock near the factory just to catch the scent of the freshly baked cream bread. The cream bun continued to be loved by the entire nation throughout the 1960s, helping Samlip grow rapidly. Now, but was this man, Heo Chang-sung, satisfied with just a cream bun? He was not. He turned his eyes to Japan once again. In 1969, in front of a shop in Japan, he saw steamed buns being sold, with steam rising from them. At that moment, the calculator in his head started spinning again. What if I made those hot steamed buns so people could eat them at home without going to the shop? It’s easier said than done. But this was a technically extremely difficult task. Why? Mass-produced factory bread usually hardens as time passes. However , this bread needed to retain its fluffy texture, as if freshly made, when reheated in a steamer at home. This was anything but easy. After countless failed attempts by the research team, the product finally hit the market in October 1971. Named for the way people blow on it to cool it down, this is Samlip Ho-ppang. Yes, it’s the very same Ho-ppang that comes to mind when you think of winter. Interestingly, this Ho-ppang was quite expensive at the time. Standard mass-produced bread was about 5 won, but Ho-ppang cost a whopping 20 won. It was four times as expensive. Yet, what do you think happened? It became an absolute hit as soon as it was released . By that December, it was selling an average of 1 million units per day. 1 million a day. Why did it sell so well? Samlip played another brilliant move here. When selling Ho-ppang, they provided dedicated steamers to local shops. So, as you walked down the street in winter, every corner store had white Ho-ppang sitting neatly in a steaming pot, and people shivering in the cold just couldn't walk past them. They completely shattered the stereotype of mass-produced bread being cold and bagged by introducing a way to serve it piping hot. This dedicated steamer was the decisive factor that turned Ho-ppang into a national snack. With this single item, Samlip Foods ascended to the undisputed number one position in the South Korean baking industry. They made a name for themselves with cream buns and took the throne with Ho-ppang. It was the moment the dream of young Heo Chang-sung, who started in a small bakery called Sangmidang, blossomed into a massive company embraced by the whole nation after 30 years. Up to this point, it’s a perfect success story. There’s no room for criticism. But, you know. They say there is no permanent number one in this world. From the very moment they reached the top, a quiet yet dangerous crack began to form beneath the feet of this seemingly perfect company. The seed of that crack began to grow as soon as the founder handed over the company he built with his own hands to his two sons. Samlip Foods, a top-tier company for the older brother, and a small, obscure company for the younger one. What on earth was the father thinking when he divided the company like that? And how did that decision lead to a single 300-million-won promissory note twenty years later? In 1988, founder Hur Chang-sung decided to step back from management at well over sixty years of age. A man who had spent his whole life focused on bread decided it was time to pass the company to his children. He had two sons. An eldest and a second son. However, the way this father split the company was truly peculiar. He gave the eldest son a company everyone would covet. That is, he handed over Samlip Foods, the country's number one bakery company. The Samlip Foods that was loved by the whole nation for its cream buns and steamed buns, with high sales and a prestigious reputation. On the other hand, he gave the second son, Hur Young-in, a company called Châterais. What kind of company was this Châterais? It was a subsidiary established in 1972 by Samlip to make premium bread, but in terms of scale, it couldn't even compare to Samlip. It was a tiny company with only a tenth of the sales of the Samlip Foods his brother inherited. Now, if you were these two brothers, which one would you have wanted? Naturally, the big and successful Samlip Foods. At the time, anyone would have said the older brother was the clear winner. It was as if the older brother inherited a kingdom, while the younger brother received a small fortress on the outskirts. But it was at this very point that the two brothers began to walk completely different paths. And that difference in choices eventually flipped their destinies in opposite directions. Shall we talk about the older brother first? The older brother who inherited Samlip Foods had great ambitions. But there was one problem. As we entered the 1990s, the economy grew, people's pockets got deeper, and their attitudes toward bread began to change rapidly. In the past, bread was something you ate because you were hungry. But now, an era had arrived where people chose to eat delicious, quality bread even when they were full. Bread had changed from a staple meal to a luxury food item. This was a painful change for Samlip. Why? Because Samlip's packaged bread, mass-produced in factories and stocked in supermarkets, was bound to taste inferior to fresh bread from local bakeries. Around this time, high-end bakeries selling freshly baked bread began springing up everywhere. As people flocked there, the sales of the once-prosperous Samlip Foods began to slowly decline. Now, if you were the older brother, what would you do in this situation? There were two main ways to handle it. One was the direct approach: focus on making our bread even tastier, since we are a bread company after all. Another option was the expansion strategy: recognizing the limits of bread and turning to other businesses. The brother chose the latter. He decided to find a way out elsewhere because his main business was wavering. But the problem was the direction of that expansion. Shall we take a look at the businesses he touched? He jumped into the beverage market with a brand called Neora. He opened a fast-food chain called Good France. In the Gangneung and Sokcho regions, he entered cable television. Yes, he even started a cable broadcasting business. And crucially, he reached out into a massive resort project near Seoraksan named Haila. Doesn't something seem strange to you? A bread-making company suddenly doing beverages, fast food, broadcasting, and resorts. We often call this " octopus-style expansion." Like an octopus spreading its legs, he was dabbling in all sorts of businesses unrelated to his core work. Resorts, in particular, are businesses that require an immense amount of money. Buying land , putting up buildings, and installing facilities requires astronomical sums. But where did that money come from? A significant portion was borrowed money. In other words, it was debt. Let's briefly touch on how this company raised money so that what happens later makes sense. It’s not complicated. Samlip Foods had several subsidiaries under its wing. When these subsidiaries borrowed money from banks, the parent company, Samlip Foods, acted as a guarantor, saying, "If they can't pay, I'll pay for them." This is called a payment guarantee. Simply put, it’s exactly like going with a friend to get a loan and signing a document saying, " If this friend doesn't pay, I will." It's just the same as stamping that seal. Do you know how much the guarantee stamps were that Samlip Foods placed on its subsidiaries' debts? It was a staggering 142 billion won. And this, mind you, when the company’s own capital was only 7 billion won. They had signed off on debts twenty times their own capital. In normal times, this isn't much of a problem. That's because the subsidiaries just need to pay back their debts on time. But what if—just what if—these subsidiaries all faltered at once? All that debt would come crashing down on Samlip Foods at once. That nightmare became a reality in 1997. You all remember it, don't you? A name no South Korean can ever forget: the IMF financial crisis. The event that drove the entire South Korean economy to the brink as the national coffers ran out of dollars. It was that terrifying time when banks tightened their purse strings and healthy conglomerates collapsed one after another overnight. This storm hit Samlip Foods head-on. Just think about it. They had borrowed heavily from everywhere to build resorts and expand their broadcasting business. But then, banks suddenly demanded immediate repayment, causing a liquidity crisis. The various businesses they’d spread themselves thin on weren't generating profit yet, and debts began pouring in from all sides, drying up the company's cash flow. And then, the day finally arrived . On May 15, 1997, Samlip Foods was declared officially bankrupt. But here comes the most shocking part of the story. What was it that brought this massive company—with 190 billion won in sales, over 2,000 employees, and a 36.4%market share (meaning nearly four out of every ten Koreans ate Samlip bread)—to a complete halt? It was a 300 million won promissory note they couldn't cover. Let me briefly explain what a promissory note is. It’s not difficult. It’s basically a written promise saying, "I don't have the money right now, but I'll pay you back on this date in a few months." If you fail to pay by that promised date, that’s bankruptcy. It’s like getting a red tag on your credit. Samlip Foods failed to cover one 300 million won note on its due date, and at that moment, the nation's top bread company was branded with bankruptcy. So, let's return to the question we started with. Did a company with 190 billion won in sales really fail because they didn't have 300 million won? That doesn't make sense, logically speaking. How could a company making 190 billion won not find 300 million? The answer is this. The 300 million won was just the trigger. The real bullet was the 142 billion won in loan guarantees I mentioned earlier. What I mean is this. When the businesses and affiliates they had expanded into were swept away by the IMF typhoon and began collapsing, all those massive guarantees Samlip Foods had signed for came crashing down on them at once. Just the immediate, problematic portion amounted to 32.1 billion won. The company was already rotten to the core from the inside. It's just like a patient with failing internal organs who eventually collapses from a simple cold. The cold was the 300 million won, and the already ruined organs were the 142 billion won. The bread itself was not at fault. Cream bread and steamed buns were still selling very well. What brought Samlip Foods down wasn't the bread, but reckless greed outside their main business and the stamps they carelessly placed on others 'debts. The bread company that the whole nation loved, that kingdom the founder spent his whole life building, was futilely rushed into the intensive care unit known as court receivership. There is a part here that is truly heartbreaking. The founder, Heo Chang-sung, had to watch all of this happen, didn't he? He had to witness the collapse of everything he had built. Watching the company he spent his whole life building—personally crafting anthracite kilns, developing cream bread, and introducing steamed buns to the world—fall apart. And not by anyone else’s hands, but by his own eldest son’s. Could there be anything more heartbreaking than this? But here’s the thing. If the story had ended there, it would have simply remained a sad tragedy. But the real twist starts right now. At the very moment Samlip Foods, inherited by the older brother, was lying in the intensive care unit fighting for its life. What on earth was the younger brother doing, who had inherited a company one-tenth the size from his father? The name of that younger brother, who received Shany—a small company only one-tenth the size of his brother’s—is Hur Young-in. Now, if you were in this younger brother’s shoes, how would you feel? Your brother got the number one company everyone in the nation knows, while you got a tiny, obscure company, so it would be natural to feel upset. But instead of feeling bitter, Hur Young-in makes a very different choice. I mentioned earlier that because bread wasn't selling, the older brother turned his eyes toward beverages, resorts, and broadcasting. But the younger brother, Hur Young-in, went in the exact opposite direction. He actually dug deeper into bread. Why do people go to local bakeries instead of buying our bagged bread? Isn’t it because freshly baked bread tastes better? Then couldn't we just sell freshly baked bread ourselves? While others were abandoning bread, he delved deeper into the essence of it. In doing so, Hur Young-in creates a very clever system. It is the "bake-off" method. The name might sound difficult, but the principle is simple. The dough is mass-produced at a factory and sent to each store. The actual baking is done on the spot at the store. What are the advantages of doing this? It keeps the low costs of factory mass production while allowing customers to be served warm, freshly baked bread right in the store. It takes only the advantages of mass production and local bakeries and combines them into one. On top of this, another brilliant idea is added. Local bakeries at the time had one major issue. Only those with actual baking skills could open a shop. But by using this bake-off method, the dough is already prepared by the headquarters, so you only need to learn how to bake it. This meant that even people without baking skills could open a bakery. What does this mean? It means they could expand their franchise stores all across the country. So what is the brand that came into the world this way ? It is the name you are likely seeing in your neighborhood every single day: Paris Baguette. He gave it a name that brings to mind France, the home of bread. Paris Baguette, which opened its first store in Gwanghwamun, Seoul in 1988, began to expand rapidly by emphasizing the strength of selling freshly baked bread with consistent quality everywhere. While the older brother was struggling with businesses outside of bread, the younger brother was quietly taking over the whole country with just bread. And then, the fateful year of 1997 arrived with the IMF crisis. Now, this is where the choices made by the two brothers lead to completely opposite results. As we saw earlier, the older brother's Samlip Foods, which had expanded recklessly with heavy debt, could not withstand the storm and collapsed. But what about the younger brother, Hur Young-in? Because he had not taken on unreasonable debt from the start and had focused diligently on his core business of bread, he actually endured this scary period relatively solidly. It wasn't just a matter of enduring. In the midst of these difficulties, the younger brother even hit a jackpot. In 1999, the Pokémon bread released by Shany took the entire country by storm. By putting Pokémon character stickers inside the bread, it became a total syndrome, with kids buying the bread again and again to collect the stickers , even throwing away the bread just to keep the stickers. For reference, this Pokémon bread became a massive trend once again after a long time had passed . Anyway, the younger brother's company was piling up cash even amidst the crisis. Now, the most dramatic moment of this story arrives. On one side, there was the older brother's Samlip Foods, struggling for survival under court receivership due to bankruptcy. On the other side, there was the younger brother's Shany, which had overcome the crisis and was flush with cash. Then, on June 10, 2002, Paris Croissant, the younger brother's company, bought Samlip Foods, which was under court receivership, for 90.1 billion won. Isn't it fascinating to think about this scene? The younger brother, who had inherited a small peripheral company, bought back with his own hands Samlip Foods, which was the very root of the family business that the father had passed down to the older brother. The younger brother reclaimed the father's kingdom that the older brother had brought down. It was the moment when the brother who started with one-tenth the size embraced the company that was ten times larger. It was a truly dramatic reversal that made one realize how life can turn around like this. And on November 25 of that year, Samlip Foods graduated from court receivership. It had walked out of the intensive care unit. Hur Young-in did not stop there. He combined everything: Samlip Foods, which he reclaimed from his brother, the Shany and Paris Baguette he had built, and the Baskin Robbins and Dunkin' brands he had brought into Korea. What was born in 2004 that way is the SPC Group we know today. The name SPC itself comes from the roots of the two brother companies, Samlip and Shany. Collapsed Samlip was reborn under the new name SPC Samlip and still serves as a solid pillar of the group today. Hoppang and cream bread are still by our side, too. But that is not where the story ends. The truly surprising part comes after that. This company, which hit rock bottom after bankruptcy, has now begun to look beyond the country. Paris Baguette was at the center of it all. Hur Young-in had a long-held dream. It was the dream of taking bread to the global stage. When you think about it, this is truly remarkable. Whose food is bread, anyway ? It's Western food, after all. We used to import bread, not export it. Yet, our company opened a store right in the heart of Paris, France—the very home of bread. This was back in 2014. Paris Baguette was the first bakery franchise to enter France. They set out to target the palates of the French not with kimchi or ramen, but with bread. Doesn't that make you feel thrilled when you think about it? Paris Baguette's challenge didn't stop there. They opened stores in the U.S., the U.K., Singapore, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Mongolia. Most recently, in 2025, they opened their first store in Ulaanbaatar, the capital of Mongolia. As of the latest data, Paris Baguette operates over 670 stores in 15 countries worldwide. Especially in Asian countries where bread wasn't a staple, our bakeries are now seeing locals lining up to buy our bread. Now, think back to that company at the beginning. The company that collapsed in 1997 because it couldn't pay a measly 300 million won. The kingdom the founder spent his life building fell in vain at the hands of his eldest son. In just over 25 years, it rose again as a company selling bread in 15 countries across the globe. A story that began at a small bakery called Sangmidang, with 10-won cream breads and neighborhood steamers, is now unfolding on the streets of Paris, New York, and Singapore. If there is a lesson we can learn from this long story, what would it be? The older brother inherited a successful kingdom but lost it all by neglecting his core business and aggressively expanding. Conversely, the younger brother inherited an insignificant company but stayed focused on the essence of bread, eventually reclaiming his brother's kingdom and expanding globally. It wasn't flashy expansion, but staying true to what one does best that had the last laugh. Behind the number 300 million, there was, in fact, this deep story of a family's crossed fates. If you pick up a Samlip bun at a convenience store this evening, you might see that bread a little differently now. Because it is a bread that holds a long journey, having collapsed once and overcome the crossed fates of brothers to reach the world. Experiencing both the highs and the lows, yet standing back up in the end. Perhaps that is the warmest takeaway this story leaves us with. Thank you sincerely for joining me through this long story.

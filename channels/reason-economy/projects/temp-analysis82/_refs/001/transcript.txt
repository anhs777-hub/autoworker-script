Whenever you mention traveling to Europe, people always have one thing to say. "Be careful not to get pickpocketed." It might sound like unnecessary nagging, but the numbers will make you change your mind. In fact , Barcelona, Spain, saw 34,000 reports of theft in just five months. That’s 225 cases a day, meaning someone’s wallet vanishes more than nine times every hour. Paris is even worse. There are over 600 robberies per year for every 100,000 people. So, the advice to wear your bag in front wasn't nagging —it was a survival rule. On the other hand, how is it in Korea? In the heart of Seoul, along the Cheonggyecheon stream, bookshelves holding over 2,000 books are just left out in the open. There are no guards, and no records of who borrowed which book. Yet, these books stay right in their place until 10 PM. It’s no wonder foreign tourists can’t help but be amazed by this sight. What exactly creates this difference? The answer was hiding in a surprisingly simple place. It’s in the report card this library receives every year. In the first half of 2025, out of every 100 visitors to the Cheonggyecheon outdoor library, 11 were foreigners. If ten people were sitting there, one of them was a guest from across the sea. As a result, over 2,000 books in foreign languages were added in the second half of that year. They even created a section specifically for translated versions of Korean novels and the original novels behind Korean films. It’s like reading the original source material of a movie you saw in theaters, in your own language, right by the water. When you think about it, it’s a truly unique scene. A traveler from the other side of the globe is sitting in the middle of Seoul, reading a Korean novel in their own language. Shall we take a closer look at where that traveler is sitting? The bookshelves themselves are shaped like waves. More than 2,000 books are tucked into shelves modeled after the flow of the Cheonggyecheon stream. On the stone steps just below, people sit with their pants rolled up and feet dipped in the water, turning the pages. Water trickles beneath their feet, and a book lies open on their laps. Reading while listening to the sound of water in the middle of the city—just hearing about it makes you feel relaxed. The city of Seoul calls this area between Mojeongyo and Gwangtonggyo "The Reading Stream." The name itself is so beautiful, isn't it? When you think of a library, what comes to mind first? Probably things like entrance doors, library cards, or text messages reminding you of return dates. This place has none of those. There are no borrowing procedures, whether for Korean or foreign language books. You just take one, read it, and put it back in its place when you’re done. That’s it. It is open only on Fridays, Saturdays, and Sundays. When the weather is mild, it lights up from 4 PM until 10 PM, welcoming guests even after the sun goes down. But the interesting thing is when the visitors flock here. According to a report in the Seoul newsletter, more people visit this place at night than during the day . Office workers on their way home, couples holding hands, and travelers with large backpacks sit side by side along the water. They each open different books and listen to the same sound of flowing water. This library wasn't by the water from the very beginning. It started in the spring of 2022 on the lawn of Seoul Plaza. All they did was place bookshelves and cushion chairs on the grass. The following year, in 2023, the stage expanded to Gwanghwamun Square, and in 2024, it finally came down to the streamside. In just three years, it has traveled from the lawn through the square to the water, covering the heart of Seoul. Then, who exactly is watching over all these books? When you hear that there are no guards, you might think there is no one at all along this wide stream. But there are librarians. The truly interesting part is what those librarians do. As the sun begins to set, Oh Ji-eun, the director of the Seoul Metropolitan Library, reportedly says this into her radio. Let's make sure the books don't close. Once those words are spoken, the librarians take something out of their apron pockets. Is it a whistle? Or is it enforcement equipment? It is neither. It is a small portable light. The librarians quietly place one light next to each person reading a book. It is so that no one has to close their book and leave because it gets dark. It means not to guard the books, but to ensure they don't stay closed. They weren't people placed there to supervise, but people placed there to brighten the way. Hearing just this much, you might think of it as just a well-decorated urban resting spot. But even this picturesque scene was an experiment that everyone tried to talk them out of at first. It was the first test paper in the report card this library receives every year. When they said they would bring library books outside, worries poured in. Everyone shook their heads, asking what would happen if they disappeared or who would take responsibility if they were damaged. These weren't baseless worries , either. There had already been a similar experiment across the sea in the United States. It is a small free library where people attach a small box in front of their house, put books in it, and let anyone take them to read. In our terms, it's like hanging a small bookshelf next to your front gate and letting any passing neighbor take a book to read. It is a common sight in American neighborhoods. But these boxes are getting wiped out entirely. According to local broadcasts, one resident in Austin, Texas, had their box emptied out six times in just two months. Not just once, but six times. Unable to take it anymore, they reported it to the police, and the answer they got back was a masterpiece. Since the books are free, we cannot make an arrest for theft. It wasn't the person who took the books, but the resident who reported it who ended up feeling awkward. It's absurd, isn't it? If a box of free books gets raided like this, imagine putting out thousands of books in the middle of Seoul. To anyone , it was a reckless gamble. So, what were the results? After the first week of operation, we counted every single book. Out of 5,000 books, only three had disappeared. That’s like losing only one book for every 600 books put out. The Austin box was raided six times in two months, while the Seoul bookshelf lost three books in a week. The people who tried to stop it were left speechless. But that’s not all. There was something even more amazing. According to an interview with Director Oh Ji-eun, a citizen took a book home thinking they were allowed to borrow it . Once they realized that wasn't the case, they sent the book back by courier. But when we opened the box, there was one extra book inside. Feeling sorry, they had included a new book as well. And no one had even urged them to return it. At this point, the library didn't suffer a loss, but actually gained interest. Doesn't that move you a little? Of course, it was just the first week, before word got out. It was a time when only those who knew about it came by to read quietly. The real test is after people start flocking there. A quiet neighborhood bookshelf and a bookshelf in a crowded plaza are completely different tests. Can this bookshelf hold up even if hundreds of thousands or millions of people flock to it? When people gather, things disappear. This has become like a law of the world. Only by understanding why things disappear in crowds can we see how truly extraordinary the Cheonggyecheon bookshelf is. Think of a village pavilion. Let’s say an elderly person playing Janggi on a summer afternoon gets up and accidentally leaves their fan behind. What would happen if you went to the pavilion the next morning? Nine times out of ten, that fan would still be sitting right there. Because the faces that frequent the pavilion are familiar. Word would spread quickly about who took it, and you'd face them at the board the next day. Now, let’s say you left the same fan on a chair in a train station waiting room packed with holiday travelers. In the ten minutes you were in the restroom, the fan would have vanished into thin air. Even if you asked the people around you , they would all just shake their heads . There were plenty of people around, but no one knows who took it. Why is that? Because the more people there are , the more two feelings grow. One is the feeling that no one will know who took it. The other is the feeling that even if I don't take it, someone else will anyway. Simply put, the crowd creates a place for hands to hide. As the number of faces increases, my own face becomes blurred, and behind that blurred face, the hand becomes bolder. There’s a Korean proverb that says seeing things breeds desire. It means you grow greedy when you see something, and the crowd acts as a mask for that greed. That’s why when people gather, lost items increase accordingly. This is common sense to all of us. Our elders’ advice to keep a tight grip on our wallets in crowded markets was based on reason. There is one more principle added to this. Fishermen cast their lines where the fish are gathered . No fisherman would spend all day casting in a reservoir without a single fish. The hands that target others ' pockets work exactly the same way. Where people gather. Especially where tourists flock, holding maps and looking lost. This is the very reason why you’re constantly warned to watch out for pickpockets before traveling to Europe. The warnings to wear your bag in front and not to leave your phone on restaurant tables always follow. When you look at the numbers, you immediately understand why these warnings exist. According to Eurostat data from 2022, Spain had the highest robbery rate in Europe at 131.8 cases per 100,000 people. Belgium followed with 103.3 cases and France with 99.8. It means that famous tourist powerhouses in Europe hold the top spots. A population of 100,000 is about the size of a small county in Korea. It means over 130 robberies happen in a single neighborhood like that in a year . Someone gets robbed every three days or less. That’s over ten robbery cases occurring somewhere in that same county every month. Barcelona is especially notable even within Spain. The probability of being robbed in Barcelona is 2.9 times the Spanish average. That figure of a wallet disappearing nine times an hour wasn't just pulled out of thin air. There’s something even more staggering. According to local English media reports, you don't get prison time for stealing items worth less than 400 euros in Spain. It means you won't go to jail if you steal something cheaper than a decent mobile phone. Even if caught, they are released quickly and the same hands return to the same alleys. The fact that the Ciutat Vella district has 611 cases per 100,000 people isn't strange when viewed this way. It’s a structure where tourists flock, thieves follow, and those hands keep coming back. So, the advice to wear your bag in front was a bit of life wisdom to physically block this structure. Now, how about Korea? There were 532 robbery cases in Korea in the same year. That’s 532 cases for the entire country in a year, not just one small county. When divided by a population of over 50 million, that’s just over one case per 100,000 people. That’s about one and a half cases per day for the entire nation. What a small town in Spain experiences over 130 times a year, our small county barely experiences once throughout the entire year. Moreover, this figure is the result of a 73%decrease over the past nine years. It’s jaw-dropping. It’s almost embarrassing to even place the three-digit number next to the one-digit number for comparison. However, the principle of pavilions and waiting rooms transcends borders. Whether in Seoul or Paris, when people crowd together, faces become obscured and hands naturally look for somewhere to hide. And the Cheonggyecheon bookshelves stand exactly where that principle should be at its strongest. There are no security gates, no borrowing records, and it’s a waterfront passed by millions every year. It’s like leaving books open not in a quiet pavilion, but in the middle of a crowded holiday waiting room. Furthermore, those books are placed at a height where anyone can reach them just by stretching out an arm. According to this principle, the Cheonggyecheon bookshelves should be getting cleaned out. Now that you understand the principle, it’s time to reveal the report card. When you see the numbers on how this principle played out at Cheonggyecheon, you probably won't believe your eyes. But what kind of scores could a library like this possibly be graded on? If you think about it, it's simple. There are no entrance doors, no checkout counters , and no borrowing logs. So, there is only one thing the operators can do. After a year of operation, they simply count the books that didn’t come back . To use an analogy, it’s like a store without a cash register closing for the day and counting the remaining inventory. The number derived from this is called the "unreturned rate." This number is the report card for this library. In 2022, the first year, the figure on the report card was 1.3%. Even this is not a bad score. It means that for every 100 books put out, nearly 99 returned. The following year, in 2023, it dropped to 0.71%. And in 2024, it finally hit 0.51%. 0.51%is about one book out of every 196. In other words, out of every 200 books put out, 199 returned to their place. If you look only at this, it might just seem like a report card that improved slightly each year. But when you place another number next to this one, the story changes completely. It’s the number of people who visited the library during the same period. In 2022 , it was 211,950 people. In 2023, it rose to 1,746,863. It increased. And in 2024, 3,133,117 people visited. In just two years, the number of visitors grew by 14.8 times. It’s as if the customers, who equaled the population of a small provincial town in the first year, grew to the size of Busan's population in two years. If you combine everyone who visited up until 2025, it comes to nearly 8 million people. It’s a scale comparable to nearly every person living in Seoul having visited at least once. Usually, if the number of visitors increases tenfold, it’s only logical that lost items also increase tenfold. According to the principle of pavilions and waiting rooms, that’s exactly what should happen. But at this library, even though visitors increased nearly fifteenfold, the rate of unreturned books dropped by more than half. A quiet pavilion turned into a bustling waiting room, yet it actually became safer than in its pavilion days. Common sense wasn't just turned upside down; it was standing on its head. Honestly, when I first saw these numbers, I thought something had been written down wrong. How on earth was this possible? There is one experiment that provides a clue. It’s an experiment published in the journal Science in 2008 by researchers at the University of Groningen in the Netherlands. The researchers placed envelopes with five-euro bills visible in them into mailboxes. Then, they watched to see if passersby would take the envelopes. When the mailbox was clean, 13%of the people passing by took the envelope. Conversely, when the mailbox was covered in graffiti, that rate doubled to 27%. It was the same envelope and the same money, but the surrounding environment caused hands to move twice as often. If the environment tells you that everyone here follows the rules, you’re less likely to reach out. Conversely, if it tells you that everyone here is careless, you’re more likely to do so. What if we apply this to the Cheonggyecheon bookshelves? If you’re standing in front of a shelf full of gaps, nobody would notice if one book went missing. But if you’re standing in front of a shelf where books are neatly in place without any gaps, it’s a different story. Nobody wants to be the person who creates the first empty space in that row. The more people who return their books, the more the bookshelf itself becomes a signal. A signal that everyone here returns their books. And that signal holds the next person’s hand back. It’s the same logic as why it’s harder to cut in line where everyone is standing properly. If everyone else is following the rules, breaking them alone makes you stand out . As more people came, both the people who saw that signal and those who continued it increased together. The number 0.51%is the result of millions of instances of people returning their books. 0.51%. So, it wasn't enforcement that created this number, but the hands of each and every person standing before the shelf. And on this report card, there is also a stamp of approval from across the sea. Seoul’s outdoor library received the 2023 Green Library Award from the International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions. It was a first among the 21,000 libraries in Korea. When we think of library awards, we usually think of places with grand buildings or rare books. But this outdoor library, with neither a roof nor walls, was the one that received the award. In 2024, the Seoul Outdoor Library ranked second out of 107 countries at the International Marketing Awards. There is even a stamp that citizens can mark themselves. In September 2025, 3,532 citizens at Gwanghwamun Square took turns reading Yun Dong-ju's poetry collection, "Sky, Wind, Star, and Poem, " one sentence at a time. The reading went on for 10 hours and 12 minutes. This record surpassed the 3,071 people who read Gandhi's autobiography in India, earning a place in the Guinness World Records. Doesn't that give you goosebumps? But the bookshelves aren't the only thing that surprises foreigners in Korea. Just visit a cafe and you'll see. These days, there's a recurring scene in videos foreigners post from Korea. It’s a scene where someone leaves their laptop or bag on a table while they go to the counter to order. Scenes of packages sitting undisturbed in front of doorways or on sidewalks are also common. According to reports, a Colombian woman posted a video calling it a scene you can only see in Korea, and it was flooded with comments from South American netizens. One comment said that in Colombia, it would be stolen in three seconds. A Venezuelan netizen reportedly wrote this: In their country, someone would have stolen not just the laptop, but the chair they were sitting on, too. When you look at the numbers for packages, it becomes even more real. A US security firm estimated that 104 million packages vanish from doorsteps annually in the United States. That’s over 280,000 packages evaporating every single day. Yet, in our apartment hallways, packages can pile up for four days without anyone touching them. Except for the owner, of course. If a package could feel, it would be most at ease being delivered in Korea. If you go to the subway, it’s even more astonishing. In 2025, there were 36,387 cases of lost wallets alone in the Seoul subway system. It has been the number one lost item for five consecutive years. That’s nearly 100 wallets leaving their owners' hands in the subway every single day. What we should really pay attention to is the cash. From 2021 to September of this year, nearly six years, a total of 2.98 billion won in cash was turned in at subway stations. And of the cash turned in during 2025, two-thirds returned to their owners. You shouldn't just look at this number as lost money. It is the total sum of money that someone found on the subway floor and turned in completely intact. These are the hands of people who, instead of putting found money in their pockets, carried it to the station office; that's how this amount gathered over six years. We already know. This isn't something innate; it's a habit ingrained in us. Do you remember the crowded buses? The sight of someone sitting down silently taking the heavy bag of a standing passenger and placing it on their lap. That heart that cared for a stranger's belongings like their own is continuing in front of the Cheonggyecheon bookshelf under a different name. What is the small act of kindness from a stranger in those days that you remember the longest? Please let me know in the comments. The world has started grading our habits. There is a ranking that the US travel magazine Condé Nast Traveler selects annually through reader votes. In this vote, which drew 798,867 ballots across all categories including cities and hotels, Seoul rose to become the world's best big city in 2026 for the first time. It’s a leap of four spots from 5th place last year. Tokyo, which was 1st for two consecutive years in 2025, was pushed down to 2nd, and 8th place was 7th. Seeing that 8th place, the one who wore their bag in front, being 7th down there makes you laugh, doesn't it? In the world's friendliest cities list selected by the same magazine's UK edition, Seoul ranked 3rd after Melbourne and Porto. It’s like we’ve received a report card from the world on our city. But the real story starts now. Don't you think a country that doesn't steal books would also read a lot of them? But that wasn't the case. In the 2025 National Reading Survey, 38.5%of adults said they had read at least one book in the past year . It was 38.5%. That’s the lowest figure since the survey began in 1994, and it’s the first time it has fallen below 40%. This means that more than six out of ten adults didn't read a single book all year long. It’s ironic that we don't steal books, yet we don't read them either. However, there was a generation that moved in the opposite direction. That would be those in their 20s. The reading rate for people in their 20s actually rose to 75.3%. The number of young people getting closer to books, whether on paper or screens, has increased. A term called "text hip" has even emerged, meaning that reading books is considered trendy. There was also an event that ignited this. In October 2024, the news broke that author Han Kang had won the Nobel Prize in Literature. In the three days following that, 1.032 million copies of Han Kang’s physical books were sold. That’s over 170,000 copies sold per day. Considering it took eight months for Haruki Murakami’s 1Q84 to surpass 1 million copies, it was truly an explosion. But, here is the thing. There is another plot twist here. Do you know which generation saw the sharpest rise in bookstore sales in the week immediately following the award? It wasn't those in their 20s. It was those in their 60s and older. With a sales growth rate of 51.9%, they ranked 1st among all age groups. The ones who picked up books again were the generation watching this video right now. So, where on earth did this habit of not touching others 'belongings and returning what we borrowed come from? And why is it that this scene blossomed specifically by the Cheonggyecheon stream? To find the answer, we must trace back the time the place called Cheonggyecheon has passed through. This stream, where clear water flows now, was actually buried underground for 47 years. After the war ended, people with nowhere to go built shacks along this stream and lived there. In those two-or three-pyeong shacks, they ate, slept, washed, and worked. It was so polluted with sewage that Seoul city records listed it as a prime example of a poor and filthy neighborhood. In 1958, construction began to cover the waterway with a concrete lid. Between 1967 and 1971, an elevated highway was built right on top of it. The name Cheonggyecheon remained, but a generation of children grew up without knowing water flowed underneath. Beside that waterway, there was another scene. Around 1959, used bookstores began gathering one by one around the market. In the 1970s, when books were scarce, their number exceeded 200. For students who found the price of even a single reference book burdensome, this alley was a reliable savior. It was an alley where old books, studied with underlines drawn by someone else, were passed on to the next student. Many of you watching this video now have likely flipped through those yellowed pages in that very alley. Of those many used bookstores, only about 20 remain now. Then, on July 1, 2003, construction began to remove the elevated highway, where over 120,000 cars traveled daily. Concerns poured in about a potential traffic catastrophe. During the shack era, displaced residents had to leave for Bongcheon-dong, Sillim-dong, or the Gwangju housing complex, and street vendors had to relocate their livelihoods. After 2 years and 3 months and an investment of 386.7 billion won, Cheonggyecheon finally emerged under the sky again on October 1, 2005, after 47 years. Over the waterway where 24 old bridges once stood, 22 new bridges were built, and Gwangtonggyo Bridge was restored to its former appearance. Amid worries about what could survive in an artificial stream, the number of fish species grew from just 4 to 21, and total biological species increased to 666. With 330 million visitors over 20 years, it’s as if every citizen has walked along it more than six times. When you think about it, it’s a strange connection. In the same alleyway where people once passed old books from hand to hand because they couldn't afford a new one, there are now rows of free bookshelves. Back then , a single book was read by passing through many hands; now, thousands of books pass through millions of hands and return to their place. The spirit of cherishing books and treating others ' belongings like our own has been passed down through generations. Now, shall we return to our initial story? In crowded European tourist spots, I move my bag to the front and grab my phone off the table first. Conversely, in the heart of Seoul, people open a book and listen to the sound of water even at 10 PM. It wasn't surveillance cameras that made that difference. It was people who returned what they borrowed, even when no one was watching . When 200 books are put out, 199 come back. That is the true meaning of that 0.51%report card. This ordinariness, so familiar to us that we never considered it a point of pride, is the real reason the world envies Seoul. Looking back, it has been a very long journey. There were times when we were so poor we had to cover the streams, and times when we went to work while drinking muddy water . Students who studied from a single worn-out book grew up to build this nation, and now their children and grandchildren sit by the same stream and open books for free. This scenery is possible because, even while fighting poverty, we never let go of the principle of not touching what belongs to others and returning what we have borrowed. Therefore, 0.51%is not just a report card for the younger generation. It is a report card shared by your generation, who silently lived through those days. Shall we look at it in terms of our wallets, too? These books were purchased with the taxes we paid. If you divide the 2024 outdoor library budget of about 7.2 billion won by the 3.13 million visitors that year, it comes out to just over 2,300 won per person. It's like a library is set up for half the price of a cup of coffee. And the secret to how that library runs without library cards was, ultimately, our hands. From now on, when you see videos of foreigners being amazed in Korea, think of the hands that returned the books rather than the surveillance cameras.

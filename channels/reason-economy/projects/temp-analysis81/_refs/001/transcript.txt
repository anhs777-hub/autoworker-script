The yellow Trio dish soap found on every kitchen sink in our homes. And Jeju Air passenger planes soaring into the sky with their orange wings flashing on the airport runway. These two things that seem worlds apart actually branched out from the hands of a mother and her sons in the same family. It was a high-value soap company worth 700 billion won, built over 50 years of blood and sweat by an ordinary 34-year-old housewife who lost her husband and was left raising four children. But the sons who inherited this company sold their mother’s firm to others for a mere 447.5 billion won, claiming they needed to plug the debt holes of their airline. We will now trace the shocking path of the money handled by the second generation of Aekyung, who sold the "mother" company and were left only with debt-ridden airplanes. How on earth was that massive empire I mentioned earlier built? Think back to when you rub your eyes and head to the bathroom every morning. The soap you hold over the sink, the toothpaste you squeeze onto your toothbrush, and the yellow detergent sitting on the counter. All these everyday items were pouring cash into one company's pockets every single day. Actually, the starting point of this massive empire was a tearful tragedy. Aekyung Oil&Fat Industry started making soap in 1954 at a small factory in Yeongdeungpo, Seoul, with a capital of 50 million hwan. It was a time of rapid growth when the nation’s first beauty soap, Mihyang, sold 1 million units a month, and the Trio dish detergent launched in 1966 swept 90%of the market share. But in the summer of 1970, the founder, Chae Mong-in, who led the company, suddenly passed away from a heart attack. This happened just four days after the birth of his youngest child. The person left behind was an ordinary 34-year-old housewife with four children, Honorary Chairwoman Chang Young-shin. Back then, it was an era where it was hard to even imagine a woman managing a corporation. Relatives and people around her strongly discouraged her, urging her to sell the company to someone else. However, the 34-year-old young mother took her young children’s hands and stepped up to the position of CEO in 1972. It was the birth of South Korea’s first female CEO. >> The path Chairwoman Chang Young-shin walked over the following half-century was nothing short of a miracle. She shattered the prejudices of those around her, staking everything on honest quality alone. In 1987, she released Spark, a concentrated laundry detergent that eliminated the hassle of boiling clothes, taking the number one spot in the market. In 1998, she introduced the 2080 toothpaste with the slogan "20 healthy teeth until age 80," turning it into the nation’s favorite toothpaste within eight months of its launch. With the subsequent success of the premium shampoo Kerasys, bathrooms and kitchens in Korean households truly became Aekyung’s stage. But there was an even more surprising move in store. They added the new wings of cosmetics onto the solid foundation of household goods built with soap and toothpaste. It was the myth of the "AGE 20's Essence Cover Pact," commonly known as the "Gyeon Mi-ri Pact," which first appeared on home shopping in 2013. This unconventional cosmetic, which forms water droplets when applied, sold out in every single broadcast. With cumulative sales far exceeding 240 million units, you can imagine how intense the craze was, right? This company was truly a goose that laid golden eggs that never ran dry. Whether the economy is good or bad, people wash their hair, brush their teeth, and do the dishes every day. Every time consumers opened their bathroom doors each morning, cash would clink into the company's safe. The goose the mother created laid heavy golden eggs every morning without any complaints. This prime cash flow finally led to the spectacular achievement of listing on the stock market in 2018. At the time, Aekyung Industrial’s performance was nothing short of dazzling. Annual sales neared the 700 billion won mark, and the annual operating profit alone reached 78.6 billion won. It means that 50 to 80 billion won in pure cash was deposited into the company's account every single year. A solid and massive cash fortress had been completed, unshakable by any crisis that might strike. This perfect soap fortress, which the mother built with all her might over half a century. It was a genuine treasure that could comfortably feed the entire group just by sitting back and making soap and toothpaste. However, did soap and detergent look too boring to the second generation who opened the doors to this massive fortress? A wind of entirely different desires began to blow over the mother’s humble soap factory. The position the mother built by making soap and detergent with dirt-stained hands her whole life. But the eyes of the eldest son, Vice Chairman Chae Hyung-seok, who stepped up to lead the management, were looking elsewhere. Selling a few thousand won worth of soap and detergent on supermarket shelves every day seemed too boring and ordinary to the second-generation management. Ambition boiled up to build a sophisticated and massive business on a glamorous stage that others would admire. The beginning was a department store. In 1993, they boldly opened the first Aekyung Department Store on the site of the old factory in Guro, Seoul. People around them shook their heads, asking why anyone would put a department store in an industrial zone, but the result was a huge success. Following that, they hit the jackpot one after another with the opening of the Suwon Station private-public joint venture department store. Gaining confidence, the second generation even acquired Samsung Plaza in Bundang in 2007, introducing the retail brand "AK Plaza" to the world. They began to gain the conviction that they could escape their mother's shadow and succeed on their own. However, the second generation's sprint didn't stop at department stores. They had their eyes on an entirely different, much larger playing field. It was the aviation industry, cutting through the blue skies. In January 2005, the Aekyung Group partnered with Jeju Special Self-Governing Province to establish Jeju Air, the nation's first low-cost carrier, with an initial capital of 20 billion won. Aekyung held a 75%stake, while Jeju Province held the remaining 25%. Back then, the aviation industry was a treacherous sea that even major conglomerates wouldn't dare to enter. In fact, countless companies had launched airlines only to go bankrupt before they could even properly spread their wings. With the two giants, Korean Air and Asiana Airlines, holding a tight grip on the skies, it was clearly a reckless challenge to anyone. It was only natural that concerns and worries poured in from all sides. But then, a miraculous turnaround happened. They boldly cut out unnecessary in-flight services and gambled on ultra-low-cost tickets with the fluff removed. College students and young travelers with tight budgets began to go wild. Jeju Air, which had been suffering from deficits, finally succeeded in turning a profit in 2011, six years after its founding, recording an operating profit of 21.1 billion won . Since then, it immediately took the top spot in passenger numbers among domestic low-cost carriers, rising as the absolute powerhouse of the skies. And in November 2015, Jeju Air made a spectacular debut on the KOSPI market, the first domestic low-cost carrier to do so. On the first day of listing, the company's market capitalization instantly surpassed 1 trillion won. The media spotlight poured onto them as the protagonists who had dismantled the stronghold of giant airlines that had monopolized the skies for decades. They had moved beyond their mother's soap company to build a new empire that dominated the skies of South Korea. This was truly the greatest achievement and a moment of glorious honor where the second-generation management proved their capabilities both inside and outside the family. But the plane that soared so magnificently was actually a giant eagle that terrifyingly devoured fuel. When it rides the wind and soars high in the sky, it looks faster and more elegant than anything else in the world. However, to keep those wings in the air for even a single second, you must continuously pour in astronomical amounts of food every moment. The moment it stops flapping its wings for even a second or the food supply is cut off, that heavy hunk of metal has no choice but to plummet to the ground. It was at that very moment, while they were popping champagne and raising toasts. Drunk on their glamorous party in the sky, the second generation failed to notice. They had no idea that the giant eagle they were riding would soon become a black hole, sucking up every last drop of the money their mother had saved her entire life. And at the very end of 2019. A massive, unimaginable storm that would swallow up the world's airways was slowly approaching. Jeju Air, which had been sailing smoothly, hit a massive reef at the end of 2019. They stepped forward to acquire their competitor, Eastar Jet , for 54.5 billion won, aiming to cement their number one position. The plan was to grow their size in the skies and establish a complete monopoly . However, the very next month, an unforeseen, worldwide pandemic disaster broke out. As air travel came to a complete halt, Jeju Air hurriedly abandoned the acquisition and terminated the contract. But they were dragged into a legal battle to get their deposit back. They were tied up for a staggering four years until the Supreme Court issued a final ruling to return 14.25 billion won of the deposit . Yet, this was merely a small preview compared to the greater catastrophe to come. The three-year period from 2020 to 2022 was nothing short of hell. Even though the planes were grounded, fixed costs like lease payments, labor, and parking fees poured out by the tens of billions of won every month. Just having the planes sitting on the runway meant hundreds of millions of won vanishing into thin air each day. During these three years, Jeju Air's cumulative operating loss reached a massive 830.4 billion won. The sheer size of an 830 billion won deficit is hard to grasp just by hearing it. It’s simple. Aekyung Industrial, which the mother built over a lifetime, usually makes only 60 to 80 billion won in annual operating profit selling soap and detergent. It meant that an astronomical amount of money—the kind that would take every household in Korea ten years of savings to amass— disappeared in just three years. The capital they had accumulated hit rock bottom in an instant, pushing the company to the edge of total capital impairment where it wouldn't be surprising if they shut down immediately. From this point on, the entire group started pouring water into a giant bottomless pit. Even though there was a huge hole at the bottom of the jar and water was pouring out like a waterfall, they couldn't stop bailing . They poured in all the clean well water that the other family members had worked blood and sweat to draw, but the hole wouldn't be plugged. Eventually, it led to a catastrophe where even the arms and legs of those pouring the water were broken. Then, who on earth filled this massive deficit hole caused by the airplanes? It was none other than AK Holdings, the holding company of the Aekyung Group. To save Jeju Air, three large-scale capital increases totaling 467 billion won were carried out over those three years. Of this, the cash poured in by the holding company, AK Holdings, amounted to a massive 272.1 billion won. The holding company shouldered it all alone: 72.4 billion won in 2020, 89.9 billion won in 2021, and 109.8 billion won in 2022. So, what is the problem? The holding company's safe, naturally, did not have that kind of massive idle cash on hand. In the end, AK Holdings began plugging debt with more debt, pledging affiliate shares as collateral and aggressively issuing 130 billion won in perpetual bonds and exchangeable bonds. As a result, the holding company's debt-to-equity ratio soared to nearly 329%in the blink of an eye. With 80%of the holding company's debt consisting of immediate financial borrowings, the entire group was reduced to a patient reliant on private loans. To make matters worse, AK Plaza, the other wing the second generation had cherished so dearly, completely collapsed. As online shopping became the norm, the flow of customers to offline department stores came to a sudden halt. Since 2020, AK Plaza has been pouring out tens of billions of won in losses every year, with its capital impairment rate well exceeding 90%. The two wings of the second generation—aviation and retail —broke simultaneously, becoming a noose tightening around the group's neck. The maturity dates for hundreds of billions of won in loans were shrinking day by day like an hourglass. As the holding company was pushed to the brink of bankruptcy, unable to handle the interest, creditors began their fierce pressure. With nowhere left to retreat, the second generation finally hung their heads and began to fiddle with a final card they had never even imagined. It was the decision to sell off the mother’s heart that had fed the family for 70 years. In April 2025, a shocking announcement turned the business world and the entire stock market upside down. Aekyung Group put one of its crown jewel affiliates up for sale to pay off the holding company's debts. However, the name of the company they put up for sale made people doubt their own ears. It was Aekyung Industrial, the parent company that holds the group's 70-year history intact. The market could not hide its shock. Why, of all things, did they have to sell the parent company filled with their mother's blood and sweat instead of other failing affiliates? The answer was cruelly simple. No one would even look at the loss-making airlines or department stores if they were put on the market. The only company that could silence the creditors and immediately generate hundreds of billions of won was Aekyung Industrial, which had honestly sold soap and toothpaste to earn tens of billions every year. This was equivalent to knocking on a pawnshop door with the most precious heirloom passed down through the family. It is like selling off a jade ring that a mother cherished and passed down for a lifetime, just to pay off the debts of a child addicted to gambling. If they could just avoid the immediate pressure of debt, family pride or history meant nothing to them. Faced with the sharp blades of the creditors, the family's solid roots were thus reduced to a mere bundle of cash. In October 2025, a buyer finally appeared. Taekwang Industrial, which handles petrochemicals and textiles, joined forces with private equity funds to enter the acquisition battle. They agreed to sell the roughly 63%stake in Aekyung Industrial held by Aekyung Group for a total of 470 billion won. The structure involved Taekwang Industrial becoming the new owner with a stake of over 31%, while financial investors divided the remaining shares. Having signed the contract, the second-generation owners might have felt relieved, thinking they could finally breathe easy. They believed that once the final payment hit their accounts, they could put out the holding company's urgent fires and overcome the crisis. However, just before the final payment arrived, an unimaginable disaster struck in January 2026. A major incident occurred with the 2080 toothpaste, an Aekyung symbol that half the nation uses every morning and night. Triclosan, a harmful chemical that had been completely banned in Korea for a decade, was detected in six of the toothpaste products. The root cause was the products that had been outsourced to overseas manufacturers to save on costs . The company scrambled to announce a full voluntary recall and began issuing refunds, but public betrayal and anger spread uncontrollably. Taekwang Industrial, which was on the verge of acquisition, immediately overturned the negotiation table. They demanded a revaluation, arguing how they could pay the full original price when the national toothpaste brand’s credibility had hit rock bottom. If Aekyung had time on their side, they could have stood their ground or walked away from the deal. But the second generation had neither the nerve nor the time left to do so. If they failed to cover the holding company's imminent debt maturity, the entire group faced total collapse. In the end, the second generation bowed their heads and accepted the opponent's demands as they were. They agreed to cut a whopping 22.5 billion won from the originally promised 470 billion won. The final pride of a 70-year family legacy, built stitch by stitch by their mother over 50 years, was brutally slashed by 22.5 billion won due to a ridiculous disaster of poor toothpaste management. The final sale price fell to 447.5 billion won. And so, in the spring of 2026, the fateful day finally dawned. The soap and toothpaste that had been our family's steady livelihood for 70 years completely passed into someone else's hands with a single signature. Then, what exactly did the second generation end up holding in their hands in exchange for selling off their mother's founding company? On March 26, 2026, a cold, brief public disclosure appeared in the financial market. It was an announcement that the final payment of 447.5 billion won from the Taekwang Industrial consortium had been fully deposited. With this, Aekyung Industrial, which began in a small soap factory in Yeongdeungpo in 1954 and served the Korean public for decades, left the family's hands forever after 70 years. It was the moment the strongest fortress, which the mother had built with blood and tears over half a century, vanished into history. As a result, the Aekyung Group became an empty nest, having handed over its core to others and left with nothing but the shell. They surrendered the delicious fruit meant to feed their young and their warm home entirely to another bird. They were left precariously perched on a barren branch , without even a single feather to shield them from the cold rain and wind . Where they had uprooted the sturdiest pillar of the house, only a chilling, penniless silence remained. The price of selling off their founding company immediately began to constrict the holding company's throat. A staggering 73%of the reliable dividend income that the holding company, AK Holdings, received from its subsidiaries evaporated overnight. The stable cash pipeline that had brought in tens of billions of won every year was cut off in a single stroke. The proceeds from the sale melted away instantly to pay off the holding company's urgent debts, leaving the firm with completely depleted stamina to sustain its future. So, what is the current state of Jeju Air, which was saved by the mother's sacrifice? Surprisingly, the crisis is far from over. As of the first half of 2026, Jeju Air’s debt-to-equity ratio has reached a staggering 1,900%. It was effectively a walking patient, with debts ten times greater than the capital it possessed. Compounding this, the tragedy in late December 2024, when a passenger plane flying from Bangkok to Wuhan skidded off the runway during landing, claiming 179 tragic victims, severely shook public trust in its safety. Cold criticisms were inevitable , suggesting that investments in safety had been pushed to the background amidst reckless cutthroat competition and financial distress. And then, in September 2026, six months after selling the founding company. Another sorrowful piece of news was delivered. Jeju Air issued yet another 100 billion won in perpetual bonds, burdened with a murderous interest rate of over 7%. Of the money raised, 90 billion won went straight toward rolling over debt to pay off short-term loans that had just reached maturity. Even after selling off the mother's soap business, the hole in the airline’s finances remained unfilled, trapping them in a vicious cycle of paying higher interest just to survive day by day. At the final stop of this tragedy, Chairman Jang Young-shin, the mother who had dedicated her life to soap, quietly stepped back from the stage. In January 2026, while sale negotiations were in full swing, he transferred all 980,000 shares of the holding company he held to Aekyung Asset Management, his family-owned firm. The only shares left in his possession were a mere 500. His mother's philosophy, built on a lifetime of soap and detergent with the belief that companies should be founded on honest sweat rather than bubbles, was thus shattered by her sons 'hands. The era of that proud mother, who stood tall as Korea's first female CEO while holding her newborn baby half a century ago, came to such a futile end. Then, what exactly is left in the hands of the Aekyung Group, which sold off its roots? In the place where soap and toothpaste once stood, only chronic deficits remained. There were only the department store AK Plaza, pushed off to an IT affiliate, the chemical subsidiary Aekyung Chemical, and the debt-ridden Jeju Air, which could implode at any moment. Once they shook off the soil of the daily goods business that had provided their firmest footing, all that remained was thin, trembling ice. The second generation of Aekyung, who sold off 50 years of soap to end up only with a debt-laden airline. What exactly is this cruel and miserable scorecard they left behind telling us in the history of Korean business? What kind of warning should we be reading within this grand act of self-destruction? In fact, such tragic catastrophes are not unfamiliar scenes to us. The Woongjin Group was like that; having built an empire on study materials and water purifiers, they grew greedy to swallow a construction firm and eventually had to sell off their heart, Woongjin Coway . The downfall of the Kumho Asiana Group was the same; a solid family business built on tires and express buses took on excessive debt to acquire Asiana Airlines and a construction company, eventually losing even its corporate identity. The path taken by companies that abandoned the solid soil of their core business to chase flashy, fleeting clouds was chillingly consistent. Neither group likely made such reckless choices because they wanted to fail. It was the greed to become a massive conglomerate that others would envy. And they were simply blinded by the complacent delusion that the cash pouring in from their core business would last forever. However, they only realized too late, after the crisis had struck, that outward expansion unsupported by a core business is nothing more than a castle built on sand. The second generation of Aekyung likewise followed the trajectory of that cruel history without missing a single beat. It was so similar to the wings of Icarus, who flew toward the sun. When you fly high into the blue sky with wings made of feathers stuck together with wax, you feel like you have everything in the world. But in the face of the sun’s intense heat, that magnificent wax had no choice but to melt away in vain. In the end, all that remained was an endless plummet into the cold sea. Let's return to the two objects we saw at the beginning. The 1,000-won yellow dish soap "Trio" found by every kitchen sink in our homes. And the orange Jeju Air passenger plane, roaring as it takes off from the airport runway. The whole world marveled and cheered, watching the airplane cut through the sky so brilliantly. But for 70 years, it wasn't the airplane that fed our families, paid our employees, or served as a sturdy umbrella in the storm. It was the small, plain bar of soap Mother pressed into my hand every morning, and the single drop of dish soap that lathered so honestly. The price of forgetting the solid value of that 1,000-won bar of soap has been this harsh and devastating. The even scarier truth is that this brutal bill of reckoning hasn't ended yet. AK Holdings , the parent company that sold off its foundational soap business, is still staggering after losing most of its reliable dividend income. Jeju Air, a debt-ridden airline with a debt-to-equity ratio hovering around 1,000%, is still scraping by day-to-day by issuing high-interest perpetual bonds as of autumn 2026. For the second generation who sold Mother's soap to keep the airplanes, the market’s cold bill of reckoning is flying toward them even at this very moment.

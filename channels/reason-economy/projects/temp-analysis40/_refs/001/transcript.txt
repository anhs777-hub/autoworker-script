Volkswagen, Mercedes, and BMW. With the German automotive "Big 3" wobbling like this, rumors are spreading about whether the German economy is in danger . But is that really the case? We need to look into the fundamental position of German companies that are not clearly visible on the surface. Until the late 1990s, Germany was called the "sick man of Europe." West and East Germany had barely managed to reunify, but it wasn't all good news. Unemployment rates were high, and they had to suffer from various aftereffects of economic and social integration. However, entering the 2000s, they carried out bold labor reforms and, with strong exports, were able to rise as one of the world's top three economies. Even when the 2009 global financial crisis hit, they reduced wages instead of cutting jobs. Eventually, they prevented unemployment from rising and transformed into the most exemplary country in Europe. They completely washed away the stigma of being the "sick man of Europe." Then, starting in 2023, Germany began to fall into a slump again, and growth has stalled for the past three years. This is because Germany's pride, the automotive "Big 3," are all shaking at once. They have started to fall behind in the Chinese market, and exports have slowed due to U.S. tariff barriers. Furthermore, they are losing market share to Chinese electric vehicles even in their own backyard, Europe. So, is the German economy collapsing like this ? The conclusion is that it is not. This is because there is a real pillar that is still holding up firmly. In today’s video, we will examine the reality of that pillar, which still maintains its position as the leader of Europe. Germany has firmly established world-class small but strong companies called "hidden champions." As the name suggests, they are companies that are not well-known but rank number one in the world in their respective specialized fields. It is a term first used by a scholar named Hermann Simon, who is considered one of Europe's top management thinkers. According to his research, while some are small businesses, there are also a significant number of mid-sized companies with over 1,000 employees. Globally, about 4,000 companies are classified as hidden champions, and they reportedly employ more than 3.5 million people. The surprising thing is that nearly half of them, about 1,600, are German companies. That is overwhelmingly more than the 350 in second-place United States, while our country, Korea, is tallied at only about 22. Then, how were Germany's hidden champions created? How do they raise funds, and which industries are they mainly concentrated in? Also, how are they different from the U.S. corporate ecosystem? To properly understand them, let's go back in time to the 16th century, the era of the Holy Roman Empire. At that time, the German region was ruled by emperors of the Austrian Habsburg dynasty. They tried to unite the scattered regional lords to strengthen their ruling power. However, because the regional lords had already established roots in their own ways for a long time, constant disputes and conflicts were occurring. On top of this, religious conflict between Catholics and Protestants broke out, eventually leading to the Thirty Years 'War. And as the war ended with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, the authority of the emperor was greatly weakened. Then, the regional lords each became independent into nearly 300 small states. This is called the fragmentation of small states. Everything from the currency and legal systems they used to their financial systems began to be operated individually. And this history of decentralization still has a major influence on the ecosystem of German companies today. For survival, people had no choice but to gather in family units or rely on local communities. The robust social system that emerged at this time was the "Zunft," a guild formed by medieval artisans. Through the Zunft guild, they gained the exclusive right to operate and strictly managed their own commercial order in each region, down to setting technical wages. Although many things collapsed after shifting to a free-market economic system, there is one great system that still remains. It is the three-stage educational ladder by which masters pass on their skills. First, to learn skills, a novice technician begins at the foundational stage called an apprentice. Although they did not receive a single cent in wages, they could live in their master's house and receive full training in their craft. The next stage is called a journeyman. They were able to travel throughout the world to gain diverse field experience and practical skills. Then, they grow into the final stage, the Meister. They master their craft sufficiently to create and present their own work to the world, becoming independent. This three-stage Meister system became the solid root of Germany's vocational education today and the DNA of "Hidden Champion" companies that grow by dedicating their lives to a single technology. Then, how were they able to go beyond Germany and grow into global companies? It is at this point that a fundamental difference from the American venture capital model arises. The U.S. model is a lightning-fast approach that rapidly grows companies that have developed original technology or promising startups. Even for a small company just starting out, if there is sufficient potential, they pour in astronomical amounts of funding. Companies that everyone knows, like Apple, Google, and Amazon, were born that way. On the other hand, German " Hidden Champions" go through a gradual, step-by-step growth process. They start in small local workshops and refine a single technology for decades, or even as long as 100 years. In the US, the standard approach is to scale up through external investment and then recoup funds by going public or through M&A. On the other hand, German companies often reinvest their earnings into technology and pass the firm down to the next generation. Naturally, the capital cycle of money coming in and going out is different from that of the United States. The industries and target customers are also different. While some companies deal directly with consumers, many are B2B firms that supply core components and equipment to demanding businesses worldwide. This is why their names are unfamiliar to the general public, despite their world-class technological prowess. Let’s look at an example. To manufacture cutting-edge semiconductors , the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography equipment from the Dutch company ASML is essential. Each unit costs hundreds of billions of won, and it is a piece of equipment that is literally in short supply. Because it takes tens of thousands of ultra-precise components and a long time to build one, even global semiconductor giants like Samsung Electronics and TSMC have to line up with cash in hand just to get one. However, this equipment must contain parts from Germany’s "hidden champions." Ultra-high-power laser components made by Trumpf, located near Stuttgart, Germany, are absolutely necessary. And the precision optical components made by Carl Zeiss, headquartered in the small town of Oberkochen, are also essential. These two companies essentially hold a global monopoly on these supplies. Hidden champion companies aren't just found in the semiconductor field. Herrenknecht, which makes massive tunnel-boring machines. Flexi, which has conquered the world with its retractable dog leashes. Krones, the world leader in filling and packaging equipment for the beverages we drink every day, is one such company. These firms are all headquartered in small German towns rather than flashy metropolises, yet they are sweeping the globe. Instead of looking for short-term wins, they have focused deeply on one field, passing down technical expertise through the " Meister" system. And the technological strength accumulated that way becomes irreplaceable by anyone else. How has the money been flowing? While American-style capital moves very quickly, the German method is quite different. The scale of the funding is relatively small, and the flow is slow. This is because once they trust and invest, they quietly wait for the company to grow over a long period. This is what we call "patient capital." It is especially well-suited for mechanical engineering or ultra-precision manufacturing industries, where trust is built by communicating closely with clients while accumulating technical know-how over decades. Patient capital flows into companies through three main channels. The first is family-oriented capital known as the Mittelstand. They are extremely reluctant to list their companies on the stock market. It is to protect their independence as family businesses without being swayed by the pressure of external shareholders. Also , instead of taking profits as dividends, they stack them away in the company treasury to serve as capital for future investments. The second channel is through regional, community-oriented financial institutions called Sparkasse. They do not pursue short-term profits; rather, they are bound to the companies for decades as if sharing a common destiny. Even when the invested companies face a crisis, they do not easily cut off funding and instead act as a reliable umbrella. This is called "Hausbank," which is essentially a local community bank. The third channel is a method where company shares are transferred to a foundation, and the business is managed through a board of trustees. Carl Zeiss, which has been run for over 130 years by a foundation established by its founder, and Bosch, the world's largest automotive parts company, are prime examples. They were able to prevent the tragedy of a company being split up due to a lack of successors or massive inheritance taxes, and protect centuries of tradition from hostile takeovers. Of course, this patient capital system is not always positive. It is difficult to drive game-changing, adventurous innovation, and because they are not listed on the stock market , they are criticized for concentrating the fruits of growth only within a few families. However, without this "quiet" money that served as a lifeline for decades, these now-famous "hidden champions" would never have been born. The German automotive industry is currently facing a very difficult phase . Of course, it is the big three automakers that are taking the brunt of these rough waves head-on. But there are others suffering even more painful agony. They are the numerous parts suppliers that have grown as one with the big three. From large parts manufacturers boasting top-tier technology like ZF, Schaeffler, and Continental to the small businesses tightly woven beneath them, all are standing on the brink of survival. The grim reality of mass layoffs, factory closures, and chain bankruptcies continues to unfold. Yet, there are still sturdy pillars that are holding up well. Even though automakers are in a difficult situation, the 1,600 German hidden champions are standing firm. Born from the Meister tradition in 300 fragmented small states and grown by focusing on one niche with the support of patient capital, these companies are silently holding their ground. However, we cannot be entirely at ease. A time of immense challenge is approaching for them as well. The first is the crisis of corporate succession. As the number of children to inherit the business decreases and more young people choose different paths, companies with centuries of tradition that relied on family succession are contemplating closing their doors. The second issue is that the links in the technology ladder are breaking. Due to the sluggish economy, vocational training opportunities are shrinking, and the ladder of expertise, which was the greatest weapon of the "Hidden Champions," is wavering. The third is China, which has caught up to them. China, once a student of German technology, is now using its massive capital and speed to fiercely push Germany out of markets like electric vehicle battery equipment. The fourth is the software void. While Germany is brilliant at precision engineering, it has a fatal weakness: it is structurally too slow to respond to innovations in AI and digital platforms , the "staples" of future industries. However, Germany is not standing still; it is struggling to change. This is because its traditional "patient capital" is transforming into venture capital. At the government level, they have begun to boldly pour funds into early-stage new technologies that the private sector is afraid to touch. They call this the evolution of "New Mittelstand," combining the depth of a Meister with the speed of AI, and they are holding onto new hope. Then, what about South Korea? We achieved rapid compressed growth through strong leadership from the state and large conglomerates. But unfortunately, we haven't fully grown the solid foundation of a centuries-old ladder of expertise or patient capital that waits silently for companies, as Germany has. If the major conglomerates that lead our economy were to be severely shaken, would we still have a sturdy pillar to support the bottom of our economy? That’s all for today’s story.

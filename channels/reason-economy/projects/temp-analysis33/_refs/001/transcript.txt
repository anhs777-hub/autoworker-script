Everyone, where did you heat up your bowl of rice for dinner today? It was probably in a microwave in the corner of your kitchen. Do you know which country invented this item? It was the United States. It was born in a U.S. company that made radar equipment the year World War II ended. Now, it would only be natural if the home appliance companies dominating American kitchens today were also American. However, if you check the 2023 U.S. home appliance sales rankings, Samsung is first and LG is second. Two Korean companies are standing side-by-side at the very front of the kitchen in the country that invented it. To use an analogy, it’s like if the kimchi refrigerators in every home in Korea, the country that invented them, were all products from foreign companies. From our perspective , that’s hard to imagine. But in the U.S., that is exactly what happened. The place where this reversal began was , surprisingly, not the U.S. It was the hallway of Samsung’s microwave research lab in Suwon, Gyeonggi-do, in 1983. The head of the home appliance division at General Electric (GE), then the No. 1 U.S. appliance maker, walked down that hallway once and decided to source his company’s microwaves from Asia. And this was only a few years after Samsung had just one hand-assembled prototype to its name. What on earth did he see in that hallway that made the appliance king of the inventing nation step down? And how did a company whose first prototype melted itself rise to become the world No. 1? Many people brush it off, saying it’s because Korean labor costs were cheap. If you watch until the end, you’ll see that the real difference that decided the outcome wasn’t the paycheck. Let’s start from the beginning. In October 1945, the U.S. defense contractor Raytheon filed a patent for an engineer’s invention. It was a cooking device born from the curiosity of Percy Spencer, who worked next to radar equipment and noticed a chocolate bar in his pocket melting. It works on the principle of using microwaves to shake water molecules in food to generate heat. The component at the heart of this principle is a vacuum tube called a magnetron. Inside a vacuum-sealed tube, a heated electrode emits electrons, and strong external magnets catch those electrons, preventing them from moving in a straight line and making them spin. That rotation creates radio waves that oscillate 2.45 billion times per second , and these waves shake the water molecules in food to heat it. Technology to maintain a vacuum, and strong magnets. Because the metal structures, carved to the thickness of a human hair, must all work together, only a handful of countries could build this. That is why it is called the heart of the microwave. The first commercial product was a far cry from what we know today. The 1947 model was about 180cm tall, roughly the height of an adult. It weighed about 340kg and cost around 5,000 dollars. It wasn't for kitchens, but a commercial machine for places like restaurants or ships. Across the ocean, Japan quickly caught up with this product. Sharp began mass-producing commercial microwaves in 1962, and in 1966, they released the first home model with a rotating turntable. That glass plate spinning inside our microwaves today actually originated in Japan. Then, in 1967, Raytheon subsidiary Amana released a countertop home model for 495 dollars, and it began to spread into American homes in earnest. The giant that jumped into the game late was GE. Starting from the company of the inventor Edison , it was a firm that dominated large household appliances like refrigerators , washing machines, and ovens in American homes. Its headquarters were in Louisville, Kentucky, and it had a large appliance factory complex that opened in 1970 in Columbia, Maryland. At its peak, 2,800 people worked there. From the late 1970s, GE developed this complex into a microwave production base, targeting dual-income families with little time to cook as their main customers. However, microwaves were a sore spot for GE. Because they entered late, they never once made a profit on this item. In the early 1980s, as Japanese companies aggressively penetrated the US market with cheap products, the deficit grew even larger. Still, the people at GE were not very worried. They believed that American consumers would ultimately buy based on the GE name. So, where did Korea stand at that same time? Let’s go to the other side of the globe, to Suwon. In 1977, American management consultant Ira Magaziner visited Samsung's Suwon complex for the first time. He later became a policy advisor to the US President, but at the time, he was advising US companies like GE on their strategies. The Samsung research lab appeared humble to his eyes. While their television work seemed decent enough, he judged that Korea lacked the depth of world-class engineers needed to compete in the global market. He didn't even believe the young lead engineer who said Samsung would soon be exporting. He figured it would take at least 10 years. But in one corner of that lab, work was already underway. The company had tasked an engineer to put together a team to build a microwave. With no proper equipment, the first thing the team did was buy a GE microwave from the US and take it apart down to the last screw. They understood the casing and wiring quickly, but no matter how they looked, they couldn't grasp the principles of several key internal parts. After a year of working 80 hours a week, they finally flipped the switch on their first prototype. The results were disastrous. The plastic wall inside the cooking chamber couldn't withstand the heat and melted into a mush. The food was supposed to be heated, but the machine itself ended up cooking instead . The team immediately overhauled the design and completed their second prototype in June 1978. In 1979, the first domestic microwave oven finally made its debut. The model, R700D, started appearing in stores that August . They boasted that it cooked seven times faster than coal and three times faster than gas, with fuel costs just a fraction of coal. The problem was that there were almost no homes in Korea at the time that could afford such an expensive item. With nowhere to sell them domestically, the only way forward was abroad. And that year, Samsung accomplished something else. They succeeded in miniaturizing the magnetron, the vacuum tube that acts as the heart of the microwave. This component even passed rigorous certification tests like the FCC in the U.S. and CSA in Canada. In just two years, the team went from learning by taking apart others 'machines to crafting the core components themselves . Unexpectedly, the place that first opened the door for exports was Panama in Central America. The order was for 240 units. After manufacturing, shipping, and balancing the books, it turned out to be a loss. Even so, it is said that a modest celebration was held in Suwon. There was something more important than money. The fact that a microwave made by our own hands had crossed the border for the first time. By the end of that year, they even received approval to sell in the United States. In 1980, a truly big door opened. J.C. Penney, a large retailer with stores across the U.S., reached out to them. They asked if Samsung could produce a microwave that they could sell in their stores for 299 dollars. The order was for several thousand units. Compared to the entire U.S. market, it was a negligible volume . But the Samsung managers were overjoyed and promised to meet whatever that retailer required. There is a point to note here. The company did not pressure this business to turn a profit right away. They promised to buy any necessary equipment, and only demanded two things. Deliver the promised goods on time. And set a foothold in the U.S. distribution network. In other words, they were prioritizing winning a loyal customer's trust over making money on the first deal. The pace picked up quickly after that. In 1982, investing 200 million won in research, they launched a multi-purpose microwave with a screen. With the press of a button, it could automatically cook 30 dishes, and you could watch TV or check who was at the front door on the attached small screen. It even won an award at an international invention exhibition. They had put the television technology, which a consultant had called the only thing worth using five years prior, right into the microwave. Then, on August 24, 1983, Samsung invested 10 billion won to complete its magnetron factory in Suwon. It was the third-largest facility in the world at the time, producing one million units a year. By manufacturing the heart of the component they had previously imported entirely, they were able to replace 15 million dollars worth of imports. The surprising part came two months later. On October 21, they signed a contract to export 100,000 of those very magnetrons, worth 1.85 million dollars, to the United States. They had started selling the component they used to import back to the country that invented it. Now, let’s return to the U.S. While Samsung was racing ahead, how was GE’s microwave business faring in the early 1980s? Looking at how they worked, it was like this. The lab was at the headquarters in Louisville, Kentucky, while the production line was hundreds of kilometers away in Maryland. Insisting on managing everything from Louisville, headquarters rented a row of apartments for engineers near the factory, and planes carrying test results flew between the two cities almost daily. They also poured millions of dollars into renovating the factory. A large company was working in a large, cumbersome way. In the midst of this, in March 1983, GE’s marketing head arrived in Suwon with an advisory group . He came to see firsthand if there was anywhere in Asia worth sourcing microwaves from. As the first day’s schedule ended, he left them with a task. "Give me a proposal detailing how much you can make them for and when you can deliver them." However, he was scheduled to leave for the U.S. the very next morning. There was only one night left. The head of Samsung’s home appliances division instructed his engineers. "Finish it by tonight." There was only one thing he wanted to show them. That they could produce a product without any compromise in quality at a lower cost than what GE was spending in Maryland. The lights in the Suwon laboratory did not go out that night. The next morning, while the GE executive was having his final meal with Samsung’s directors, the door opened. The engineers, who hadn't slept a wink, walked in carrying the documents. Their hair was disheveled and their eyes were bloodshot. But when he opened the documents, he was astonished. They were exactly in line with the targets GE had wanted. He later remembered that morning as a scene that was hard to believe. Looking at the actual numbers, the difference was this. Back in 1983, GE spent 218 dollars to produce one microwave, while Samsung spent 155 dollars. That’s a gap of over 60 dollars per unit. But I will explain where this 60-dollar difference came from a little later. That’s because the real twist of this story is hidden there. A few weeks later, someone much higher up came to Suwon. This is Roger Schipke, who oversaw GE's entire home appliance business. He originally managed a small division like dishwashers, but rose to head of appliances after significantly boosting their market share. His personality was said to be cautious and conservative. He was walking down a research lab hallway, guided by people from Samsung. A crowd of young people in white uniforms came rushing from the opposite direction. He had to press his back against the wall to let them pass. There were dozens of them. The Samsung employee guiding him said, "Those are the new microwave engineers we just hired." The number of them was greater than all the engineers in his entire microwave division. And that was just counting the fresh recruits. Among them were people who had returned after studying at prestigious American engineering schools. He did the math in his head. Just in terms of technical personnel, Louisville was lagging by about ten times. While one side was struggling to spend millions of dollars to fix a single factory, the other side had young technicians filling the hallways and churning out units for $ 60 less each. He concluded that he could not win this fight. The decision came quickly. In June 1983, GE headquarters announced that they would buy small and medium-sized microwaves from Asia and only manufacture the large ones themselves. The largest order went to a Japanese firm, while Samsung received a trial order of about 15,000 units. It was a way to test if Korea could really match that quality at that price. GE sent their technicians to Suwon to teach them their quality standards. From GE's perspective, it was just managing a subcontractor. But Samsung took this as an opportunity to learn world-class technology for free. And they hammered home one single ironclad rule to their employees. Meet the delivery deadline, no matter what. There were initial quality issues, but they were quickly resolved with the help of GE engineers, and the GE manager reportedly became increasingly impressed. The end result was this. In 1985, GE completely shut down its microwave production lines on American soil. Nearly 900 jobs disappeared from the Maryland plant. Microwaves with the GE label continued to be sold, but the interiors were made in Japanese and Korean factories. And the Columbia appliance complex, where 2,800 people once worked, officially closed its doors in December 1990 after letting go of the last remaining employees. However, it wasn't just microwaves that GE stepped away from. In 1984, they sold off their entire small appliance business—irons, toasters, coffee makers—to Black& Decker. In 1987, they handed over their entire television business to the French electronics company Thomson. Even after having bought RCA, the symbol of American television, just one year earlier. If a business couldn't be first or second, it was to be sold off. This was the principle set forth by Jack Welch when he became the head of GE in 1981. Thanks to this principle, GE focused its power on lucrative businesses like finance, medical devices, and power generation, becoming the most valuable company in the world at one point. However, the price for this was that the items GE personally made began to disappear from American kitchens and living rooms one by one. The final turn for this principle was large home appliances, where refrigerators and washing machines remained. In 2016, GE sold its entire home appliance business to China's Haier for 5.4 billion dollars. They didn't sell it because they were failing; they put it up for sale themselves to transform into a digital industrial company. What is strange is what happened next. Haier didn't buy the name "GE," but agreed to rent it for 40 years, including extensions. They also decided to keep the headquarters in Louisville as it was. The owner of the products that Americans still call "GE appliances" is now a Chinese company. Of course, there is a perspective that applies this story directly to Korea. It was reported that in April of this year, Samsung Electronics' appliance division decided to shift mid-priced products like microwaves and dishwashers to external manufacturers and shut down its Malaysia factory, which had been producing small appliances since 1989. This is because, with the low-cost offensive from Chinese companies combined with rising parts costs and logistics expenses, the appliance and TV business posted an operating loss of 200 billion won last year. However, it is difficult to see it as identical to GE in 1983. Back then, GE decided it couldn't win and stepped out of the game, eventually ending up in a position where it had to rent out its own name. Samsung, on the other hand, is planning to shed low-profit assembly within the same market and shift its people and money toward expensive, high-margin premium products, AI appliances, and building cooling systems. All while keeping its brand, technology, and customers intact. Leaving the game and choosing one's position on the board are two different things. Now, let's return to the question we put aside earlier. The cost difference that exceeded 60 dollars per unit. Was this really caused by cheap Korean labor costs? If you look at the analysis of this competition as summarized in the Harvard Business Review at the time, there was certainly a difference in assembly worker labor costs. Because wages in Korea in the 1980s were much lower than in the US. But if you break it down by category, the biggest gap wasn't labor costs. It was the administrative costs incurred in managing factories and running headquarters. The administrative cost per unit for GE was 10 dollars, while for Samsung, it was 2 cents. That is a 500-fold difference. Indirect costs for supervising the site and maintaining equipment, as well as the costs for moving parts within the factory, were also several times heavier for GE. Productivity was the same story. The number of microwaves one person produced in a day was four for GE and nine for Samsung. Do you remember the plane that flew back and forth between Louisville and Maryland every day? One side flew a plane just to move a single test result, while the other side, having their lab and factory in the same complex, pulled all-nighters to generate quotes. In the end, what decided the outcome wasn't the size of the paycheck, but the way they worked. There is one more twist. The analysts who wrote the report pointed this out. They said Samsung wouldn't have grown this fast without orders from JC Penney and GE. By meeting the standards of demanding American customers, they gained design skills, quality control, mass production experience, and a track record to show buyers in other countries. The quality training that GE considered mere subcontract management essentially ended up cultivating a competitor that would take its place. The magazine summarized Samsung's secret in three words. People, production, and patriotism. The results appear in the numbers. Around 1988, in terms of annual microwave production, they surpassed all of Japan's Matsushita, Sanyo, and Sharp. These three were veterans who had already been selling microwaves for over a decade while Samsung was still struggling with prototypes in the lab. Starting from a melted prototype in 1979, Samsung overtook all those veterans in less than ten years to become the world's largest microwave manufacturer. Of course, success brought opposition. In the fall of 1986 , the European Community launched a dumping investigation into Korean microwaves, and French customs blocked imports, citing safety standards. Samsung broke through head-on. Having already established its first overseas factory in Portugal in 1982, they built more in the UK in 1987 and Malaysia in 1989 to manufacture products directly near where they were sold. Meanwhile, they expanded the domestic market by releasing a "Korean-style" microwave that could cook rice and stews. And what about that Europe now? Market research firm Euromonitor tracked sales in 19 European countries, and Samsung microwaves have been#1 for 10 consecutive years from 2015 to 2024. They even received certification for ceramic interior coating that kills 99.9%of bacteria. They now stand at the very front of the market that once locked its doors against them. LG followed the same path. In 2002, LG Electronics produced 12.1 million microwaves a year, rising to#1 in global production. It meant two Korean companies took turns occupying the top spot in the world's microwave factories . It wasn't just two Korean companies, either. Daewoo Electronics, which jumped into microwave production in 1985, surpassed 50 million units in cumulative production in 2003 after 18 years, and broke through 100 million in 2016. Samsung entered the Russian market in 1991 and sold 10 million microwaves there alone by 2008. Microwaves from Korean factories found their way into kitchens across the US, Europe, and Russia. Finally, let's revisit the American kitchen we asked about at the beginning. In 2023, the US home appliance market share was 21%for Samsung, 19%for LG, 18%for GE—now using its brand name under a Chinese firm—and 15%for Whirlpool. When American consumers were asked which brands they consider when buying new appliances, Samsung was cited most often. Back in a 1983 hallway in Suwon, one company left, while the other stayed and held on until the end. Forty years later, it was the one that stayed that stood at the forefront of the inventor's own kitchens. We see this story as reading as follows. Invention happens once, but the top spot is redefined every time by the ability to make things better. Although the US invented the microwave, it was the Korean engineers—who wrote estimates all night and tore apart melted machines—who clung to the way to make them best. Behind that single bowl of rice you warmed up tonight are the 40 years of those young technicians who once filled that hallway.

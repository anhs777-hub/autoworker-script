Everyone, 600 billion won is truly an astronomical amount. And they didn't even sell cars or semiconductors. There was a company that earned over 600 billion won a year just from that one thing we put in our living rooms: massage chairs. It was the brand that every wealthy home in Gangnam had to have, and it made people wonder during every holiday if they should get one as a gift for their parents. The commercials where you'd turn on the TV and see a handsome actor lying comfortably, saying, "Father, it's time to rest now." Those very ads. The name nine out of ten people thought of when hearing "massage chair" was Bodyfriend. But here's the thing. Do you know what has become of this king of the massage chair industry? With three consecutive years of deficits and a massive debt bomb of 230 billion won, the private equity firm and the founder are now waging a war, suing each other in court over who owns the company. And more than anything, the consumers who were once crazy about this company are now saying this: "Do you think consumers are pushovers?" Now, we need to start by looking at how incredible this company was to make the story interesting. In the past, a massage chair was just that thing in the corner of a local bathhouse or sauna where you'd put in a 500-won coin to get pounded. It was far from having a premium image. But Bodyfriend completely changed that. They turned the massage chair from a simple home appliance into a symbol of success, a must-have item in the living rooms of successful people. This was truly brilliant marketing. What was their method? First, it was advertisements featuring top stars. By using famous actors and sports stars everyone knows, they planted the image of "You've succeeded in life, now take a rest in this chair." Second was home shopping. When they sold massage chairs on TV home shopping channels, it was an instant sell-out. When the show host would shout, "Orders are pouring in right now!" The phones would literally fly off the hook. And decisively, the " filial piety" code for parents. As a dutiful child, you want to massage your aging parents 'shoulders, but since you can't do it every day, this chair will do it for you. This sentiment struck a chord perfectly with children in their 40s and 50s. What was the result? When the COVID-19 pandemic hit, people were stuck at home. As the trend of taking care of your health at home exploded, these massage chairs started selling like wildfire. The peak of that was in 2021. In that year alone, Bodyfriend recorded a staggering 611 billion won in revenue. It was the highest record since the company was founded. The operating profit—the actual profit they put in their pockets—was 88.3 billion won. They earned that much just by selling massage chairs. They were truly the king of massage chairs, the absolute number one in the South Korean market. Looking only this far, it really is a perfect success story. But here is the thing. In the shadow of this spectacular success, cracks were starting to form, one by one. The first crack was the price. Have you ever looked into the price of a massage chair? It’s quite interesting; someone once told me this. "When they first came out, even a 2 million won chair seemed incredibly expensive, but I made up my mind to buy one for my parents, and before I knew it, they were 10 million won." 2 million won is already a lot of money, but at some point, 10 million won massage chairs appeared. That’s the price of a car. From the consumer’s perspective, they couldn't help but wonder, "Why on earth is a chair this expensive?" But the bigger problem was whether this high price was actually worth it. One consumer says this. "I bought a 900,000 won massage chair, and there wasn’t much difference from the expensive ones .""It's all a bubble." Someone even said they asked an expert, who told them that expensive stone beds are no different from cheap ones. They were told that massage chairs are the same. Of course, whether that’s really true might differ from person to person, but the important thing is that the suspicion, "Isn't this just a bubble?" started to spread among consumers. There was one more thing that added fuel to the fire. It was the controversy over Chinese-made parts. People bought them at a high price, believing they were a leading domestic massage chair brand, only to find out that a significant portion was made in China. That’s how this story emerged. In fact, a media report noted that a new Bodyfriend product was swept up in a "Made in China" controversy, even with a price tag of 5.8 million won. What did the consumers say? They claimed the company was ripping off consumers by disguising Chinese products as high-end goods. Nearly 1,000 people liked that comment. This means it wasn't just a complaint from one or two people, but that quite a lot of people felt the same way. There is one more thing consumers pointed out here. "You should avoid products from companies that spend so much on advertising.""They use top stars because they aren't confident in their product, and they're overcharging us." This is painful because, in reality, Bodyfriend spends an enormous amount on advertising. As I’ll mention again later, even when the company hit hard times, their advertising expenses actually increased. So, from the consumer’s perspective, it leads to the suspicion: "Did the high price I paid go toward the celebrity's ad fee rather than the product quality?" They begin to have those doubts. Now, up to this point, it was just at the level of "It's a bit pricey" or "There's a bubble." True trust began to collapse after that. So why did consumers turn their backs so completely? The second crack. This was truly decisive. It was the lies. Everyone, massage chairs massage the body and relieve fatigue. You all understand up to this point, right? But Bodyfriend went much further than this. While selling massage chairs for teenagers, what did they claim in their ads? That it helps you grow taller. They said it increases the speed of brain fatigue recovery. The idea that sitting in a massage chair makes you taller is quite tempting, isn't it? What parent wouldn't be swayed by the claim that it helps their child grow? But it turned out this wasn't true. In 2020, the Fair Trade Commission, the national agency that monitors whether companies are deceiving consumers, investigated. The KFTC conducted an investigation. Do you know what the result was? Bodyfriend had never even conducted a clinical trial to prove the height-growth effect . They just advertised that it would happen. Even more absurd is that the clinical trial they cited for brain fatigue recovery was an experiment conducted on their own employees. This is problematic because company employees are people who might feel pressured to produce results favorable to the company. So, experiments like this are supposed to be reviewed by an Institutional Review Board, but they didn't even do that. The KFTC eventually issued a corrective order, imposed a fine of 22 million won, and even reported them to the prosecution. The problem is that this wasn't the end of it. In 2024, they got caught again. This time, they wrote in the manual for their "Ajera Plus" massage chair that it was effective for improving concentration and memory. That sitting in the massage chair improves concentration and increases memory. Of course, there was no objective basis for this either. The KFTC again issued a corrective order and imposed a fine of 46 million won. What did the KFTC say? They created false and exaggerated information by using an indirect method , sneaking claims they couldn't put in ads into the product manual. It was very cunning. How angry must the consumers have been when they saw this? One viewer says this: All those YouTube ads saying it hits growth plates and makes you 17cm taller are scams. The expression is a bit harsh, but it shows how deep their sense of betrayal was. Think about it. You spent a lot of money you didn't really have so your child would grow tall and study well, only to find out it was a baseless lie. It's not just that the product was bad; it's that they lied to you. People can tolerate a product that isn't very good . But they cannot tolerate being deceived. This is exactly the point where consumers' hearts began to turn cold. But the sense of betrayal didn't end there. The third crack was the problem after purchasing. A massage chair isn't a product you buy once and never think about again. Since it's a product used for a long time, it breaks , the seat needs replacing, and it requires maintenance. And that's where complaints about after-sales service exploded. One consumer laments like this. I requested a seat replacement, but it’s been a month and I’ve heard nothing. What kind of company is this? You pay a high price for it, so how frustrated would you be if the service is this poor? On top of that, these massage chairs are hard to get rid of. They’re bulky and heavy, you know. One consumer shared this experience. They said that when the upholstery wears out and the chair gets old, even if you try to give it away for free on apps like Karrot, nobody wants it. Why? Because the cost of hiring a truck to move it is more than the chair is worth. Even if someone takes it, the inside is often crumbling and shedding debris, so it just ends up being passed on as a nuisance. They’re expensive to buy, difficult to maintain, and costly to dispose of, leaving consumers in a bind. There was one more thing that really infuriated consumers. It was the issue of rental cancellation fees. Since massage chairs are so expensive, many people opted for rental plans to pay monthly instead of all at once. But if you had to cancel the contract midway, you had to pay 30% of the remaining balance as a penalty. How much money is that? One consumer posted a title online saying, "Do not buy Bodyfriend." They paid the 30% penalty and switched to a different product. They said they hoped no one else would go through what they did. Why did this become a controversy? For other rental products like water or air purifiers, the standard early cancellation fee is around 10%, according to the Korea Consumer Agency. But Bodyfriend was charging 30%, which was three times the standard. As the controversy grew, Bodyfriend quietly lowered the penalty, but by then, consumers were already convinced that the company saw them as easy targets. One consumer's words summarize it all. Why did you sell these Chinese products if you weren't going to provide proper after-sales service or maintenance? Do you think customers are suckers? Now, just as consumers started turning their backs one by one, another storm was brewing from the outside. The market itself had changed. As I mentioned earlier, massage chairs were selling like hotcakes during the COVID-19 pandemic. But that was the trap. Everyone who wanted one bought it during that period. One consumer hit the nail on the head. It’s just a product that everyone who wanted one already bought. The fact that the trend has passed also plays a part. That’s right. Massage chairs aren't like cars that you replace every few years. Once you buy one, you use it for over 10 years. So, after the pandemic boom caused everyone to buy one, there was no one left to buy new ones. The numbers are shocking. Revenue, which was 610 billion won in 2021, plummeted to 422.6 billion won in 2024. It shrank by more than 30%in just three years. Operating profit is even worse. It plummeted from 88.3 billion won to 11.5 billion won. And then there's net income. I mean, the final profit actually turned into a deficit starting in 2023. A 6.3 billion won loss in 2023 . A 7.6 billion won loss in 2024. And a 5.9 billion won loss the following year . They've been in the red for three consecutive years. The emperor stopped making money and started losing it. But what was even more painful? They lost their throne to a rival. You've heard of a company called Ceragem, right? It's famous for those thermal massage beds that are good for your spine. In 2024, Ceragem recorded 549.8 billion won in revenue. That's much higher than Bodyfriend. Operating profit was 25.8 billion won, a massive 1091%jump from a year ago. It surged more than tenfold. While Bodyfriend struggled with losses, Ceragem took flight. On top of that, Coway, famous for water purifiers, entered the massage chair market with its "BEREX" brand. They are effectively being attacked from all sides. The massage chair emperor has been pushed off the throne and is now being chased by its pursuers. Now, hearing this, you might think: "Oh, so it's just a bad market and they lost the competition." Of course, that's part of it. But here's the thing. The real knockout blow that ruined Bodyfriend was something else. It wasn't outside, but inside the company. What on earth was happening inside the company? Bodyfriend is currently in the middle of a literal war over who owns the company. It’s a bit complicated, so let me explain it as simply as possible . In 2022, two private equity firms joined forces to buy Bodyfriend. What is a private equity fund? Simply put, it’s an investment firm that pools money from various people to buy and sell companies for profit. The two firms were Stonebridge Capital and Hahn &Brothers. They pooled their money to acquire a 46.3%stake, becoming Bodyfriend's largest shareholder. In other words, they became the new owners . The problem is that while they were partners at first, they clashed over how to manage the company. One side wanted to take control, while the other insisted they work together as agreed. It’s already complicated, but then one more person gets involved. That’s the founder and former chairman, Kang Woong-chul. He had remained the second-largest shareholder even after selling the company to the private equity firms. But when the funds became actively involved in management, he tried to return, arguing he was the one who built the company. In the end, it turned into a three-way battle for control of the company. It would have been fortunate if this had ended as a mere war of words, but it escalated into a mudslinging fight with lawsuits filed against each other with the prosecution. One side sued the founder, accusing him of embezzling company funds. They claimed he pocketed personal profits through things like employee invention compensation, consulting fees, and corporate cards. In response, the founder filed a countersuit, claiming they harmed the company while acquiring it using borrowed money. And, in fact, the prosecution sent both the founder and the private equity major shareholders to trial. The company's owners are now tearing each other apart in court. There is a very interesting story here. It is said that this kind of talk circulated among former employees who left the company. That the founder was splashing company funds around recklessly. Even a consumer reported there were many rumors that the CEO was squandering company money. It is truly infuriating to think that the high price consumers paid for massage chairs might have leaked out into these things instead of being used for the company. Now, why is this internal strife so fatal? Think of a ship. A large ship is sailing, and three people are fighting with knives over the captain's seat. What would happen to the ship in the meantime? It cannot set a course, and if a hole springs up, no one patches it ; it just drifts. The company was the same. Because the management was fighting, they couldn't make important decisions on time. They couldn't quickly decide on anything, whether to invest, what to cut, or how to overcome the crisis. In the meantime, the company's image kept worsening and sales continued to decline. And there was a real bomb here. It was debt. When the private equity funds bought this company, they didn't just use their own money; they bought it by borrowing a huge amount from financial institutions like banks. This is called acquisition financing, and its scale was a staggering 230 billion won. This debt must be repaid when the due date arrives. But as the company's situation worsened, they ran out of money to pay it back. So, Bodyfriend ended up in a position where they had to beg the financial institutions that lent them the money to extend the maturity. They were in a position of asking for more favorable terms. Why is this such a scary situation? If they fail to get a maturity extension, an "event of default" occurs. It's a difficult term, but simply put, it's like this. The bank can decide that the debt, which was originally supposed to be paid back slowly in installments, is no longer acceptable. They gain the right to demand that the entire amount be paid back right away. There is no way the company has that much cash on hand. In the worst-case scenario, the company could even go bankrupt. A 230 billion won time bomb was ticking away. How did the company try to overcome this crisis ? Originally, they planned to list the company on the stock market—that is, to raise a large sum of money by selling shares. But even this plan for an IPO has effectively fallen through. With the owners fighting like that, poor performance, and endless controversies, who would want to buy stock in this company? When the dream of going public eventually hit a dead end, the largest shareholder changed course. They decided to sell the company altogether. In other words, they decided to sell it off and wash their hands of it. The emperor who once ruled the massage chair market has ended up as a piece of property for sale on the market. Now, let’s wrap this up. Why did this emperor, who once wrote a 600-billion-won success story, collapse? Here is what I think. What actually brought down Bodyfriend was not the massage chair product itself. What collapsed was, in fact, trust. First, inflated prices combined with controversies over Chinese-made parts raised suspicions of being a rip-off, and second, claims that it increases height. Deceiving consumers with baseless false advertisements about improving concentration caused a sense of betrayal, and third, selling at high prices while providing poor after-sales service and charging excessive penalties led to being branded as treating customers like fools. And finally, inside the company, the owners engaged in mudslinging fights over money and power, while leaving 230 billion won in debt neglected. Consumers turned their backs on them from the outside, and the management destroyed themselves from the inside. As these four factors overlapped, the myth came crumbling down. Of course, Bodyfriend is not just sitting idly by. They claim they will transform from a simple massage chair company into an AI healthcare robot company, launching a new product called "Q3" and planning to export their technology overseas. They are touting advanced technology that allows legs to move independently and aids in full-body exercise. Whether this will succeed or fail is not something I can say for sure. However, experts all point out the same thing. No matter how good the technology is, it’s useless if they can’t regain the consumer trust they once lost. There is also a cold perspective questioning how many 10-million-won robot massage chairs can be sold when the economy is bad and people's pockets are tight. Ultimately, the story of Bodyfriend leaves us with a very bitter but clear lesson. That no matter how successful a company is, the moment it starts treating consumers like fools, its myth begins to end. A company that hid behind flashy advertisements and top stars to inflate the real value of its products, deceived consumers for short-term profit, and festered with infighting over money. I suspect the current state of Bodyfriend is exactly that end. That glorious number of 600 billion has now become a bitter figure that highlights the scale of their downfall.

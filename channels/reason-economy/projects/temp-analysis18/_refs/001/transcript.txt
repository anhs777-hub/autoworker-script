966 km on one full tank and one full charge. That's enough to drive from Seoul to Busan and back with 160 km to spare. Last August, at a hotel in Yeouido, Seoul, Hyundai Motor unveiled this figure to investors from around the world. But the real story today isn't about this distance. It's hidden in the size of the battery that will go into this car. When most people hear the number 966 km, they think like this : "Just how massive a battery did they put in there?" But the reality is the exact opposite. Hyundai didn't increase the battery size; they cut it by more than half. 966 km without even using half a battery. That is the true meaning behind those words. Typically, a large electric SUV uses a battery of around 100 kWh. However, this car of the same class plans to run on a battery of only 40 to 50 kWh, less than half that size. The battery was cut in half, but the range actually increased. Behind these contradictory numbers lie 15 years of accumulated technology from Korean engineers. The name of this car is the Santa Fe EREV. It stands for Extended Range Electric Vehicle. Just hearing the name, you might wonder how it's any different from a hybrid. But the structure is completely different. In a hybrid, the engine and motor work together to turn the wheels. But in an EREV, it is only the electric motor that turns the wheels. The engine is not mechanically connected to the wheels. The only job the engine has is to generate electricity to keep the battery charged. To put it in perspective: If a hybrid's engine is a second horse pulling the carriage, an EREV's engine is a generator loaded on the back of the carriage. There is only one electric motor pulling the carriage , and the generator only serves to keep feeding that motor. So the sensation you feel in the driver's seat is exactly that of an electric vehicle. That feeling of being quiet and leaping forward the moment you press the pedal. Then how is it different from a plug-in hybrid? Plug-in hybrids can be charged, too, but eventually, there comes a moment when the engine directly turns the wheels. They require a transmission and complex devices to switch between the engine and the motor. The EREV has completely severed that link. They have clearly divided the roles: the engine is only for power generation, and the motor is only for driving. According to Hyundai’s revealed plan, this car's battery is about 55%smaller than that of a pure electric vehicle. So the "50% " mentioned in the title isn't about the remaining battery charge, but about the battery size itself. This is significant because the battery is the most expensive and heaviest part of an electric vehicle. The battery pack of a large SUV weighs hundreds of kilograms alone. It’s like constantly carrying several adults underneath the floor of your car. If you reduce that battery by more than half, the car gets lighter, the price goes down, and it travels further on the same amount of electricity. And there is one thing you must remember here. It is the side of this car. Why specifically the side, you ask? I will show you the reason myself in a moment. But a strange point arises here. Wouldn't it be normal for the power to be halved if the battery is cut in half? Usually, that is the case. In an electric vehicle, the battery acts as both the fuel tank and the heart. How much electricity can be poured into the motor at once? This is exactly what determines acceleration. So, if the battery is small, it is common that not only the driving range decreases, but the instantaneous power also weakens. It is originally very difficult to powerfully drive a heavy SUV with a small battery. This is where the card Hyundai has pulled out comes in. It is a new high-performance battery cell designed by Hyundai itself . Kim Chang-hwan, Vice President leading the Electrification Energy Solution division at Hyundai Motor Group, said this on the Investor Day stage. This cell has more than twice the output of existing high-nickel batteries and a 40%shorter charging time. What does it mean to have double the output? To put it simply, it is like this. Let's assume there are two identical water containers. One has a thin faucet and the other has one twice as thick. Even if the amount of water is small, the water pouring out at once will be much greater if the faucet is thick. What Hyundai touched was not the size of the container, but the faucet itself. Vice President Kim went a step further. He said the ERV battery can deliver the same output and driving performance as an Ioniq EV with less than 50%of the capacity. The battery is half the size, but the power remains the same. The car becomes lighter and the cost goes down. And a 40%reduction in charging time. You shouldn't just overlook this number either. Having a smaller battery also means you have to charge more frequently. But if charging becomes faster, that disadvantage largely disappears. For example, if it took 30 minutes to charge, it now finishes in 18 minutes. It means the charging is done while you order and wait for a cup of coffee. It is a structure where fast charging makes up for the weakness of a small battery. But there is an even more surprising fact here. Although Hyundai designed this battery, they do not manufacture it themselves. What does that mean, you ask? Let me talk about semiconductors for a moment. The chips used in iPhones are designed by Apple. But the factories that actually produce those chips are not Apple's. They are made by TSMC of Taiwan. Companies that only handle design are called fabless, and companies that specialize in production are called foundries. Do you remember what happened when Apple started designing its own chips? Unlike the days when they used off-the-shelf chips , they began outperforming competitors in speed and battery efficiency by using chips perfectly tailored to their products. Hyundai Motor has chosen this exact approach for its batteries. The structure is that Hyundai designs the cells needed for its vehicles, while specialized partners handle mass production. Korean media is calling this a "fabless-foundry strategy" for batteries. So, why don't they manufacture them directly? Building a single battery factory costs trillions of won. Hyundai decided to focus that money on design capabilities rather than factories. Why is this important? Until now, car companies have been in a position where they had to buy cells made by battery manufacturers. They would receive mass-produced products made for various clients and try to fit them into their cars. It’s similar to buying ready-to-wear clothing. But when you design the cells yourself, the story changes. It’s like tailoring a suit that fits your car perfectly. Performance, cost, and safety can be customized for each vehicle model. In fact, Hyundai is categorizing its batteries by use case. They use high-output cells they developed for high-performance EVs, and mid-nickel NCM batteries with lower costs for mass-market EVs. This is a strategy of operating multiple battery systems simultaneously, including LFP batteries . According to international reports, the mid-nickel NCM batteries for mass-market EVs can reduce costs by about 30%. Hyundai hasn't stopped there ; they have also started actively hiring talent for battery development and manufacturing. They are transforming from a company that buys batteries to a company that understands and designs them. Having already built its own hybrid systems and the EV-dedicated E-GMP platform, Hyundai has now reached the innermost part of the battery: cell design. But it’s not just the batteries that have changed. This car features a motor that performs two tasks at the same time. Looking at the EREV structure Hyundai unveiled at the 2025 Investor Day, it uses two motors. First, there is a drive motor on the rear axle that focuses solely on turning the wheels. What’s interesting is the front part. The front motor acts as both a motor and a generator. It can charge the battery using power from the engine, or turn the front wheels directly when needed. To put it simply, it’s like this. Imagine a small shop with one employee; when customers flock in, they work the register, and when it’s slow , they go to the warehouse to restock. Since one person switches between two roles depending on the situation, there’s no need to hire extra staff. When there are fewer parts, the weight drops, and when the weight drops, the car goes that much farther. And having one motor in both the front and rear means it can drive all four wheels. In the U.S., where there are many snowy roads and unpaved paths, this is a major advantage. The industry expects this dual-motor system will also be used in the Kia Telluride. In a 2025 announcement, Vice President Kim Chang-won explained the system this way . Unlike existing EREV systems, Hyundai's approach is based on 15 years of experience in hybrid and EV development. He added that they would lower costs without sacrificing EV performance through the optimal balance of battery and motor. The key word here is "balance." It’s not enough for just the battery to be good, or just the motor. A small battery, a powerful dual-role motor, and an engine that only generates electricity. These four elements must mesh like gears to finally achieve that 96km range. So, what would this car look like on the actual road? An interesting trace was found on a test vehicle spotted in the U.S. Remember how I told you earlier to keep the side of this car in mind? This is exactly what I was talking about. The internal codename for the test vehicle being driven in the U.S. is MXO , and the 'A' at the end means it's for the North American market. But a unique feature was discovered on this car. It had two doors. A fuel filler door for gas, and a separate charging port. A single car with two intake points for energy. These two small doors reveal the identity of this car at a glance. If you have a charger near your home, you can plug it in like an EV, and when going on a long trip, you can just stop by a gas station. There is no need to go searching for charging stations. Think of a family in the U.S. On weekdays, they charge it overnight in their home garage, using not a single drop of gas while commuting to school, work, and the grocery store. Then, on the weekend, they head out on a trip to a national park hundreds of kilometers away. It doesn’t matter if there are no charging stations along the way. Because gas stations are everywhere. They get to enjoy the quietness of an EV and the freedom of a gasoline car all in one vehicle. Hyundai President José Muñoz said this. That the Santa Fe EREV prototype is already on the production line at the Alabama plant. He also said that for customers who don't want to change their refueling habits but want the EV experience, there is now an answer. And he emphasized that the answer is "Made in USA." The price has not yet been officially announced. However, local U.S. media outlets expect the entire lineup of the refreshed new Santa Fe to be priced between approximately $ 35,000 and $ 55,000. If the EREV is released within this range, it could be a much more realistic option than a pure electric SUV equipped with an expensive, large battery. Reducing the battery size by half ultimately puts more money back into the consumer's pocket. But there’s a backstory to that 966km figure that many people aren’t aware of. Hyundai first introduced its EREV strategy back in 2024. The target range presented at that time was over 900km. And now, two years later, this August, the target for the Santa Fe EREV has been refined to 600 miles, or about 966km. Executive Vice President Kim Chang-won once said this while introducing the car. He noted that a range exceeding 600 miles is the true hallmark that justifies the "Extended Range" name. It means that since they included the word in the name, they intend to prove it with actual distance. In the automotive industry, the numbers initially announced often quietly shrink during the development process. That’s because they run into the wall of reality. But Hyundai did the opposite and increased the numbers. This is a detail that can be interpreted as growing confidence as development progressed. That confidence is also evident in other remarks made by President Moon. He said this at the same event. I believe there is no more chasm. The speed of adoption just differs by market. "Chasm" refers to the period just before a new technology becomes mainstream when demand suddenly drops off. Over the past few years, global automakers have cut investments and delayed plans in the face of the EV chasm. However, Hyundai endured that period with hybrids and has now brought out its next card: the EREV. President Moon explained this figure in another way as well. He stated that the Santa Fe EREV's range is about 1.6 times that of the gasoline Santa Fe. It means that for the same car with the same name, the distance you can travel on a single charge/tank is 1.6 times greater. If we translate this into our daily lives, it looks like this. If a gasoline Santa Fe can travel from Seoul to Busan with a little left over on one tank, the EREV can make it to Busan, drive back to Seoul, and still have range to spare. Plus, for short daily commutes, you can run on battery power alone without using a single drop of fuel. What is the most common reason people hesitate to buy an electric vehicle? It is exactly that: range anxiety. It's the anxiety of being in the middle of a highway with 10%battery left, not knowing how far the next charging station is. The 966km figure is not just a record; it’s a declaration that they intend to erase that anxiety entirely. But here, a question arises. Why is Hyundai launching this car in the U.S. first, rather than in Korea? The answer, unexpectedly, lies in China . According to the China Passenger Car Association, 1.2 million EREVs were sold in China in 2024. That figure is a 79%increase from a year ago. If you break it down by day, that means over 3,000 new EREVs hit the roads every single day. A massive market has already been proven. And Hyundai Motor plans to launch a model that doubles as an EREV in the Chinese market next year in line with this trend. What about the United States, then? The U.S. is a vast country. It is common to drive hundreds of kilometers one way, and there is a significant gap in charging infrastructure depending on the region. On top of this, there is the added uncertainty of electric vehicle policies. In a report this March, KOTRA analyzed that this uncertainty has led the U.S. automotive industry to focus on EREV strategies. Wait, then why choose EREV instead of a pure electric vehicle? The answer is cost. Since EREVs use smaller and cheaper batteries than pure EVs, production costs go down , and the structure is simpler than a plug-in hybrid. It is a structure where consumers worry less about charging and the company can lower production costs. China has already proven it. And now, the door is just starting to open in the U.S. Hyundai Motor is standing right in front of that door. CEO Jaehoon Chang declared that Hyundai will become a pioneer of EREVs in the U.S. And that car will be developed in Korea and manufactured in Alabama, USA. Behind this one car is a much bigger picture. Hyundai Motor has set a goal to launch over 100 new car models and achieve 5.55 million in global sales by 2030. Electrified vehicles will account for 60%of that total. They also announced plans to increase production capacity in North America by 500,000 units and raise local parts procurement to over 80%. It is not 555,000 units, but 5.55 million units. They intend to sell as many cars annually as more than one-tenth of Korea's entire population to the world. The Santa Fe EREV is the vehicle at the forefront of that massive plan. But one concern remains here. If you make the battery that small, won't it wear out quickly since it will be charged and discharged more often? The most definitive answer to this question did not come from a laboratory. It came from one driver. Mr. Young-eum Lee drove an Ioniq 5 for work. And he left this post online. He drove 580,000 km in 2 years and 9 months without a single breakdown. Since the Earth's circumference is about 40,000 km, 580,000 km is about 14 and a half times around the Earth. It is a number you get only if you drive nearly 580 km every single day for 33 months. It is equivalent to driving one and a half times the distance from Seoul to Busan every day. This news even reached the ears of Hyundai Motor researchers. The team led by Senior Researcher Da-ryeong Yoon, who was studying EV components for long-distance driving, personally contacted Mr. Lee. An ordinary work vehicle became a subject of intensive inspection overnight. The research team was building a model to predict battery durability by comparing it with actual driving data. This car was one of the few living proofs in the world that could verify if those predictions were correct. What if you had driven this distance in a combustion engine vehicle ? Hyundai estimates that fuel costs alone would have amounted to about 80 million won. That’s like the price of a decent luxury sedan disappearing into fuel costs. And the results are in. The battery's remaining lifespan is 87.7%; even after 580,000 km, the battery held nearly 90%of its original capacity. It was the moment when real-world road data proved there is no need to worry significantly about battery degradation , even under extreme conditions. Think about the smartphone you use. After about two years, the battery starts draining noticeably faster, right? But this car was driven hundreds of kilometers every single day for two years and nine months, repeating charge and discharge cycles. And yet, the battery still held nearly 90%of its new capacity. This isn't the only evidence. In Germany, they tested how far three Kona Electrics could travel on a single charge. Reflecting European city traffic, they drove for three days at an average speed of around 30 km/h. The result was that all three vehicles surpassed 1,000 km. The car that went the furthest reached 1,026 km. Of course, since it was a record set at slow speeds, it’s not exactly the same as daily driving. However, it clearly shows how efficiently a single battery can be used. What’s even more surprising is the final scene. It traveled over 20 km even when the battery was at 3%. And even after the dashboard showed 0%, it rolled for several hundred more meters before finally stopping. In Madrid, Spain, a Kona Electric also drove 790 km in real city traffic over 15 hours and 17 minutes. At that time, the electricity consumed per 100 km was 8.2 kWh, far less than the official standard of 14.7 kWh. A car that doesn't stop immediately even at 0%. A battery that doesn't wear out even after 580,000 km. Hyundai’s battery technology wasn't just numbers in a presentation; it was already being proven on the road. And the experience gained this way is leading to a new car called the Inster. But Hyundai’s plans don’t end with the Santa Fe. A car that goes even further is being prepared. It is none other than Genesis. Genesis, which caught the attention of local media last August in San Francisco by unveiling its first large flagship SUV, the GV90, will release an EV SUV in early 2027. The target driving range is 640 miles, or about 1,030 km. They intend to break the 1,000 km barrier. And there is another "first" prepared for Genesis. It is the Battery Thermal Runaway Prevention system, or TRP. You probably remember the electric vehicle fire that occurred in the basement parking lot of an apartment complex in Incheon last summer. Since then, many people have started worrying about fires whenever they think of electric vehicles. The scariest thing about an EV battery is when heat starting from a single cell spreads to other cells. Just like when one domino falls, they all tumble down in a row. TRP is a technology that prevents the first domino from spreading to the next. Hyundai Motor has tested this system 200 times with prismatic and pouch-type NCM batteries, planning to first apply it to Genesis models. Prismatic cells look like rigid metal boxes, while pouch cells resemble thin plastic bags. Having passed repeated tests on two types of batteries with different shapes and properties means this technology isn't limited to specific ones. Battery life will also continue to increase. The goal is to extend battery life by an average of 20%by 2028 by advancing cloud-based Battery Management Systems, or BMS. It’s a method of analyzing driving and charging data in the cloud to manage the battery's condition. In other words , it’s like assigning a dedicated physician to every battery. Farther, safer, and longer. Hearing this so far, it sounds perfect. However, there are things we must calmly address here. First, 96 km is still just a target. It is merely a development goal set by Hyundai, not an official figure certified by the U.S. EPA. Moreover, key specifications like battery capacity, output, and fuel efficiency have not yet been disclosed. The talk that the engine acting as a generator will be a 2.5 L turbo is currently just a forecast. We will only know the real numbers once the actual mass-produced car is released. Second, this car still uses gasoline. You might be able to commute on electricity alone, but to drive 966 km, the engine must eventually turn on. You must be clearly aware that it is not a completely zero-emission vehicle for long distances. Third, there are also strategic risks. The 5 th-generation Santa Fe plug-in hybrid was not released in the U.S. Some view this as leaving that spot open for the EREV. This is not officially confirmed, but if so, the void could be exposed if the EREV struggles in the market. Fourth, competitors are not standing still. China's Xpeng is introducing a range-extended model for its SUVs in Europe, boasting a combined range of about 0 km. While it's true Hyundai is leading in the EREV market, they are not running alone. If they fail to widen the technological gap, the pioneer's advantage could disappear faster than expected. Nevertheless, there are certainties as well. The battery durability that withstood 580,000 km is real data. Cells with double the output have already been designed. And prototypes are currently on the production line at the Alabama plant in the U.S. The numbers are still goals, but the technological foundation supporting them is already a reality. Think back to when you first started this video. Hearing the number 966km, we imagined a massive battery. Bigger, more, heavier. Because that’s how the EV race has always been until now. Who puts in a bigger battery, who packs more cells into the floor. In that competition, cars have only become heavier and more expensive. But Hyundai found the answer on the other side. They cut the battery size in half and doubled the power of each cell instead. They gave the motor two jobs and tasked the engine with acting as a generator instead of driving the wheels. And whether that battery truly lasts was proven first by an Ioniq that drove 580,000 km. They found a way to go further with less, instead of stopping more often. The secret to going further wasn't more power, but the courage to strip things away. If you ever see a Santa Fe on the road with both a fuel cap and a charging port, think about the half-sized battery inside. Because that small battery holds the time spent by Korean engineers perfecting hybrids and EVs over the last 15 years. The time spent by those who kept thinking about how to go further with a smaller battery while others jumped into the race for bigger ones. But another question remains here. Lately, Chinese electric vehicles advertise ranges of over 1,000 km on a single charge. Is that number actually the same on real roads? There is a hidden difference in certification methods that we aren't familiar with. Depending on the country's standard, the driving range for the same car can vary by hundreds of kilometers.

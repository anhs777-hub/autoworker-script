What’s the first thing that comes to your mind when you hear about local festivals on the news? Overpriced food like a bowl of fish cake soup, noisy performances, or predictable night markets? Honestly, that’s probably what everyone thinks of first. I do, too. But the festivals actually facing extinction as millions of visitors vanish weren't failing because of price gouging. A seaside event that once drew 2 million people is down to 250,000, another doubled its duration but lost half its crowd, and the most famous winter festival in Korea couldn't even open its doors for three consecutive years. These are the very representative festivals you’ve likely visited at least once. With the number of festivals growing every year, why have people stopped visiting the famous ones we all remember? Today, we’ll count down from 5th to 1st place based on the largest drop in visitors, and look at the key differences in three festivals that saw record-breaking crowds instead. Once you finish watching, your perspective on choosing where to go next weekend will be completely different. Let’s start with 5th place. 5th place is the Ulsan Whale Festival. Have you ever boarded a boat in Ulsan’s Jangsaengpo to go whale watching? As soon as the boat leaves the port, the children are already hanging onto the railings. Every time the waves crash white in the distance, tiny fingers point out first. "Look, what’s over there?" The moment a child shouts, the heads of all the adults on board turn in that direction at once. A moment where the whole family holds their breath in the middle of the sea. They came all this way just to see a whale. In fact, Jangsaengpo wasn’t a village for watching whales, but for catching them. A sailor from those days remembers that time like this. When they returned from sea, vendors selling whale meat were lined up everywhere. But when whaling was banned, the people who filled the village left one by one. What the emptying town of Jangsaengpo clung to as a last resort was the memory of whales. The village launched a whale festival. They even built an old neighborhood on one side that perfectly recreates the Jangsaengpo of that era. The captain’s house, the gunner’s house, the whale processing site, the tearoom, the barbershop, and even the inn. A village that once lived by hunting whales set out to survive by showing them instead. On festival days, whale parades would pass through the streets, and whale-watching boats would head out fully loaded with passengers. In its heyday, 670,000 people. That many people flocked to a small port village. But there is one guest for this festival that you can’t schedule , no matter how carefully you prepare. It’s the main attraction: the whale. Just a few years ago, for every five whale-watching trips, you would encounter a whale once. Then, how was it last year? You had to go out about 24 times to see one just once. Simply put, you could ride the boat every single weekend for a whole month and still finish without seeing a single whale fin. You can’t blame anyone for this. They say that when the water is cold, the whales’ food sources— anchovies and squid—don't appear, and consequently, the whales rarely show up . The wait was even longer this year. The first boat heads out to sea at the end of March. Spring passed, and almost all of early summer went by, yet the sea remained empty. It wasn't until late July that a pod of dolphins finally revealed themselves. They say a welcoming shout of joy erupted on the boat that day. Given the boat had been roaming the empty seas near the shore, that's only natural. With no whales coming, the number of visitors dropped as well. The festival, which used to draw 670,000 people, saw only 320,000 last year—more than half the crowd had vanished. The port area, once bustling with whale meat vendors, was essentially said to be closed for business years ago. It reached the point where a restaurant owner who had sold whale meat for 20 years said they would soon close their doors. That doesn't mean Jangsaengpo is completely empty, though. Something strange happened. The Hydrangea Festival held in the same Jangsaengpo actually saw an increase in visitors by over 30%last year. So, the path to Jangsaengpo hadn't been cut off after all. It was only the footsteps of those coming to see the whales that had decreased. The difference is simple. Hydrangeas bloom without fail when the season arrives. But no one knows if the whales will come, even when their season arrives. Think back to that child on the whale-watching boat. Excited by the promise of seeing a whale, the child hangs onto the railing for a long time, only to step off having seen nothing but empty sea. They might promise to see them next time while in the car on the way home. But whether that promise will lead them back to Jangsaengpo the following year is uncertain. In the days when we hunted whales, you were guaranteed to see them if you launched a boat, but now, days without whales are far more common even though we advertise seeing them. No matter how much care is put into preparation, it means the entire fate of the festival has been left to a natural environment that cannot be controlled. Even so, Ulsan still launches boats and holds festivals. The 4th place is the exact opposite. People left even though it was held longer and on a larger scale. 4th place is the Boryeong Mud Festival. In the middle of summer on the sandy beach of Daecheon, people covered head-to-toe in gray mud slide down slides and slip into the mud. Screams and laughter erupt from all around. Mixed in between were foreign tourists also covered in mud. Getting up close to a stranger, everyone clicks a photo with mud-covered faces. If you visited with your child, you probably still have one such photo somewhere in your phone's gallery. The beginning of this festival was truly humble. All it took was for an official to hold a brush and dab some mud on tourists once. The festival that started with just that brush later drew major international news agencies and broadcasters to cover it. In its heyday, Psy performed on the opening stage, and nearly 4 million people visited Daecheon over 10 days. There was even talk of growing this mud festival into one of the world's top four festivals. After that, Boryeong extended the festival period. From 10 days to 17 days. It’s easy to expect that if you extend the festival period, visitors will increase accordingly. But what was the result? This summer, 1.62 million people visited over the 17 days . They nearly doubled the duration, but the actual number of people is not even half of what it was in its prime. The beach became quieter by the day, and with the rainy season and typhoons, thousands who had reservations turned back. So, where did all those people go ? The answer lies at the Daecheon Beach right in front of the festival grounds. During the summer when the Mud Festival was most crowded, over 12 million people visited Daecheon Beach. It was the first summer since it opened that the visitor count exceeded 10 million. But this summer, fewer than 5 million people visited the sea. The Mud Festival was, from the start, a festival that people vacationing at Daecheon Beach would drop by to enjoy. As the number of visitors to the beach dropped, the number of people diving into the mud dropped as well. Boryeong City also cited the record-breaking heat wave and fewer beachgoers as the reasons for this year's decline. The empty space was most noticeable among foreigners. Compared to its peak, four out of every five foreign guests who used to roll around in the mud together have disappeared. It’s not that the foreigners who cherish this festival have vanished. Isaac, from Ghana, who visited for his third year this summer, said it feels like every foreigner living in Korea comes here. However, that bustling scene where people would cover themselves in mud side-by-side with strangers just isn't what it used to be. This summer, 350 tons of mud were still laid out on the Daecheon sand beach. There were mud slides, mud pools, and even tubs lined up to sit in after applying the mud. A space for pet dogs to romp around in the mud was also created. At night, DJ performances and drone shows filled the sky. There was plenty of mud and no shortage of things to see. There was just one thing missing: the people to jump into that mud. If there's not enough mud, you can just scoop more and lay it down. But for the third-place festival, the main attraction cannot be laid down by human hands. It is ice on a river that only forms when the sky freezes it. Third place is the Pyeongchang Trout Festival . In the middle of winter in Pyeongchang, people in short sleeves and shorts line up by the frozen Odae Stream. Just looking at them makes your teeth chatter. When the signal is given , they splash into the ice-cold water and chase after trout with their bare hands. Even the spectators shiver, but those who jump in come out beaming, clutching the slippery trout. When you think of the Pyeongchang Trout Festival , this scene is likely the first that comes to mind. But did you know this ice festival was born out of a flood disaster? When flood damage devastated the local economy, residents rolled up their sleeves and held the first festival the following year. They literally rebuilt a town destroyed by water with a festival of ice. People setting up tents on the ice to wait for trout. People sitting in indoor fishing spots, holding their children's hands. And even people diving into the freezing water. At its peak, 700,000 people flocked to this river. The problem is that this festival is struggling with winters that lack ice. One winter, the opening was delayed twice. Even after finally opening, the river ice wasn't thick enough to hold the crowds. So, where did the guests who came for ice fishing cast their lines? It was an indoor fishing spot. Families who had packed thick coats to squat on the ice ended up casting their lines under a roof. That year, a newspaper wrote that winter festivals without ice are like a steamed bun without red bean filling. Since then, winters that force delays have returned every time we seem to forget about them . It was the same this year. The festival, originally set to start on New Year's Day, opened on the 9th, and the duration was shortened accordingly. The number of visitors has also dropped significantly. From 700,000 visitors, this year saw only 250,000. It means that for every three holes drilled in the ice, nearly two remain empty without a fisher. Then, when did this festival start to falter? It was the very winter after 700,000 people had gathered. That winter was exceptionally warm. Even in mid-December, the Odae Stream ice was only half the thickness needed for people to step on it. The opening was pushed back by a week, and only 200,000 people visited that winter . More than two out of every seven people stopped coming. After that, the festival had to skip two entire winters due to COVID-19. And even after reopening, the number of visitors has struggled to climb out of the 200,000 range. Of course, you can't blame everything on the ice when it comes to the drop in visitors. However, every winter when the opening is delayed, families who eagerly awaited the festival must have had to reschedule their plans multiple times. Changing their lodging dates and erasing and redrawing circles on their children’s winter break calendars. Overcoming flood damage, this festival created by the locals has reached its 20th winter this year. The festival committee chair called this festival a form of winter tourism that has grown along with the region. However, they set the table for its 20th birthday not on New Year's Day , but more than a week later. The festival, which started after overcoming a water crisis, now just waits for the river to freeze properly. When the ice was late, Pyeongchang waited. But the second-place festival did not wait. To avoid the weather. They changed the season entirely. Second place is the Pohang International Fireworks Festival. The beach area around Yeongildae in midsummer. Families are sitting on the sand that was heated up during the day. A child sits on their dad's lap, and a cool breeze blows in from the sea. Everyone is just looking at the sky, right? And the moment the first fireworks soar over the sea, countless heads filling the beach turn to the sky at once. Boom! The bursting sound resonates in your chest, and light spreads once more over the dark sea. In the summer of 2017, 2.03 million people flocked to the festival over five days. It was the largest crowd since the festival began. During those few days, sales at shops along Yeongildae beach jumped by at least half, and at most, more than doubled. On nights when fireworks exploded, the whole neighborhood by the beach would be buzzing with excitement. But that very midsummer heat became a hurdle. It was just too hot. As people started saying they were reluctant to visit the festival due to unprecedented heatwaves , Pohang made a decision. They moved the festival, which used to be held in late July, to the last week of May. They cut the five-day duration down to three days and moved the stage away from the beach to the banks of the Hyeongsan River for a while. Considering the sweltering heat, it was a decision that made perfect sense. What was the result? In the first year after moving it, 1.51 million people gathered over three days. When calculated per day, it was actually more than during midsummer. Moving the season was not the wrong choice. But the very next year, COVID-19 struck. The festival stopped, and for several years after that, it couldn't be held properly. And then, spring returned to its original form. The number of visitors over three days was 250,000. Even when broken down by day, it was as if only one person was looking up at the sky where five people used to look during midsummer. And last June, a scene emerged that would not be easily forgotten. At 9 PM that night, Italian and Canadian teams were scheduled to launch fireworks, and drones were set to line up and draw pictures in the night sky. But at 4:30 PM, a heavy rain advisory was issued. And soon, a text message flew onto people's phones. It was a message saying the festival was completely canceled and to head home safely while being mindful of accidents . Among those who received the text, there were people who had traveled for hours. Some had flown in from Jeju, and others had even taken time off from work. "Shouldn't you have sent it at least by morning?" Complaints poured in . The city had its side of the story as well. They claimed that the lives and safety of the citizens came first. They couldn't just leave that many people by the riverside with a heavy rain advisory in effect. However, for those already on their way, the text message arrived far too late. I don't think what Pohang lost was just the fireworks . Fireworks go off the same way on a spring night, too. Was it really just for a few fireworks that people flocked to Yeongildae every midsummer? Sitting on the warm sand during vacation, feeling the sea breeze, and waiting for that first firework—that whole time was part of the festival. The first year they moved the season, people still came to the spring night festival they had attended every year. But after a few years of pause, the steady stream of visitors was broken. When the fireworks went off again, the fireworks were the same, but the summer night was already gone. As the unique sense of season and emotion etched into people's memories vanished, the festival lost its power. When the rain stops, the Yeongildae sea and the Hyeongsan River banks are right back in their place. But the festival is different. The very place meant for the festival disappeared underwater. And that has happened for three years in a row. This story is about the Inje Smelt Festival. In midwinter, in Inje, Gangwon-do, people are scattered across the frozen, icy surface of the upper Soyang Lake. Each person sits in front of a small hole drilled into the ice, holding a short fishing rod. When they gently wiggle it and pull it up, several transparent, finger-sized smelt come dangling up. Dip one into gochujang right there and pop it in your mouth, and the taste of the cold lake fills it . The Inje Smelt Festival was known as the original winter festival. Smelt are fish that only live in very clear water . So, the festival's name was also a boast that Soyang Lake was that clean. Every year, 200,000 people gathered on this ice to drill holes. But these days , a sheet of ice doesn't form on Soyang Lake in winter. The festival hasn't been held even once in three years. Isn't it strange? Winter hasn't disappeared, so why can't they hold the festival on this lake? The answer lies in the Soyang Dam, which blocks the lower part of the lake. When it rains heavily in autumn, the dam fills up and the lake level rises. Then the festival grounds are submerged, and in the deepened lake, the ice doesn't freeze thick enough for people to stand on. That winter when the festival was canceled, the lake water was a story and a half of an apartment building higher than the level needed to hold the event. The dam authorities had their own situation, too. They had to keep enough water in reserve to prepare for floods and droughts. That winter, the county governor bowed his head, saying he was deeply sorry and regretful that they could not hold the festival. But the following winter, the water rose even higher, and the ice didn't form at all. And this January, the water, swollen from a late autumn rainy season, covered a significant portion of the festival grounds. That is how three winters passed. The foundation in charge of the festival said it would prepare a festival that remains unshaken by climate change. The festival, which was born on ice, is now in a position where it must first find a way to survive without it. Inje shifted its focus toward year-round tourism. They decided to hold a summer festival instead of a winter one. But at the end of June, even that summer festival was canceled. It was water again this time. The festival grounds were submerged, and algae had even bloomed in the lake. The lake, home to icefish that only live in clear water, had turned murky green. The same water that blocked the ice festival had now swallowed the summer festival as well. In Ulsan and Pyeongchang, even when the number of people dwindled, they still held their events. Even when whales didn't appear, the boats set sail, and even when the ice was late, the trout fishing site eventually opened. But Inje surrendered the very space where they were to hold their event to the water. People who used to sit huddled by ice holes waiting for fish have had nowhere to go for three years, and the town waiting for those visitors has spent three years without a winter festival. Only after the festival disappeared did we finally see it. How precariously the local economy was leaning on this single sheet of ice. If so, were the festivals that couldn't avoid blaming the weather and the environment really helpless? There are festivals that didn't just look to the sky but found answers in completely different ways, resulting in huge success. Let's look at three festivals that saw crowds flocking to them during the same period. The first is the Hwacheon Sancheoneo Ice Festival. It is a Gangwon-do ice festival just like the ones in Inje and Pyeongchang. They worry about the weather just the same. Last January, the weather was mild, so they pushed back the opening by a week. Up to this point, it's no different from Pyeongchang. But how many people came to Hwacheon that year? 1.87 million. That was the largest number of people ever. In the face of the same crisis, what did Hwacheon do differently? There were two things. First, they didn't leave the ice entirely up to the heavens. They precisely controlled the water flow and volume upstream, built barriers downstream, and even mobilized large pumps to drain water in preparation for winter rain to protect the ice. Before blaming the environment, they did all the preparations that humans could possibly do. Second, they connected the visitors who had been confined within the festival grounds to the entire town . They allowed people to enjoy night ice fishing for free if they presented a lodging receipt from their stay in Hwacheon. As a result, three out of five night-fishing visitors stayed overnight in Hwacheon. A portion of the fishing experience fee was returned as Hwacheon agricultural vouchers. They made visitors fish on the ice during the day, stay at local lodgings at night, and take home local produce on their way back. The second example is the Chuncheon Makguksu and Dakgalbi Festival. In fact, until just two years ago, this festival was notorious for overcharging. There was an outcry when a 500g whole roasted pig cost 40,000 won. Then, last year, Chuncheon completely overhauled the festival framework. They completely excluded outside night markets and vendors, and had local Chuncheon restaurant owners come out to sell the food themselves. They also set fixed prices for Dakgalbi and Makguksu. On top of that, they provided discount coupons that could also be used at downtown restaurants. They made sure the taste experienced at the festival carried over to the restaurants in the downtown alleys. What was the result? The number of people who visited the festival last fall more than doubled compared to a few years ago. The third is the Gimbap Festival in Gimcheon, Gyeongbuk. Let me ask you one thing. What comes to mind first when you hear "Gimcheon"? When Gimcheon City asked young people this, most of them thought of the snack bar chain, "Gimbap Heaven." And that's despite the fact that not a single sheet of seaweed (gim) is produced in Gimcheon. It’s a funny yet potentially bittersweet story, but the official in charge thought about it differently. The idea was: if we can't beat the brand awareness of Gimbap Heaven, let's become Gimbap itself. That’s how the first Gimbap Festival was held the fall before last. The gimbap they prepared, worrying they might have made too much, sold out by around 2 p.m. They even gained the humorous nickname, "Gimbap Heaven without Gimbap." The following year, they expanded the venue fivefold. Even so, more people than the entire population of Gimcheon flocked there over two days, leading to sell-outs from the first morning. They completely skipped tedious opening ceremonies and introductions, and strictly curbed price gouging. In October that year, tourists visiting Gimcheon increased by nearly 30%from the previous year. A single clever shift in perspective made people intentionally visit a small city that had no festival to brag about. Hwacheon, Chuncheon, Gimcheon. The starting points were different, but the secret to success was the same. Controlling price gouging was fundamental, and they didn't just rely on the festival site alone. Hwacheon connected fishing guests to lodgings and farms, Chuncheon connected festival-goers to downtown restaurant alleys, and Gimcheon connected visitors to the entire city. They ensured that people didn't just drive away like an ebbing tide as soon as the festival ended, but that the warmth and spending seeped into every corner of the neighborhood alleys. Shall we recall the current scenes of these festivals one by one? In Jangsaengpo, whale boats return after circling the empty sea. The mud is spread thick across Daecheon Beach, but there aren't enough people to jump in. Pyeongchang had to delay its 20th winter festival by over a week , and in Pohang, visitors who drove for hours had to turn back after receiving a single cancellation text. And for three years now, the ice has failed to form on Soyang Lake in Inje. Meanwhile, the number of local festivals across the country has actually increased. Now , on average, more than three festivals are held somewhere in the country every single day. The number of people visiting these festivals is also higher than ever. But it’s strange, isn't it ? The amount of money each person actually spends at these festivals has conversely decreased. Festivals are overflowing in number, but what remains in the local area after they pass is getting lighter and lighter. If we compare five struggling locations with three successful ones, it becomes clear what gives a festival its vitality. Whether it's whales, mud, ice, or fireworks, they can draw a crowd at least once. However, the power to keep people coming back, even when unexpected weather or crises strike, ultimately lies outside the festival grounds. It's the memories of a warm room where you had a comfortable night's stay, a restaurant where you enjoyed a meal at an honest price, or the local specialties you brought home in full hands. What determined the success or failure of a festival wasn't just the weather or a price-gouged meal , but what remained in the neighborhood after the festival ended. Winter is almost here. If Soyang Lake provides the venue and freezes solid this winter , people might be able to squat in front of ice holes again for the first time in four years. The people of Inje are likely still watching the lake's water with an earnest heart. What do you think? Seasonal festivals where the weather doesn't cooperate due to climate change.

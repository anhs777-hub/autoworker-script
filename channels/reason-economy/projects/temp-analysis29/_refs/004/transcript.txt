I thought the claim that the cost of a 4 million won luxury bag was 80,000 won was an exaggeration. However, this figure came from court documents. In June 2024, the Milan court in Italy placed Dior's bag production subsidiary under court supervision. The subcontracting factory received 53 euros for making a single bag, which was approximately 80,000 won at the exchange rate at the time. The retail price was 2,600 euros, or about 3.8 million won. This means they received nearly 50 times the production cost. Meanwhile, the price of a Chanel Classic bag rose from 7.15 million won to 18.8 million won. It jumped 2.6 times in less than seven years. A symbol of wealth that people used to line up for from dawn has now become a laughingstock whenever the topic of production costs comes up. Gucci's sales have been cut in half in just three years. Everyone blames the economic downturn. They say it is because wealthy Chinese people have closed their wallets. That is correct. The economy is bad, and the market in China has cooled down. However, that is only half the truth. There used to be a formula that the higher the price, the better it sells. That formula is now strangling luxury goods. Today, I will thoroughly investigate the true nature of the grave that luxury brands have dug for themselves. Let me warn you in advance: this is by no means a story limited only to those who buy luxury goods. Before we begin, please subscribe and like. Let's recall the scene at a department store just a few years ago. In the early dawn, before the sun has even risen, people line up in long queues in front of the main entrance. They unfold folding chairs, cover themselves with blankets, and wait only for the doors to open. The moment the doors open, people start running toward the store. This is known as the "opening." The only item they rushed in to buy was a single bag. However, a strange profession emerged here: people who stand in line on behalf of others and receive money for it. In early 2023, a "One-Line Stone Age" part-timer stood in line from 6:30 AM to 12:30 PM. The money received for standing for six hours was 90,000 won. In monetary terms, that means 15,000 won. If they actually bought a watch or a bag, they received an additional 100,000 to 500,000 won. There were about 74 job postings for " line-standing part-time work" uploaded over a ten-day period. However, the truly surprising figure lies elsewhere. The highest-earning employee at a line-standing agency made 4.54 million won a month, even after withholding taxes were deducted. It is said that they only took two or three days off a month, doing nothing but standing in line. Both the people buying the bags and the people standing in line on behalf of others were obsessed with these bags. So, which country was this obsessed with luxury goods? It was neither France nor the United States. It was South Korea. Global investment bank Morgan Stanley estimated Korean luxury spending in 2022 at $ 16.8 billion. This is a 24%increase from the previous year. In Korean currency, that amounts to 20.9 trillion won. Divided per citizen, it is approximately $ 325, or 404,000 won. Americans spent $ 280, while Chinese people spent $ 55. This means that one Korean spent nearly six times more money on luxury goods than one Chinese person. South Korea ranked first in the world for per capita luxury consumption. It was, in effect, a "Republic of Luxury," the country that loves luxury goods the most in the world. However, there is one point that needs to be addressed here. It is not that the people of this country have always liked bags. This is because luxury goods were not originally sold as mere bags. Luxury goods were originally simply high-quality items. They were objects crafted with great care by skilled artisans who selected the finest materials. Many names originated from workshops in Europe that made horse harnesses and travel bags for the aristocracy. That is why they were expensive, and that is why only a select few wealthy people carried them. However, at some point, luxury goods began to transcend being mere objects. If you carry a bag, those around you recognize it without you having to say a word. It signifies, " That person is well-off." In this way, luxury goods ceased to be mere objects and became an identity card. And luxury companies have very cleverly cultivated this identity card business. Their first weapon was scarcity. They intentionally produce small quantities and intentionally stock small amounts. Popular products do not exist right now . You never know when they will be back in stock. The store refusing to sell the goods stubbornly insists they won't sell them. Isn't that strange? However, the more you refuse to haggle, the more people want to own them. The second weapon was price. Usually, raising the price of an item reduces sales. However , luxury goods sold better as their prices increased. In economics, this phenomenon—where demand actually rises despite the price increase due to the desire to show off—is called the Veblen effect. A psychology professor at a university explained that the luxury craze in Korea reflects, in part , this effect where people perceive items as more special the more expensive they are. Social media added fuel to the fire. Unboxing videos of newly purchased bags and shopping videos touring luxury stores filled screens. A feeling spread that if everyone else was closing the store, one would be left behind if they weren't. Luxury goods, once the possessions of CEOs and their wives, began to become the items of entry-level professionals and university students. Luxury companies raised a toast to this boom. Up to this point, it is a formula for success. However, one premise was hidden within this formula. The premise is that people will stick with the price even if it is raised. Luxury companies decided to test that premise—and quite boldly at that. Let’s trace the price tag of Chanel’s flagship bag, the Classic Flat Bag Medium. In November 2019, before the arrival of COVID-19, the bag cost 7.15 million won . Around that time, the average monthly wage for a worker in Korea was 3.49 million won. This meant that one could buy this single bag by saving their salary for two full months. Then, COVID -19 hit. Overseas travel was blocked, and places to spend money disappeared. The trapped money flocked to luxury goods in department stores. This is known as "revenge spending." Luxury companies did not miss this opportunity . Chanel raised its price ten times over two years. By the summer of 2022, the bag that was 7.15 million won had become 122.39 million won. This means the price jumped by 73%in less than three years. Yet, people did not get angry. Whenever news of a price hike circulated, people flocked to buy before the price went up. The phrase " Today is the cheapest day" became a buzzword. Raising prices effectively became a form of advertising. This was not unique to Chanel. In January 2025, the price of the Hermès Buckingham Bag in size 30 (Togo leather) rose from 18.31 million won to 2.11 million won. A single bag had entered the 20 million won range. In the same month, Chanel also raised the price of its calfskin bags: the mini size from 8.24 million won to 8.53 million won, and the large size from 9.83 million won to 1.17 million won. Changing price tags in the very first month of the new year had effectively become an annual event. And this year, in July 2026, Chanel raised prices once again. It was the fifth price hike in a long time. The Classic Flat Bag Medium rose from 17.9 million won to 18.8 million won. A single price hike added 900,000 won. The Boy Chanel Flat Bag also rose by 620,000 won, going from 11.73 million won to 12.35 million won. This followed a price hike of approximately 7%on some bags in January of this year. Compared to 2019, prices have increased by 162.9%. Meanwhile, the average monthly wage for workers rose from 3.49 million won to 4.09 million won, an increase of only 7.2%. This means that the price of bags has grown about 9.5 times faster than a monthly salary. A bag that cost two months 'salary in 2019 now costs four and a half months' salary. Your feeling that luxury goods have become more expensive was not an illusion. This is where the numbers start to go awry. Salaries are standing still, while bags are flying away. So, what happened to the people who used to buy bags with their salaries? The answer to this question runs through the entire story today. However, before looking at the answer, let me first establish the explanation that many people are thinking of. First, let's look at the report cards of luxury companies. There is LVMH, the world's largest luxury group. It is the king of the luxury industry, owning dozens of luxury brands such as Louis Vuitton and Dior. The heart of this group is its fashion and leather goods business. Revenue in this sector decreased from 42.169 billion euros in 2023 to 37.77 billion euros in 2025, a drop of 4%. During the same period, operating profit fell by 5 %from 21.5%. This means that profits collapsed more than twice as fast as revenue. The stock price is even more disastrous. Since the beginning of this year, LVMH's stock price has fallen by over 35%. And in September of this year , a symbolic event occurred: it lost the top spot in market capitalization on the French stock exchange to the cosmetics company L'Oréal. L'Oréal's market capitalization is approximately 23 billion euros, while LVMH's is 201 billion euros. This marks the first time since 2017 that a company that is not a luxury brand has ranked first in market capitalization based on closing prices. The situation is even more serious for Gucci. The 2025 revenue of the Karin Group, which owns Gucci, was 14.675 billion euros, a 13%decrease from the previous year. Operating profit vanished by 33%. Looking at the Gucci brand alone, 2025 revenue was 5.992 billion euros, a 22%drop. Revenue , which exceeded 10 billion euros in 2022, has fallen to around 6 billion euros in just three years. In the fourth quarter of 2025, Gucci revenue also fell by 10%to 1.622 billion euros. Every time talk of hitting rock bottom emerged, there was a bottom below that. The company was in a hurry. In September 2025, Goodsee welcomed its fourth CEO in just two years. The previous CEO had left the company less than a year after taking office. This implies that despite changing the captain four times, the ship continued to sink. On top of this, anxiety mounted as the parent company continued to acquire high-priced companies, causing the debt of Karin and its holding company to grow. Chanel was no exception. Chanel's global revenue in 2024 was $ 18.7 billion, a 4.3%decrease from the previous year. Operating profit fell by 30%. In particular, sales in Asia dropped by 7.1%to $ 9.2 billion. Cracks had appeared starting from the continent where luxury goods sold best. Korea was no exception either. In July 2024, sales of famous overseas brands at department stores decreased by 11%compared to a year earlier. While the market for general goods and home appliances expanded, only the luxury sector took a step backward. The same applies to the market as a whole. According to the consulting firm Bain&Company, the global luxury market peaked at 369 billion euros in 2023 and subsequently declined to 358 billion euros in 2025. So, who brought down this market? The first name that comes to mind is China. For the past decade or so, the major players sustaining the global luxury market were the wealthy Chinese. However, the Chinese real estate market collapsed, and domestic demand froze. According to Bain&Company data, sales in the Chinese luxury market plummeted by 20%in 2024. This performance fell significantly short of even the levels seen between 2021 and 2023, when cities were under lockdown due to COVID-19. It amounts to one of the pillars supporting the luxury empire shaking completely. On top of this, high inflation and high interest rates compounded the situation. When prices rise and loan interest rates balloon, the first thing people cut back on is luxury goods. This is because a handbag is not necessary for immediate survival . In fact, while LVMH was collapsing, one company unexpectedly benefited: the cosmetics company L'Oréal. Over time, L'Oréal's stock price rose by 5%, while LVMH's plummeted by over 35%. This was because mass consumers shifted from expensive bags and clothes to more affordable items like cosmetics and perfumes. This is the so-called " lipstick effect," which suggests that lipsticks sell well during a recession. Essentially, the recession forced people to choose lipstick over luxury bags. Consequently, it seems only natural that luxury goods are selling less. By this point, the conclusion converges on one thing: the economy has deteriorated, China has tightened its belt, and luxury goods are victims of the recession. I believed this up to this point, too. Indeed, many economic articles explain it this way, stating that luxury goods will make a comeback once the economy improves and China recovers. That is not wrong. However, according to that explanation, there is one thing that must inevitably happen. If the economy were the problem, all luxury brands should collapse together. This is because a recession does not discriminate against specific bags. So, I examined a country's report card. It is none other than our country, the world's number one republic of luxury goods. However, the numbers told the exact opposite. Chanel Korea's sales for 2025 were 2.013 trillion won, a 9% increase from the previous year. Operating profit rose by 25%to 336 billion won. This marks an all-time high, surpassing 2 trillion won in sales for the first time. Louis Vuitton Korea also recorded a record-breaking 1.8543 trillion won in sales last year. Its operating profit jumped by 1%to 525.6 billion won, up 35.1%. In the first half of this year, luxury sales at Lotte, Shinsegae, and Hyundai department stores increased by approximately 35%each. This means that while the global economy claims luxury goods are collapsing, they actually sold more in Korean department stores. LVMH also announced its first-quarter results, stating that sales in Korea grew despite sluggish performance in Europe and Japan. The situation is similar overseas. On April 15, 2025, Hermès's market capitalization reached 247 billion euros. On that day, LVMH fell to 245.4 billion euros. It was the day the "King of Luxury" ceded the throne to Hermès for the first time. That day, LVMH's stock price dropped by 7.5%at one point in the morning, while Hermès's stock price fell by only 0.4% . Investors were valuing LVMH shares at 50 times their expected earnings. This is a higher value than any other competitor. Brunello Cucinelli, famous for its ultra-national cashmere, saw its 2025 sales increase by 10.1%, surpassing 1.4 billion euros. Isn't something strange here? If the economic downturn were the cause, both Hermès and Chanel Korea should have collapsed together. Yet, while one hit an all-time high, the other saw its sales cut in half. All this happened under the same instability, in the same country, and within the same department store. There is one more bizarre statistic. There are "big spenders" whose annual luxury purchases exceed 20,000 euros. These are the people buying luxury goods worth over 30 million won. The proportion of total luxury spending accounted for by these big spenders rose from 30%in 2019 to approximately 47%in 2025. This means that while the market has shrunk, the share held by these big spenders has actually grown. If so, whose wallets were it that shrank? According to Bain& Company data, the number of luxury customers dropped from approximately 400 million in 2022 to 330 million in 2025. About 70 million people left the market in just three years. This implies that more people than the population of South Korea have turned their backs on luxury stores. And those who left were not the wealthy. They were the middle class and young consumers who used to save up their salaries to buy a single bag. This is where the core issue begins. A recession clearly hit the luxury market . However, the recession was not the cause; it was a symptom. The real illness lay within. Luxury brands dug their own graves. And they dug them using three shovels: the shovel of the price tag, the shovel of the cost price , and the insert of the now commonplace logo. Let's examine them one by one. The first shovel was the price tag. Earlier, I mentioned the story of how a Chanel bag went from costing two months 'salary to being a comfortable purchase . However, this was not just a story about Chanel alone. According to the global bank HSBC, luxury prices in Europe have risen by at least 52% compared to 2019. In effect, every luxury brand has swapped out its price tags as if by prior agreement. But what else rose along with the 52%increase in prices? The founder of a fashion platform stated it bluntly: "The quality hasn't improved by 52%. Prices have skyrocketed, but the quality remains the same." It wasn't that the leather had turned to gold, nor had the stitching method changed. Only the price tag had changed. At first, people endured it. They thought it was acceptable because it was a luxury item , a high-quality product. However, as this trend repeated every season, questions arose. Why on earth does this bag have to be this expensive? And the first to ask that question were not the wealthy. It was the people who could no longer afford the price tag. A partner at Bainland Company analyzed that while luxury prices rose by about 20%since 2021, approximately 50 million customers were pushed out of the market by 2024. There was a limit to the formula that sales increase as prices rise. The moment that line is crossed, the formula starts to turn in reverse. A bag that a salaried worker used to buy even if it meant stretching their budget becomes a bag they cannot afford no matter how much they stretch their budget. So where do those people go? They split into those who give up, those who turn to second-hand goods, and those who turn to similar fakes. However, luxury companies were not worried about this exodus. Instead, they filled the void left by those who left by selling to big spenders at higher prices. The fact that the share of big spenders increased from 30%to 47 %is the direct result of this. In the short term, it was a clever calculation . Chanel Korea achieving its highest-ever performance is also based on this calculation. However, this is where the real crisis begins. For a luxury item to serve as an identity card, there is one condition: there must be people who envy that identity card. Let's consider a monthly salary of 4.09 million won. An 11.11 million won Burberry bag is equivalent to five months of that salary. An 18.8 million won Chanel bag is roughly half a month's worth. One must save up for nearly half a year, just breathing, to get one bag in their hands. At this point, luxury goods cease to be a dream and become a story from another country . The 70 million people who could no longer afford bags have become mere spectators rather than enviers. And spectators soon become people who mock. What ignited that mockery was the second shovel. There is a phrase always cited as the reason for the high price of luxury goods: craftsmanship. It is a scene of an elderly European artisan selecting fine leather and sewing stitch by stitch. We paid millions of won for that scene. However, in 2024, an Italian court opened the door to what lay behind that scene. It started with Armani. In April 2024, the Milan court issued a court-ordered administration to Armani's bag manufacturing company. The investigation results were as follows: Bags made by subcontractors were transferred to an Armani supplier for 93 euros. The supplier then resold these bags to Armani for 250 euros. And in stores, they were sold for 1,800 euros. This means the price became more than 19 times higher than the cost when they left the subcontracting factory. Two months later, it was Dior. Four subcontractors employed undocumented Chinese nationals to run their factories 24 hours a day, seven days a week. The cost of the bags produced in this way was Size 53, and the retail price was 2,600 euros. The Milan court ordered Dior Italy's bag manufacturer to be under the supervision of a court-appointed administrator for one year. That summer, Italian competition authorities, along with financial police, raided the Italian facilities of both companies. Bags churned out in factories that operated without holidays commanded nearly 50 times their original price the moment they hit the shelves. However, it did not end there. In July 2025, it was Ropiana —the brand known for producing the world's most expensive cashmere. A cashmere jacket sold for 3,000 euros in stores was made for a second-tier subcontractor who received only 80 euros. Even based on the first-tier subcontractor's standards, the price was only 11,818 euros. This amounts to a price markup of over 37 times. The Milan court stated that Ropiana profited by intentionally failing to supervise the illegal activities of its subcontractors. It may be an accident once in a while. However, within a little over a year, scandals involving Armani, Dior, and Loro Piana erupted three times. This reveals the hidden truth. People were not outraged because the production costs were low. Everyone vaguely knew that there was a bubble in luxury goods. Their anger stemmed from the fact that the stories were false. What they believed to be artisan workshops turned out to be subcontracting factories operating 24 hours a day. People were not deceived by the bags themselves, but by the stories. Furthermore, those who felt cheated were even more enraged the higher the price they paid. However, the truly frightening aspect lies elsewhere. Once a story is shattered, it makes people believe even fake stories. In April 2025, while the tariff war between the U.S. and China was in full swing, strange videos began appearing on TikTok. Against the backdrop of a Chinese bag factory, a man claimed that while the factory cost of an Hermès bag was merely $ 1,395, the retail price was $ 38,000. They even added the claim that about 90%of the total cost is for the logo. Videos also circulated showing that $ 100 yoga pants could be purchased for $ 5 or $ 6 at the factory supplying Lululemon. Let me clarify this: these videos have not been verified as fact. Lululemon and Louis Vuitton have stated that they do not manufacture their products in China . Experts warned that it is highly likely to be a marketing tactic by manufacturers trying to sell counterfeit goods. Yet, people watched these videos and laughed. And they spread them widely. Why? Because court documents have already shown it. If it is true that the Giul bag is made in a No. 53 factory, then the video regarding the cost of the Buckingham bag also looks plausible. It seems people don't need to know the actual cost; they just need something to laugh at. This was the very moment luxury goods became objects of ridicule. As the stories people believed crumbled, even these fake videos became credible narratives. We must pause here for a moment. What were luxury companies doing in Korea at the time this cost-cutting exposé broke? The Korean subsidiary of one luxury brand donated 19.2 million won in a single year. This is equivalent to the price of a single Grand bag. Meanwhile, the dividends sent by the same entity to its headquarters amounted to 242.6 billion won. That brand is Dior. This means that the dividends were more than 12,000 times greater than the donations . Reports emerged that Louis Vuitton Korea, which generated 1.65 trillion won in sales, donated zero. The money spent by Korean consumers—who rank first in the world—did not remain in Korea but flowed to the headquarters. The cost was 80,000 won. The money left in Korea was the price of a single bag. The story of craftsmanship was thus shattered twice. The third blow was scarcity. To be precise, it was the collapse of scarcity. The power of luxury goods comes from the fact that not just anyone can possess them. However, during the COVID-19 retaliatory spending period, luxury goods became items that anyone could own. College students and young professionals alike bought these items, even if it meant taking out installment plans. Unboxing videos of luxury goods were uploaded to social media every day . The same logos were overflowing everywhere you went. So what happens then? The ID card that everyone possesses is no longer an ID card. The logo turns from a signal into noise. And then something strange happens. The truly wealthy have started hiding their logos. Plain clothes without logos. Sophistication recognized only by those in the know. This is the so-called " quiet luxury." The numbers speak for themselves. This year, a research company surveyed 1,000 adults. 76.8% responded that the ostentatious luxury style seen in science is actually tacky . 8%responded that "quiet luxury," where the logo is not visible, looks more sophisticated. Only 11.6%and a mere 6%responded that having a large luxury logo is better. In other words, nearly eight out of ten people view large logos as tacky. This means that logos, which were symbols of wealth until a few years ago, have now become a sign of ostentation. Here, another phenomenon emerges: duplication. This refers to consumption where consumers deliberately seek out cheaper substitutes with similar designs. One consumer stated that instead of using their usual Chanel beauty products, they buy similar items at Daiso for just 3,000 won. Walmart, the largest retail chain in the U.S., released a bag reminiscent of the Buckingham Bag. People called this bag the "Walking Bag ." Similar in both name and design to the Buckingham Bag, which costs tens of millions of won, this bag was priced in the 100,000 won range and sold out instantly upon release. In the past, carrying a fake would have been something to be ashamed of. However, now buying it cheaply is a source of pride. Those who bought the real thing at a high price are actually treated like suckers. The direction of shame has been reversed. And this reversal has changed the landscape of stores, starting with the store scene. On July 10, 2023, Chanel Korea abolished the pre-registration system that had been in operation at domestic department stores. It was analyzed that the opening was eliminated due to a decrease in on-site customer demand. Last January, luxury goods sales at department stores decreased by 7.2% compared to the same month of the previous year. The lines that used to form starting at 6:30 a.m. have disappeared. The profession of earning millions of won a month by standing in line for others has also lost its footing along with those lines. Bags that were once impossible to buy because they were sold out have become bags that no one bothers to stand in line for. Here, luxury goods fall into a dilemma. If they sell a lot, they become common; if they sell little, sales decline. If prices are raised, people leave, but if prices are lowered , there is a fear that they will be perceived as cheap. They have become bound by their own self-made rule that the more expensive something is, the more precious it is. Price tag cost, logo that has become commonplace. As Crown Prince Lee’s shovel dug the ground all at once, a tomb was revealed . However, you may raise an objection here. You might ask why Chanel Korea achieved its highest-ever performance when Chanel itself continued to raise prices. That is a good question, and the real answer lies within it. Let us place the same two companies side by side in the same Korean market. Chanel Korea saw its sales increase by 9%and operating profit by 25%in 2025. However , Christian Dior Louvre Korea, the Korean subsidiary of Dior, recorded sales of 945.3 billion won in 2024, a 9.6%decrease from the previous year. Its operating profit was 226.6 billion won, a 4%decrease from 27.4%. Within this same "Republic of Luxury," some rose while others plummeted. What caused the difference? The answer can be found by looking at the LMS. Hermès Korea's sales in 2023 were 797.2 billion won, an increase of 22.6%from the previous year. Hermès did not mass-produce goods even during the COVID-19 pandemic. To get your hands on a single Kelly bag, you must spend over 8 euros and wait months, or sometimes years. Consequently, Hermès bags often trade at higher prices in the secondhand market. Even the Burr bag, which is said to have dropped in value, still commands a resale price of about 50%above its original price. A bag that retains value even after a price drop is not the same as a luxury item that depreciates immediately after purchase. Hermès raised prices but did not sell its scarcity. Conversely, struggling brands sold off their scarcity at rock-bottom prices during the COVID-19 boom. A professor of retail studies analyzed the situation as follows: the scarcity of the luxury bag category itself has diminished compared to the past, and growth is being driven by ultra-high-end brands rather than the entire market. In other words, the luxury market has split into two branches: the top tier, which held out until the very end and was not easily conquered by just anyone, and the middle tier, which became commonplace after selling to everyone. The top tier caters exclusively to high-spending customers and sells at higher prices, while the middle tier fails to retain customers who are leaving. However, there is one chilling fact here: the way the top survived was ultimately by pushing out ordinary people. The fact that a Chanel bag became worth a hundred and a half million won a month was not a success, but a choice. It was a choice to erase the salaried worker from the customer list. At that moment, luxury goods transformed from a symbol of wealth that everyone dreams of into a luxury item for only a select few. And the majority, deprived of their dreams, began to mock those luxury goods. The product itself survived, but the symbol of "luxury" went into the grave. However, it was not the luxury companies that went into the grave first. It was the people who bought and sold luxury goods. Until a few years ago, luxury goods were referred to as a form of investment. It was a resale investment based on the belief that if you buy today, you can sell it for a higher price tomorrow. The statement that today is the cheapest day meant that if you resell it tomorrow, you will make a profit. Luxury platforms sprang up one after another upon that belief. But what happened when that belief collapsed? On March 31, 2025, the rebellion of first-generation luxury platforms filed for corporate rehabilitation with the court. The amount of money sellers were unable to receive totaled approximately 17.7 billion won. However, Banban itself assessed its liquidation value at about 1.268 billion won. It was calculated that if the company went bankrupt, the total amount to be returned to all commercial creditors would be merely 300 million won. This means that 300 million won would go to those who had been cheated out of 17.7 billion won. In other words, someone entitled to 100 won would receive less than 2 won. Since its establishment in 2015, Banban had never once posted a profit, and its accumulated deficit stood at 89 billion won. In the year immediately preceding its collapse, revenue stood at 27.862 billion won, a decrease of 29.1%. The market for buying and selling luxury goods was shrinking. Other platforms faced a similar situation. Musti's revenue for 2024 was 11.9 billion won, a decrease of 52.2%. Trenbe fell by 48% to 20.7 billion won. Both were cut in half within a year. So, what happened to the value of the bags and watches purchased? The resale premium for Rolex plummeted by half, dropping from 14%to 6%over the past two years. Even the Hermès Buckingham Bag, considered the epitome of luxury, saw its resale value fall to its lowest level since 2017. Excluding popular models, standard Hermès models are effectively trading at their original retail price. This means the calculation that one could profit by adding a premium has completely vanished. Resale investment was not an investment strategy at all. It was a gamble on the single premise that prices would continue to rise. And the stakes of that gamble were not the wealthy, but the installment payments of salaried workers. Let me make one thing clear here: the collapse of luxury goods did not happen all at once . The blunders continued steadily, one year at a time. Let us trace the events year by year. In November 2019, the Chanel Classic Flat Bag Medium cost 7.15 million won. It was the last time one could buy it with two months' salary. In the two years following COVID-19, Chanel raised prices ten times. Lines formed in front of bookstores at dawn, and waiting in line became a full-time job. In 2022, South Korea ranked first in the world for per capita luxury consumption. This was the peak. And the same luxury customers began to...In 2023, the global luxury market peaked at 369 billion euros. In July of the same year, Chanel Korea closed its doors. In April 2024, Armani , followed by Dio in June, successively entered court administration. A court ruling revealed that a size 53 bag was being sold for 2,600 euros. And so, sales in the Chinese luxury market plummeted by 20%in 2024. In March 2025, the luxury platform Rebellion files for corporate rehabilitation. In April, Hermès surpasses LVMH's market capitalization for the first time. In the same month, a video mocking the cost of a Buckingham bag went viral. In July, Ropiana enters court administration. In September, Gucci welcomes its fourth CEO in two years. And in July 2026, Chanel raises the price of its Classic Flat Bag Medium to 18.8 million won. In September, LVMH cedes the number one spot in French market capitalization to a cosmetics company. When laid out like this, it becomes clear. The Chinese economy cooled down in 2024. However, the shovel for price tags—which became common since 2019—was digging around 2021. The recession was not a shovel that dug a grave; it was the dirt poured onto a grave that had already been dug. Luxury brands walked into the grave they dug themselves. Then, to whom will the bill from this grave fly? Will it be the luxury companies? No. Hermès has reached the number one spot in market capitalization, and Chanel Korea has achieved record-breaking performance. The bill flies in exactly four directions: my closet, my shop, my bank account, and my pride. The first bill is my closet. If the bags and watches I bought in a crowd a few years ago are still in my closet, their value is already dropping. The more I endured the dawn in line at Open and even added udon noodles to buy the item, the greater the loss. Bags that were once said to be profitable to sell have now become bags that result in a loss when resold. The second bill is my shop. Sellers who listed their goods during the upheaval have not received 17.7 billion won. However, the amount they can potentially recover is only 300 million won. It is as if small business owners, who never even bought luxury goods, are left to clean up the mess caused by the luxury bubble. The fact that sales on Mustik and Trenbe have been cut in half means that the sales of the countless sellers operating on those platforms have also been cut in half. The first billing line is my bank account. The price of a Chanel bag has risen by 162.9%, while my salary has increased by only 17.2%. If you follow an item that is selling 9.5 times faster than your salary on installment payments, that installment will eventually be paid off by your bank account. It was not a delusion that you felt your salary had stagnated . The speed at which the bag sold was simply abnormal. Furthermore, a significant portion of the bag's value did not remain in Korea. The dividends sent by Dior Korea to headquarters amounted to 242.6 billion won, while the donations made to Korea totaled only 19.2 million won. The money we paid on installments became the cost of the logo and crossed the ocean. The fourth billing line is my pride. Only a little over one in ten people—a mere 11.6%—like having a large luxury brand logo. The logo you bought a few years ago, believing it to be a symbol of success, is now viewed as tacky. Spending money only to be ridiculed— this is the most expensive bill. As you listened to the story to the end, you probably thought to yourself, "I don't buy luxury goods, so it doesn't matter to me." But is that really the case? This isn't just about handbags. It is the story of every product that raises its price by selling a story. Apartments labeled "Premium," limited-edition sneakers, and even a cup of coffee supposedly crafted by a master artisan. The moment the story attached to the price tag outweighs the substance, the same thing happens. First, people line up; next, they are suspicious; and finally, they laugh. And when that ridicule begins, the person who suffers the loss is always the one who bought it last. That person could be me a few years from now. The claim that the cost of a 4 million won luxury bag was 80,000 won was not an internet exaggeration; it was a court document. And what is scarier than that number is the fact that people started laughing as soon as that figure was revealed. I hope you will keep just three things in mind. First, don't just look at the price tag; ask why the price is right. Ask if the materials have changed, if the maker has changed, or if only the price tag has changed. Second, don't buy based on mere wordplay. Saying "today is the cheapest day" is exactly what sellers love to hear. Third, choose based on who you are—the user—rather than the logo. An item recognized by others loses its value the very day their eyes change. I will leave you with this in a single line. Luxury brands didn't sell bags; they sold stories, and they turned those stories into cheap goods themselves. The shovel called the price tag, the shovel called the cost price, the shovel called the common logo. Luxury brands walked into the grave they dug for themselves using those three words.

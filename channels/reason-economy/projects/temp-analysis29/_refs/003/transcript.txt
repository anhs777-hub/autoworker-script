Our country’s shipyards have released their report cards for the first half of this year. Out of every 1 million won worth of ships built, how much was left as profit? Samsung Heavy Industries made about 100,000 won, and Hanwha Ocean about 140,000 won. However , one company cleared nearly 270,000 won. That is the highest figure among domestic shipbuilders. The company is located on the coast at the very end of Haenam, Jeollanam-do. It is a shipyard with just one single dock for building ships. Its name is Daehan Shipbuilding. But this shipyard was on the brink of being shut down 17 years ago. After that, the company's fate was left to the courts, and it took 13 years just to find a new owner. It’s a company that posted a deficit of 199.4 billion won just five years ago. How did this " Land's End" shipyard, which almost closed, outperform Samsung and Hanwha? The answer, surprisingly, lay in building the exact same ships over and over again. While it might just look like a single line in a news report, that line contains the entirety of a 17 -year journey of falling hard and rising again. This summer, a business news reporter visited this shipyard on the coast of Hwawon-myeon, Haenam. The first thing the reporter encountered was a huge, deep pit carved into the ground. Have you ever heard the term " dock"? Simply put, it’s a giant bathtub built by the sea. They drain the water and build the ship entirely on the floor. Then, they fill it with water to float the ship out. The length of this bathtub is 460 meters. It is 72 meters wide, leaving plenty of room on both sides even with a large ship inside. But there wasn’t just one ship inside. There was one ship standing at the front. And right behind it, another ship, about one-quarter complete, was attached. Two ships were tucked inside one bathtub, lined up one after another. Above them, a Goliath crane capable of lifting 1,500 tons was moving back and forth. Large shipyards operate several of these docks. They float a ship out of one dock while building another in a different dock. However, Daehan Shipbuilding has only this one dock. Please keep this single dock in mind. It will become the key to this story later on. Now, let’s bring up the numbers I mentioned at the beginning again. To be exact, it’s 26.8%. In the first six months of this year, they did 662.7 billion won worth of work and cleared 177.9 billion won. So, was it just a lucky six months? That is not the case. In the last three months of last year, they recorded the highest profit margin in their history. Even entering this year, they have maintained that level every quarter. It has already been seven quarters of maintaining a profit margin in the 20% range. Since a quarter is three months, that’s almost two years. That’s how much they’ve been making. You can see how rare this is by looking at how shipyards operate. It takes several years from the contract to the delivery of a ship. During that time, the cost of steel and labor keeps changing. That is why shipbuilding is considered a business where it's hard to make a large profit. Even big companies are praised if they make a profit on one out of ten ships sold. The reporter also wrote this in an article this summer. That it is an exceptional figure in the shipbuilding industry. Then, what kind of ships is Daehan Shipbuilding building in this one dock? The name of the ships coming out of that dock in a row is Suezmax. It’s an unfamiliar term, but the answer is right in the name. 'Suez' refers to the Suez Canal, and 'max' means the maximum size. A Suezmax is the largest oil tanker that can pass through the Suez Canal fully loaded. The Suezmax ships built by Daehan Shipbuilding are 157,000 tons and 274 meters long. That’s the length of two and a half football fields connected together. These ships mainly carry crude oil from the Middle East and West Africa to Europe and the Americas. There are even larger oil tankers. Very Large Crude Carriers carry much more at once, but they can only enter very deep ports. Suezmax ships can enter and exit ports of various depths. You can't fit a large double-door refrigerator in a small kitchen, no matter how spacious it is. A slightly smaller refrigerator fits into most kitchens. The Suezmax is that slightly smaller option. But lately, shipowners looking for these ships have increased. Why is that? Because winds have been blowing from three different directions at once. First, the Suezmax ships at sea have grown old. Four out of every ten ships are already over 20 years old. Oil tankers usually last about 20 years. And very few new ships were built. Second, the rules have changed. As international regulations took effect in 2023, old ships must either slow down or undergo modifications. It has become that much harder to hold on with old ships. The third reason was the biggest one. War broke out in the Middle East at the end of February this year, effectively blocking the Strait of Hormuz. The sea route from the Middle East to Korea, which used to take 25 days, has increased to nearly two months due to detours. When the route gets longer, you need more ships. Imagine the village well is blocked, so you have to go to a faraway well. You would need more water buckets to fetch the same amount of water, right? The same thing has started to happen at sea . As ships became scarce, the massive supertankers ran out first, and orders flooded into the Suezmax class. In the first half of this year alone, 70 Suezmax vessels were ordered worldwide. That's more than three times the number from the same period last year. But this trend didn't just blow toward DH Shipbuilding. Samsung Heavy Industries is also building the same type of ships . This July, a Turkish shipping company ordered Suezmaxes and split the order, giving two ships each to Samsung Heavy and DH Shipbuilding. It was the same month, the same ship, and the same client. Yet, their report cards were different. For every 1 million won in sales, DH Shipbuilding's profit was nearly three times that of Samsung Heavy and nearly double that of Hanwha Ocean. You might ask if they made less profit because they are a smaller company. That's a fair question. But K Shipbuilding, a mid-sized shipyard with sales similar to DH Shipbuilding in the first half of this year, also posted a 16.7%profit margin. That's a solid number. DH Shipbuilding is significantly higher even than that. It’s the top position among domestic shipbuilders. Let me make one thing clear. This is about the profit margin. In terms of the total amount of profit, Samsung Heavy Industries 'figure is more than three times that of DH Shipbuilding. Because their scale is much larger. What we are looking at is not the size of the business, but its efficiency. Now, to find the secret, we need to open the company's order book. They have 36 ships remaining, worth about 4.8 trillion won. That’s enough volume to fill their docks until 2029. But as you flip through this book, something catches your eye. Almost all 36 ships are of the same size. Even if the ship names differ, most are of the Suezmax class in size. Usually, shipyards don't do this. They mix various types of vessels, building oil tankers, container ships, and gas carriers. That’s because if one market dips, the others can provide support. It’s like planting lettuce, perilla leaves, and pumpkins in your garden instead of just chili peppers. If the peppers get diseased this year, you can still save the lettuce. DH Shipbuilding went the opposite way. They started heading that way around 2018. It was like planting only one crop of chili peppers in the entire garden. Why did they do that? Ships like giant oil tankers or LNG carriers are the home turf of large shipyards with multiple docks. With only one dock , it’s difficult to compete head-on in that arena. Instead, they already had the skills for oil tankers. As of 2019, they were the world's top recipient of orders for Aframax tankers —one size smaller—for four consecutive years. So, DH Shipbuilding chose a ship they could handle with one dock and the expertise they had accumulated. And at the same time, a ship that fetches a high enough price per vessel. That was the Suezmax. Then, what kind of difference does building only one type of ship make on the actual site? That difference begins to show on the sea. Ships aren't built in one piece inside a dock. A Suezmax ship is made by dividing it into 118 blocks. We make large steel pieces separately and weld them together later. The factory that makes these blocks is in the Daebul Industrial Complex in Yeongam, not Haenam. The completed blocks are loaded onto barges and brought to Haenam by sea. Three to five of them every day. But they can't just set sail whenever they want. They have to time it with the tides. Those who have lived by the sea will know. You have to time the entry and exit of ships with the rising and falling tides . The arriving blocks are joined into larger units of two or three, sometimes four. About 35 of these large units then go into the dock in sequence. The site monitors the weather one to two weeks in advance. If there's a forecast for strong winds, crane operations are moved up, and people even work weekends because blocks can't be left hanging in high winds. But what happens if the ship being built changes every time? The block shapes, the assembly order, and the barge departure dates all have to be planned from scratch. Conversely, if the same ships are built in a row, you can apply what you learned from the last ship to the next one. When you make kimchi for the first time, you scramble because you don't know how much salt to use. But after doing it for a few years, your hands know exactly how long to brine the cabbage. People, factories, and barge schedules also start to get into that rhythm. In the shipbuilding industry, this is called serial construction. The ships themselves have also been refined little by little. According to reports, this shipyard has revised the design of the same ship several times, and the current version burns 37 tons of fuel per day. It consumes several tons less than a Chinese ship of similar size. That's why European shipowners say that even if they pay 10%more, they can recoup the cost in seven years through fuel savings. In June this year, the head of the shipbuilding division at Daehan Shipbuilding explained it this way. By establishing a serial construction system, productivity has increased significantly, and by shortening the production cycle with accumulated know-how, they were able to secure profitable orders. Shortened the production cycle. I will break down what those few words actually meant in terms of days shortly. Before that, let's look at the buyers. Did the shipowners notice this difference? They did. And very quickly, too. In January of this year, global shipping companies ordered a total of 11 Suezmax ships. Six of them came to Haenam. This one shipyard at the edge of the land took over half of all the ship orders made in the world that month. The ship contracted at the end of that month was one that wouldn't be delivered until 2029. They’re lining up now to order ships for delivery three years from now . The orders didn't stop the following month. With six more ships added in the months after that, the total reached 12 in just three months. They hit their entire annual order target in just three months. At that point, for every four Suezmax vessels ordered globally, one was from Daehan Shipbuilding. By summer, the orders piled up even higher . By the end of July, they had 17 orders, worth about 2.25 trillion won. It’s the biggest order intake since the company was founded. And this is from a shipyard that was nearly shut down. But what’s more noteworthy is the price. It’s the same type of ship , but the price climbed steadily like a staircase. A ship that cost about 125 billion won early in the year was contracted at the highest price in company history by summer. It means they aren't just selling more, but getting better value for the same product. The words of their sales head point in the same direction. Back in January, he stated this. He said that among overseas shipping lines, the perception is spreading that Daehan makes the best Suezmaxes, and the repeat order rate is steadily rising. In fact, the number of shipping lines doing business with the company has grown from five to 14 over the past four years. Think of a skilled local hair salon and it's similar. A customer who visits once comes back, and next time, they bring a neighbor along. So, at the edge of the land, there is one dock, and they build almost only one type of ship. Orders flocked to this shipyard, which mastered that one type through repetition, and prices went up as well. But there is a catch here. If there is only one dock, where did they find the room for all these extra orders? The most precious thing at a shipyard is the space inside the dock. One ship must be finished and floated out before the next one can enter. So, how many ships are built in a year depends on how many times the dock empties and fills. It’s easy to think of a restaurant with only one table. To serve more customers, you either have to add tables or reduce the time each group stays. Daehan Shipbuilding’s single dock operates on the basis of building 11 Suezmaxes in a row per year . Usually, when a shipyard wants to earn more, they first think about digging another dock. But Daehan Shipbuilding found a different way without expanding its docks. They reduced the time each ship stays in the dock. The words of the business division head, who mentioned shortening the process cycle. That is exactly what this number is. The time a ship stays in the dock has been shortened from 4.5 weeks to 4 weeks. It was only a four-day reduction. And this shipyard is now actually launching a ship every four weeks. According to the company, it’s rare for a shipyard to launch ships of this size so frequently. You might think four days isn’t such a big deal. But the story changes when it adds up 11 times a year. Four days times 11 is over a month. That creates a gap of over a month on the calendar. An entire ship can fit into that gap. That’s how new slots were created in 2028 and 2029 for one ship each. A vacancy appeared on a calendar that was previously packed with work. The newly created 2028 slot didn't stay empty for long. A contract to build two ships came in at the end of May this year, with delivery scheduled for the end of 2028. The price was 141.4 billion won per ship. It was the highest price received that year up to that point. It’s a slot created by cutting four days without digging an extra dock. The company is going further, aiming to build more than 12 ships per year in the future. It means they will keep trying to clear one product faster in the same dock. So, who was the shipowner that filled these three slots ? Looking through that shipowner’s order book, you find some unexpected records. The party that filled that slot on May 29th was a shipping company in Oceania. They are not a long-time regular customer. They were a new client that first started doing business with the shipyard last year. If you follow this shipping company’s orders in order, it looks like this. They entrusted one ship for the first time last September. Two months later, they entrusted one more. After the new year, they started ordering two ships at a time. Once in the winter, and twice in the spring. That adds up to eight ships. But there is something remarkable about these eight ships. This shipping company hasn't even received their first ship yet. They committed to eight ships before even seeing with their own eyes how their ship turns out. The company even specifically highlighted, while introducing this client, that they placed more orders before the first ship was delivered. The price of one ship is well over 100 billion won. It’s like entrusting the next house, and then the one after that, to the same carpenter. And not to a carpenter they’ve known for a long time, but to one they met just last year. Why did they do that? Because when a shipowner chooses a shipyard, they don't just look at the price. Will the ship be delivered on the promised date? Will this ship be made as well as the previous one? Can they still build it like that several years from now? A shipyard that has built the same ship multiple times knows exactly when the next one will be ready. If it’s a dock with a familiar schedule, the delivery date is effectively already marked on the calendar. And the shipowner knows that too. To a shipowner, a ship delivered on time is money in itself. If a ship is late, the oil it was meant to carry and the freight revenue both come to a halt. We shared a story about Ulsan in 1975 a while back. It was the story of an oil tanker that the shipowner refused to accept because it was one day late. For a shipyard, a delivery deadline is that heavy of a promise. Last summer, the company's CEO said this. Even when management was difficult, we never once missed a delivery date. He said that thanks to those kept promises, there are shipowners who have been doing business with them for over 20 years. That short phrase, "when management was difficult," is something I can't just brush off. Because this company wasn't always this kind of company. Its roots trace back to a small shipyard in Yeosu in 1987. In 2004, a mid-sized group based in Gwangju and South Jeolla Province bought the company and renamed it Daehan Shipbuilding. The parent company was the Daeju Group. And the following year, they began building a new shipyard on the coast of Hwawon-myeon, Haenam. Now, let’s go back to 2:00 PM on April 11, 2008. The first ship rose to the water at the newly opened Haenam shipyard. It was a 170,000-ton class bulk carrier ordered by a Norwegian shipowner. It is a ship that carries dry cargo like coal or iron ore. It headed out to sea just eight months after the keel was first laid. The president at the time said this ship gave significant momentum to their shipbuilding business. It wasn't an empty promise. There were 43 ships written in the order book. In monetary terms, it was over 3.5 billion dollars' worth of work. The new shipyard at the edge of the land was just about to spread its wings. But it was exactly five months later. The financial crisis that began in the US in September 2008 swept over the world. Ship prices collapsed, and new orders came to a sudden halt. Daehan Shipbuilding was in the middle of building the shipyard and fulfilling orders all at once. The crisis struck at a time when they needed the most capital. On January 20, 2009, after the turn of the year, the creditors evaluated and announced the credit ratings of construction and shipbuilding companies. Daehan Shipbuilding received a C rating. It was a "workout" decision, meaning signs of insolvency were showing, so creditors would manage the company to keep it alive. But there was one more name in the same announcement. It was Daeju Construction, from the same group . What Daeju Construction received was a D rating, meaning expulsion. It was a one-notch difference. Watching its sister company get the boot right from the sidelines, Daehan Shipbuilding barely managed to stay on the surviving side. So, now that it had survived, did a path forward open up? Not quite. No one was stepping up to buy this shipyard. Several sale negotiations took place, but they all fell through. Ultimately, from 2011, another company began managing it instead. It was Daewoo Shipbuilding&Marine Engineering, now known as Hanwha Ocean. However, five years into the workout program, an even bigger hurdle faced the company. On June 27, 2014, at the Seoul Central District Court, Daehan Shipbuilding filed for rehabilitation proceedings. It is commonly known as court receivership. It’s a process where the court evaluates whether or not to save the company. What held them back was a debt guarantee from a defunct former affiliate. It was as if they were about to lose their home because of a single stamp of guarantee for money they hadn't even spent. Yet, the company stood back up from this faster than expected. In October 2015, the autumn of the following year, they graduated from court receivership. The court stated that they had paid off all their debts as required. By that time, 39 ships had sailed from these docks. Still, there was no owner to be found. After graduation, management by the Korea Development Bank continued. 2021 was a year of deficit. For every 1 million won worth of ships built, they were losing over 150,000 won. There is one employee who remembers those days. Having worked at the company for 21 years, they looked back on that time like this. A past where they had to accept cheap orders, knowing they were poisoned chalices. Compared to now, it feels like an entirely different era. It was a time when they had to take on work despite knowing it meant a loss just to survive. But throughout this long time, one thing never changed. The ships left on schedule. The words of the CEO, claiming they had never once missed a delivery date, even in difficult times. Those difficult times were exactly this period. The company explains that even in the years they received a 'C' rating, were managed by others, or were frequenting the courts, the dates promised to shipowners were always kept. While researching this story, this was the scene that stayed with me the longest. Even as the business was crumbling without an owner , the people working in the docks were looking at the date the next ship would depart. Perhaps the reason the Oceanian shipping company placed another order before even receiving their first ship was being built up during these very days. In August 2022, a new owner finally appeared. It was 13 years after entering the workout program. And in August 2025, Daehan Shipbuilding was listed on the stock market. A company that has fallen once did not try to grow its size prematurely while standing back up. The company that took a big risk and failed didn’t choose a bigger shipyard, but the one ship they knew best. From layoffs in 2009 to court receivership in 2014, it took 13 years to find a new owner, and this year, it became the most profitable shipbuilder in the country. But isn't there any concern about relying so heavily on just one type of ship? Of course there is. First, it’s the fact that they are focused on a single vessel type. Almost all of their remaining orders are for Suezmax-sized ships. If demand for these ships drops, the shock will hit all at once. Also, there are other factors mixed into the current numbers. Shuttle tankers ordered two years ago are now showing up in the revenue, and they are the same size as Suezmax ships but priced about 1.5 times higher. The company explicitly stated they also benefited from the exchange rate. This means this year's profit margin isn't solely due to their efficiency. In fact, having too many orders could also become a future risk. Currently, one out of every four Suezmax ships sailing the seas has been ordered globally. It’s the highest volume in 14 years. If these ships all hit the water at once in 2028 and 2029, freight rates could plummet. Once the war ends, some of the surged demand might also disappear. There are also demands to increase the prices of the thick steel plates used to build these ships. That’s why the company has started preparing for what comes after this ship. In June of this year, their design for a very large gas carrier received basic approval. In the same month, they were selected for a project to maintain naval vessels. It’s not that they can’t build other ships, but rather that they’ve been focusing their efforts on one. There are three things to watch going forward. Whether the goal of 12 ships actually shows up in the numbers, whether freight rates hold up when new ships pour in, and whether the gas carrier design leads to its first order . From that dock in Haenam where the first ship floated in April 2008, through the ups and downs of court receivership, one ship now heads out to sea every four weeks. The shipyard at the end of the land that almost closed down has become the most cost-effective shipbuilder in Korea.

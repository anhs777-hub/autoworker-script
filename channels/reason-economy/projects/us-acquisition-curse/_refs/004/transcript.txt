If you look at the 1990 global semiconductor sales rankings. Number one was Japan's NEC, and right behind it was Toshiba. Six out of the top ten companies were Japanese. As for Samsung Electronics. Their name wasn't even on the list. Samsung didn't enter the top 12 globally until the mid-1990s. That's not all. In December 1992, Toshiba handed over its NAND flash design license to Samsung Electronics. A teacher essentially handed their textbook directly to a student. But now . On December 20, 2023, Toshiba shares disappeared from the Tokyo Stock Exchange. It had been exactly 74 years since they listed in 1949. You can't buy Toshiba stock anywhere in the world anymore. Conversely, for that NAND flash, Samsung Electronics is now world number one, and SK Hynix is number two. Even though it was an invention by Toshiba. How was this possible? Did the U.S. suppress them to make it happen? Or was it a grave they dug for themselves? To start with the conclusion, just four decisions were enough for Toshiba to collapse. And those four decisions share one chilling commonality. Before I tell you that commonality, you need to see what kind of company Toshiba was. Otherwise, you won't grasp how absurd this story really is. Toshiba's roots go back to 1875. It started as a small factory in Tokyo opened by a man named Hisashige Tanaka. Do you know this man's nickname ? He is the Edison of Japan. During the Edo period, he made spring-powered dolls, oil lamps that drew up fuel automatically, and later even steam locomotive models. In other words, Toshiba started as a company that made things from the very beginning. Please keep this point in mind. It will come up again later. Then in 1939, Shibaura Engineering Works and Tokyo Electric merged to become the Toshiba we know today. In May 1949, they listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange. After that, Toshiba was behind almost everything Japan produced. They released Japan's first automatic electric rice cooker in 1955 and created Japan's first microwave in 1959. The T1100 they launched in 1985 is considered the first product to popularize IBM-compatible laptops worldwide. It was the same for semiconductors. In 1985, Toshiba was one of the leading companies that succeeded in developing the 1MB DRAM. And in the mid-to-late 1980s, Toshiba's share of the global DRAM market was enormous. No company today holds that kind of position with a single product. So, in the 1980s, Toshiba wasn't just a big company; it was a company that kept being the first to create things the world had never seen. And this wasn't just Toshiba's story alone. In 1990, six out of the world’s top ten semiconductor companies were Japanese. There were only a few American companies, even including Intel. No other country has managed to repeat that record since. Do you know how anxious the U.S. was in the 1980s? The U.S. DRAM market share, which was 70%in 1978, plummeted to 20% by 1986. It had been cut to a third in just eight years. As of 1985, 92%of the 256K chips sold in the U.S. market were made in Japan. Nine out of ten products in American homes were Japanese. Back then, U.S. media ran daily articles claiming Japan was buying up the entire country. And at the heart of that was Toshiba. So, what was Korea doing at the same time? On February 8, 1983, Chairman Lee Byung-chul declared in Tokyo that he would enter the semiconductor business. This was the so-called Tokyo Declaration. Do you know how the Japanese business world reacted? They laughed. Korea doing semiconductors sounded like a joke to them. It is said that a report actually circulated in Japan detailing exactly why Samsung could not succeed in the industry. But that same year, Samsung developed a 64K DRAM. It was the third in the world, following the U.S. and Japan. Still, the gap was enormous. It wasn't until 1986 that Samsung Electronics 'semiconductor revenue exceeded 100 million won. That was 11 years after they started. Reaching 1 trillion won took until 1991. It took a whopping 17 years. During that time, Samsung's semiconductor division was consistently in the red. After acquiring Korea Semiconductor in 1974, they only poured money in for 14 years before turning a profit in 1988. Just imagine how it felt to walk into a boardroom every year with a deficit report. And that went on for 14 years. So, the picture in 1990 looked like this. On one side, there was Toshiba, holding numerous "world's second" and " world's first" titles. On the other side was Samsung Electronics, which had just turned a profit after 14 years of losses. To anyone, it looked like the game was already over. But at that very moment, inside Toshiba, something was already made that would completely change the company's fate. The problem was that Toshiba was treating that invention as the most trivial thing in the company. That is the first fatal decision. There was a man born in 1943 named Fujio Masuoka. He was an engineer who earned his doctorate from Tohoku University and joined Toshiba's R&D center in 1971. In 1980, this man filed a patent. A memory that keeps data even when the power is turned off. It was the prototype of what we now call USB drives, SSDs, and smartphone storage. But there is something strange here. Masuoka was in the DRAM division at the time. This patent was not his assigned job. So, what he did was work on it on Saturdays and Sundays. It wasn't something the company ordered; he sat alone on weekends and drew the blueprints for something that didn't exist in the world. It is said that the name was also coined within the company . Apparently, a fellow researcher saw the memory erasing process and said something. "It looks like a camera flash going off," they said. That is known to be why it became called "flash memory." In 1984, Masuoka presented NOR-type flash at the International Electron Devices Meeting, and in 1987, he presented NAND-type flash in San Francisco. He was 40 years old then. The NAND flash currently found in data centers, smartphones, and laptops worldwide began with that 1987 presentation by a Toshiba researcher. So, what did Toshiba give this person? They gave him a bonus. A few hundred dollars, to be exact. In our currency, that’s just a few hundred thousand won. That was the company's valuation of an invention that changed the world. And what followed is even more absurd. After that, Masuoka was pushed into a marginal position. It is also said that because his invention wasn't selling within the company, he personally went around to American computer companies to explain it. The specific details vary slightly depending on the source. However, the result is clear. He later stated it himself. That at Toshiba, the flash memory technology was not valued at all. Think about this for a second, logically. If a company has a product the world has never seen before, you would usually want to sell it first. But Toshiba moved in the complete opposite direction. Do you know what the result was? In 1988, Intel beat them to the punch. They successfully commercialized a 256 k-bit flash chip. Toshiba invented it, but America made the money first. It is known that only after Intel succeeded did Toshiba's management approve Masuoka's NAND. It was their own product, but they only acknowledged it after seeing someone else make money from it. Then, in December 1992, Toshiba transferred the NAND flash design license to Samsung Electronics. At the time, it was a trivial decision. They viewed it as a business that wouldn't work out anyway. They probably calculated that it would be better to have multiple companies making it to grow the market. But look at the result this brought about. Samsung released a 16M NAND in 1994, developed a 1-gigabit product in 1999, and began mass production of 1-gigabit NAND for the first time in the world in 2002. That year, Samsung's global NAND market share was 54%. Just 10 years after receiving the license, they took over half the market. The funny thing is that in 2001, Toshiba proposed a joint venture to Samsung. Samsung declined. They figured they could do it on their own by then. It was the moment when the positions of the one who gave the textbook and the one who received it were completely reversed 10 years later. Masuoka left Toshiba in 1994 to become a professor at Tohoku University . Then, in March 2004, he filed a lawsuit against the company at the Tokyo District Court. He claimed 1 billion yen, arguing that Toshiba had earned at least 20 billion yen from his invention. Do you know how much he received in total compensation for his inventions during his tenure? It was 6 million yen. That's less than 60 million won in our currency. A settlement was reached on July 27, 2006 , and the amount Toshiba paid was 87 million yen. That amount included everything: all service inventions made while employed, foreign patents, and even pending know-how. From the company's perspective, the inventor's share for the technology inside every smartphone was just 87 million yen. While Toshiba was losing what it had created with its own hands, something far more frightening was happening outside. It was July 2, 1987, in front of the U.S. Capitol in Washington. Several members of the House of Representatives stood in front of the cameras. They were people like Don Ritter of Pennsylvania, Helen Bentley of Maryland, and Elton Gallegly of California. Do you know what they were holding in their hands? It was a large sledgehammer. And on the ground sat a Toshiba radio. The congressmen smashed the radio to pieces with the sledgehammer in front of the rolling cameras. Why did they do that? It was revealed that Toshiba Machine, a subsidiary of Toshiba, had violated COCOM regulations by exporting machine tools to the Soviet Union. If the Soviets used these machines to mill submarine propellers, the noise would be significantly reduced. That would make it much harder for the U.S. Navy to detect Soviet submarines. The U.S. government found out through a tip-off from a company employee in late 1986, and it blew up when Japanese media reported it in March 1987. The U.S. Department of Defense concluded that it had dealt a real blow to Western security. Eventually, the Japanese government imposed a one-year export ban on Toshiba Machine, and even the president of the parent company, Toshiba, had to step down. What’s interesting is that the U.S. media barely covered this sledgehammer performance. It was just one of many common political shows. But in Japan, this scene was broadcast repeatedly. The image of a Japanese company being smashed with a hammer in front of the U.S. Capitol. This was no longer just news; it became a collective sentiment. The feeling that the U.S. could strike us at any time. One year earlier, on September 2, 1986, the U.S.-Japan Semiconductor Agreement was signed. Looking at the background, you can understand why the U.S. went to such lengths. The U.S. DRAM market share, which was 70%in 1978, had plummeted to 20%by 1986. But here, we need to overturn a common piece of conventional wisdom. People say the U.S. Semiconductor Agreement killed the Japanese semiconductor industry. If you only look at the years immediately following the agreement, it was the exact opposite. As Japan put quotas on export volumes, supply shrank, and prices actually went up. Japanese companies saw their profits increase. Even after the agreement was signed, Japan continued to dominate the DRAM market for a while. So, it wasn't the text of the agreement that brought Toshiba down. It was the attitude that remained afterward. "We must not provoke the U.S.""It's dangerous to take big risks.""When the market is bad , we must play it safe." This mindset became deeply ingrained within the company. And the semiconductor industry is a place where that judgment is most fatal. It is an industry where the company that builds factories even when prices crash becomes the leader in the next cycle. During the same period, South Korea walked the exact opposite path. In the late 1980s, when DRAM prices plummeted, Samsung never stopped investing. In August 1990, they succeeded in developing a 16M DRAM prototype, which came at a time when no other overseas company could produce one. They commercialized it in March 1991, and in 1992, they developed 64M DRAM, rising to become the world's number one in DRAM. In 1993, they became the number one in the entire memory semiconductor sector. They have held that position ever since. Those few years when Toshiba decided to play it safe were the time the tables turned for Korea. So, what happened to Toshiba's DRAM business? In December 2001, Toshiba completely stepped away from the commodity DRAM business. They handed over their Dominion Semiconductor plant in Manassas, Virginia, to Micron. It was reported that 600 of the 1,400 people working at that plant lost their jobs. The company that created the world’s first 1MB DRAM in 1985 had completely exited the DRAM business 16 years later. They voluntarily folded a business that had once held the "world's first" title. But Toshiba didn't stop there. They decided to fill the void left by quitting production with acquisitions. And that decision would end up costing the company its life. In early 2006, news broke that Toshiba had acquired the U.S. nuclear power company Westinghouse. What kind of company was Westinghouse? It was the company that built the world's first commercial pressurized water reactor. It is a name referred to as the origin of nuclear power generation. The contract was finalized in mid-October of that year, with a total acquisition price of $ 5.4 billion. Toshiba took a 77%stake, paying $ 4.158 billion just for their share. Let's compare just one number here. Westinghouse was acquired by the British company BNFL in 1999. The price back then was 1.1 billion dollars. In just seven years, the company's value had increased nearly fivefold. It wasn’t as if Westinghouse had suddenly become an incredible company in that time. Given the profits the company was making, the prevailing view was that it was absurdly expensive. Japanese media reported that Toshiba had bid several times the market price to win the deal instantly. It is said that Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, a competitor, was skeptical about whether the investment would yield any real returns. In simple terms, Toshiba alone paid a huge premium for an asset that other companies deemed too expensive. The person who pushed for this decision was the then-president, Atsutoshi Nishida. Born in 1943, he was sixty-two at the time of the acquisition. It was reported that he had received the board's approval to make the final decision. Nishida, along with Vice President Norio Sasaki, then made a public pledge. They would win contracts for 39 new nuclear reactors by 2015. They intended to triple the size of their nuclear business. Thirty-nine reactors. They were claiming that one company would win in ten years the volume of projects that a single country would build over several decades. There was an atmosphere that made such a plan seem plausible. In the mid-2000s, the world was talking about a "nuclear renaissance." With rising oil prices and discussions on greenhouse gas regulations, there was an influx of forecasts that the era of nuclear power had returned. But there is a very important point we must address here. Toshiba was already a company that knew how to build nuclear reactors. Even without buying Westinghouse, they were already in the nuclear business. So, this wasn't about buying technology they didn't have; it was a decision to bet 5.4 billion dollars to expand a business they were already doing. Then, five years later, on March 11, 2011, the Fukushima disaster occurred. There is something chilling about this. Out of the six reactors at the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant, Toshiba built units 3 and 5. GE handled the design, but the manufacturing was split between GE, Toshiba, and Hitachi. Just five years after Toshiba spent 5.4 billion dollars hoping to make a fortune in nuclear power, they were live-streaming the scenes of their own reactors instilling global fear of nuclear energy. Following the accident, orders for new nuclear plants froze worldwide. Germany even decided to phase out nuclear power entirely. The plan to win 39 contracts became nothing more than a piece of paper. However, Toshiba did not pull out at this point. There was construction already underway in the United States. These were the Vogtle units 3 and 4 in Georgia and the V.C. Summer units 2 and 3 in South Carolina. Both broke ground in 2013. The problem was the terms of the contract. They were effectively fixed-price contracts. It was a structure where the builder, not the client, had to shoulder the cost difference no matter how much construction expenses ballooned. And in 2015, Toshiba pulled the final trigger. Westinghouse acquired Stone&Webster, the construction division of a company called CB&I. Here is why this was fatal . It meant Westinghouse took on the entire risk from the builder's side, which until then had been shared in the event of construction cost overruns. Delays are standard in nuclear power plant construction. Safety regulations change, designs change, and labor becomes hard to find. Toshiba ended up receiving the bill for all of those overruns all by itself. The bill arrived shortly thereafter. In April 2016, they cleared out 260 billion yen in losses related to Westinghouse. That year, Toshiba's final net loss was about 483.2 billion yen. But this was only the beginning. In late December 2016, Toshiba warned that losses in its nuclear business could reach hundreds of billions of yen. They couldn't even state the exact amount; it was just a warning that they were likely in for a massive hit. At those words, the stock price plunged by more than 25%in a single day. One-quarter of the company's value vanished overnight. Two months later, on February 14, 2017, Toshiba announced the finalized loss amount at 712.5 billion yen. The chairman stepped down from his position on the same day. The final net loss for the 2016 fiscal year was 965.6 billion yen, which is over 9 trillion won in our currency. Ultimately, on March 29, 2017, Westinghouse filed for bankruptcy protection in a U.S. court. This was 11 years after Toshiba had bought it. Then , what was South Korea doing during the same period? Around the time Toshiba bought Westinghouse. South Korea was preparing to apply its self-developed APR1400 reactor model to Shin-Kori units 3 and 4 for the first time. Actual construction began between 2007 and 2008. On one side, there was a group trying to become a nuclear powerhouse by buying another company, and on the other, a group that had just broken ground after developing its own reactor model. I will explain later where these two paths eventually meet again. But at this point, a different kind of collapse had already been underway inside Toshiba for a long time . One day in 2012, Toshiba President Norio Sasaki called in the head of the PC business. And he said this. "Improve operating profit by 12 billion yen in the three days remaining until the end of the first half.""Report the results tomorrow." At the time, the projected deficit for that business unit was 24.8 billion yen. Telling them to come up with 12 billion yen in three days is practically an order to cook the books. Toshiba called this the "Challenge." It means to take on a challenge. It was a setting where the president would throw a number at a division head, demanding it be met that quarter, and hinting at business withdrawal if they failed. It was called a challenge, but in reality, it was an order. In February 2015, Japan's Securities and Exchange Surveillance Commission launched an investigation. In April, the company formed a special investigation committee, and in July, a third-party committee of outside lawyers released a report. The conclusion was this. From 2008 to the third quarter of 2014. Meaning, over seven years, they inflated pre-tax profits by 151.8 billion yen. And this figure later increased to about 224.8 billion yen after further investigations and re-audits. Their methods included abusing percentage-of-completion accounting, creating fake profits by selling parts to subsidiaries, and pushing necessary costs to the next quarter. On July 21, 2015, President Hisao Tanaka stands at a press conference. And he bows his head for a long time in front of the flashing cameras. At this event, not only Tanaka , but also his predecessor Norio Sasaki and the one before him, Atsutoshi Nishida, resign. Eight board members also step down together. Three presidents disappeared all at once. If you look at the relationship between these three for a moment, you can see what state the company was in. Nishida had originally strongly pushed for Sasaki as his successor. It is said that he even stated there was no one else but him. But after Sasaki became president, the two turned against each other. In 2013, when announcing Tanaka as the new president, Nishida said to get Toshiba back on a growth trajectory . Basically, he publicly made a remark that sounded like the previous management was a failure. While the company was collapsing, the top leadership was busy pushing each other out. On December 25 of the same year, the Financial Services Agency imposed a fine of 7.373 billion yen on Toshiba. It was the largest amount in Japanese history. Since the previous record was around 1.6 billion yen, it was more than four times higher. In September, the Tokyo Stock Exchange designated Toshiba as a security on alert. From then on, there are only stories of selling things off. In 2016, they handed over their white goods business to China's Midea. It was 53.7 billion yen for an 80%stake. The company that made Japan's first electric rice cooker sold its rice cooker business. They sold the medical equipment business to Canon for 665 billion yen. In 2017, they sold the TV business to China's Hisense for 12.9 billion yen. From the company that made Japan's first color TV. In 2018, they handed over the PC business to Sharp. The company that made Japan's first laptop sold its laptop business. The list Toshiba used to boast as "world's first" and " Japan's first" became their list of assets to sell. But here, common sense is flipped once again. Usually, when a company is in trouble, they sell the failing businesses first. Toshiba was the exact opposite. They sold what was doing the best. In 2017, Toshiba put its semiconductor division up for sale. NAND flash, that is. The very thing Masuoka sat alone to design on weekends . After a 271-day acquisition battle, it went to a U.S.-Japan-Korea consortium led by Bain Capital in September 2017. The sale price was 2 trillion yen. SK Hynix invested 395 billion yen into that consortium. That’s over 4 trillion won in our money. The company that handed over NAND blueprints to Samsung in 1992 ended up selling that NAND division to a consortium including a Korean firm 25 years later. Why did they sell it? Because if they hadn't, the company would have ceased to exist. As of March 2017, Toshiba's net assets stood at -581.6 billion yen. Debt exceeded assets by more than 5 trillion won. In other words, it was in a state of complete capital erosion. On August 1, 2017, it was relegated from the first section to the second section of the Tokyo Stock Exchange. It took another six years after that. In December 2017, they raised 600 billion yen in capital, bringing in about 60 overseas activist funds. This meant 35%of the issued shares changed hands at once after the capital increase. The fight between management and the funds began then, and a report released in June 2021 was the final blow. It stated that the 2020 shareholder meeting could not be considered to have been operated fairly . It claimed the company colluded with the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry to block shareholder proposals . In March 2022, a plan to split the company was rejected at the general shareholders' meeting. Finally, in March 2023, a fund called Japan Industrial Partners stepped up to buy all of Toshiba for 4,620 yen per share, totaling 2 trillion yen. The tender offer was successful on September 20, and on December 20, 2023, Toshiba was delisted. Now, look at those four decisions again. The man who invented NAND left the company with 6 million yen. The man who bought Westinghouse for three times its market value became president. The man who demanded 12 billion yen be created in three days was also president. And the people who met those numbers were promoted. Do you know what they have in common? Yes, all four times, the person who built things was pushed out, while the person who bought them or cooked the books moved up. A company founded by a man who made clockwork dolls in 1875 had become a company that undervalued making things after 148 years. There is something we need to point out for fairness here. So , is Toshiba a failed company now? That is not the case. It is quite the opposite. In the fiscal year ending March 2026, Toshiba posted a net profit of 1.9673 trillion yen. About seven times higher than the previous period. It is the largest net profit in the company's history. With the AI boom, demand for data center hard disks exploded, reviving their core business as well. The private equity fund that acquired them says they plan to re-list as early as 2028. Just looking at the numbers, it looks like a comeback story . But if you look at where that 1.9673 trillion yen actually came from, the story changes. What exactly was the 2.277 trillion yen that Toshiba reflected in its earnings that year? It was the capital gains and revaluation profits from selling Kioxia shares. What is Kioxia, anyway? It is the semiconductor division that Toshiba sold off for 2 trillion yen in 2017. Kioxia is simply the new name they gave it. In short, the biggest profit in Toshiba's history wasn't from making and selling goods, but from selling shares of a child they had already sold off. So, how big has that "child" grown since then? As of early August 2026, Kioxia, which listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange in December 2024, has a market cap between $ 180 billion and $ 190 billion. It’s inside the top 100 global companies. Stock price volatility is so high that the ranking changes every time you look. However, when the fund bought all of Toshiba in 2023, they valued it at 2 trillion yen, or about $ 14 to $ 15 billion at the time. To summarize it, it looks like this. The single semiconductor company that Toshiba cut off and sold has grown to be worth over 12 times the entire Toshiba corporation. What Toshiba sold to save its own life was actually the only real asset the company had left. And looking at Kioxia’s shareholder registry is even more ironic. As of 2026, Toshiba is the top shareholder with a 15%stake. The de facto second-largest shareholder is SK Hynix, holding 14%. The difference is just 1 percentage point. Toshiba is still selling off its shares, and SK Hynix hasn't even converted its convertible bonds into stock yet. The company that invented NAND flash and the company from the country that adopted the technology are now sitting side-by-side as the top two shareholders of the same firm. Market share had already flipped a long time ago. As of Q3 2025, the NAND market share is 32.3%for Samsung Electronics in 1st place, 19.3%for SK Hynix in 2nd, and 15.3%for Kioxia in 3rd. The inventor is in 3rd place. What about the nuclear power side? I mentioned earlier that the two paths would eventually meet again. Westinghouse, which Toshiba sent into bankruptcy, was sold to Brookfield in 2018, and then bought back again in 2023 by Canada's Cameco and Brookfield for $ 7.9 billion. And do you know who Westinghouse sued in October 2022? It was Korea Hydro&Nuclear Power. They claimed that Korea’s APR 1400 was based on their technology, so it shouldn't be exported without their permission. In September 2023, a U.S. court dismissed the suit, stating Westinghouse lacked standing, and the two sides reached a final agreement in January 2025. And Korea wins the contract for the Dukovany nuclear plant in the Czech Republic. The deal is worth approximately 18.6 billion dollars. The company that Toshiba bought for 5.4 billion dollars and ultimately drove into bankruptcy failed to block Korea's nuclear exports 20 years later. Where did it all go wrong? It wasn't because they lacked the technology. Toshiba invented NAND and knew how to build nuclear reactors. It wasn't because they lacked money, either. They were a company that could spend 5.4 billion dollars in cash. The divide boils down to just one thing. Did the company value the act of making things the most, or did it value the act of acquiring and balancing the numbers? Toshiba chose the latter, and that choice cost them 74 years of listing history and 148 years of reputation. Think back to the 1990 ranking I showed you at the beginning. 1st place NEC, 2nd place Toshiba, 3rd place Hitachi. Samsung Electronics was not on that list. And in December 1992, Toshiba handed over their NAND flash blueprints to a company not even on that list. Back then, no one in the Toshiba boardroom would have thought this was a major decision. It was a business that wasn't making money anyway. 34 years later, the company that received those blueprints has become world number one, and the company that handed them over has vanished from the stock market. The lesson Toshiba left behind is not that they had their technology stolen. The technology remained within Toshiba the whole time. They still had NAND, they had reactors, and they had plenty of " world's first" records. The problem was that the people who created that technology held the lowest positions in the company. The person who drew the blueprints alone on the weekend left with 6 million yen, while the person who bought another company at three times the market price became the president. The person who demanded results in three days was also the president. Companies don't fail because they can't make things. They fail when they undervalue the act of making things. That is why this story doesn't just sound like something happening in a foreign country. Korea currently bets nearly a quarter of its exports on semiconductors alone. And the reason Korea holds that position is because there were people who built factories when prices plummeted in the 1980s and endured 14 years of deficits. This does not mean I am saying Samsung will become the next Toshiba. That would be a lazy analogy. Samsung continues to spend a record amount on R&D every year , and SK Hynix has become the second-largest shareholder in the company Toshiba sold. The situation is completely different. However, this is what Toshiba teaches us. That even in a collapsing company, the technology remained until the very end. What disappeared was not the technology, but the value the company placed on the people who created it. A company founded in 1875 by a man making wind-up dolls in a small Tokyo factory closed its doors 148 years later, having spent its time cooking the books instead of making products. At the top of the shareholder list of the world's most successful NAND flash company, the country that invented it and the country that acquired it are listed side by side, with only a 1 percentage point difference. Which way that single-slot gap widens will likely depend on who treats their creators with more respect today.

There was a flood of criticism that Samsung had gone too far. In 2016, Samsung made the biggest gamble in the history of South Korean corporate acquisitions. A staggering 9.4 trillion won. Yet, the company they bought with that massive sum was Harman, an American company known to most just as a speaker brand. The timing was absolutely terrible as well. It was a time when the Samsung brand was shaken by the Galaxy Note 7 incident and sentiment both inside and outside the company was cold. Normally, this would have been a moment to lie low and weather the storm. But Samsung did the exact opposite. With Samsung having failed at cars once before, the market's skepticism about them spending that much on a speaker company was natural. Why would Samsung, having folded its car business, insist on jumping back into the automotive world? In fact, Harman's operating profit plummeted to the 50 billion won range in the first year after the acquisition , making the move look like a failure. But people were missing one key thing. Samsung didn't buy a speaker company. They bought a master key to the automotive electronics market, opening doors to world-renowned brands like Mercedes, BMW, and Ferrari. How did Samsung, which was ousted from the auto industry 20 years ago, use that supposedly reckless 9 trillion won gamble to enter the inner sanctums of the world’s finest cars? We’ll break down that dramatic turnaround step by step right now. To truly understand the story, we have to start with Samsung’s old wound. In 1995, Samsung established Samsung Motors with the goal of building cars. They built a massive factory in Busan and even launched the SM5 in 1998. The cars weren't bad. In fact, they were well-regarded enough to eventually be called classic cars. The problem was the timing. The Asian financial crisis hit right then, and Samsung Motors, still in its infancy, couldn't withstand the wave. Ultimately, in 2000 , Samsung sold its auto business, into which it had poured massive funds, to Renault of France and stepped out of the industry. They made good products, but they couldn't stay in the market. To Samsung, the car industry became an old, painful wound. But Samsung hadn't completely abandoned that dream. Instead of whole cars, they decided to find answers in automotive components. Coincidentally, cars were becoming more and more like electronic devices. Large screens were being installed in dashboards, and every function in the car began to run on software. It was no longer an era where cars were judged only by their engines and wheels. An opportunity opened for Samsung to integrate its world-leading semiconductor, software, and display technologies into cars. Furthermore, Samsung was in desperate need of a new revenue stream. A structure relying solely on semiconductors was always precarious. Because if the semiconductor market hit a downturn, the entire company could be shaken. So, in 2015, Samsung formed a new automotive electronics team and knocked on the door of the auto industry once again. But this door didn't open easily either. Why didn't it open? It was because the automotive parts market has been an established world for over 100 years. Giants like Bosch and Continental of Germany had been working with car manufacturers for decades. It is like an old shop that has only served regulars for three generations. Furthermore, a car is a product that human lives depend on. To introduce a single new part, you must pass years of rigorous testing. No matter how good the technology is, it was nearly impossible for a newcomer to break into that long-standing trust overnight. Samsung was still a guest standing outside the door in front of the automotive industry. The key to overcoming this barrier all at once was a company called Harman. It was a company founded in 1953 by an audio engineer named Sidney Harman. You have likely seen products from this company at least once. JBL speakers, AKG headphones, Harman Kardon, and even the synonymous high-end audio brands Mark Levinson and Lexicon are all brands under Harman. So, most people just called Harman a company that makes good speakers. But for car companies, it was completely different. To them, Harman was not a speaker company, but a core partner entrusted with the vehicle's screens, sound, and electronic equipment. While we thought of JBL speakers, Mercedes and BMW were already embracing Harman as a key partner inside their cars. While the world called it an audio company, the world’s top luxury car brands were actually holding onto it for fear of losing it. In fact, it is known that four out of every ten car audio systems in the world use Harman's sound, making Harman a hidden giant that had already taken over the inside of the vehicle. Shall we look at an example? Mercedes uses Harman Kardon, Audi uses Bang& Olufsen, Rolls-Royce uses Lexicon, and Ferrari uses the sound of JBL. Essentially, this one company was responsible for the sound of world-renowned cars that we recognize just by hearing their names. The workforce reached 30,000, and they held thousands of patents in their hands. Above all, Harman had already built long-standing trust with companies like Mercedes, BMW, and Audi. The very relationship that Samsung had struggled to gain despite knocking on the door for so long. Now, it is the moment of decision. The year is 2016. This year was truly the worst of times for Samsung. Their ambitiously released Galaxy Note 7 began catching fire due to battery issues. It eventually escalated into an unprecedented situation where they had to recall millions of units sold worldwide and completely halt production. Two trillion won went up in smoke, and the name "Samsung" suffered a major blow. Normally, it would have been a time to lie low. It was a time to think about defense rather than new challenges. However, Samsung moved in the exact opposite direction. They were preparing for the acquisition of Harman without anyone knowing. This operation was top secret. Samsung strictly prevented information leaks by using the bare minimum number of necessary advisors. As a result, nobody in the market suspected this deal until the announcement was made. And finally, they quietly dropped the bombshell over the weekend when the stock market was closed. They announced they would buy Harman for 9 trillion won. It was an incredibly bold move to make in the middle of a crisis. $ 112 per share of Harman stock. That was a premium of nearly 30%over the market price at the time. It was the largest acquisition of a foreign company by a Korean firm in history. That record remains unbroken to this day. It was effectively the first major gamble directly overseen by Vice Chairman Lee Jae-yong right after he became a registered director at Samsung. Making a bet of this magnitude as his first move while the company was unstable was no different than burning his bridges. A short while later, Harman shareholders approved the deal with an overwhelming 95%vote, and it passed regulatory reviews in Europe, the U.S., and China one after another. However, the market was still in turmoil. While there was cheering for a brilliant move, there were also significant concerns. Wasn't a company in crisis spending too much money? Does Samsung, which had even given up on cars, truly understand the automotive parts market? Is it right to spend over 9 trillion won on a speaker company? The doubts did not easily subside. So, how did this gamble turn out? At first, it looked like the worries were justified. Once the announcement was made, rosy forecasts poured out from the securities industry. There were expectations that this single acquisition would accelerate Samsung's timeline to becoming a global leader in automotive electronics by 10 years. People thought that if Samsung's semiconductors supported Harman's impressive client list and technology, massive synergy would soon erupt. But here is the thing. The atmosphere turned completely strange right after the purchase. A perfectly healthy company that had been generating 9 trillion won in annual sales just before the acquisition saw its operating profit plummet to 57.4 billion won in 2017, its first year under Samsung. Compared to the previous year, that was a staggering drop of over 90%. Then, people started whispering. They asked if Samsung, knowing nothing about cars, had acted recklessly under pressure. They asked if it was a mistake where they paid too high a price. There was even talk that Lee Jae-yong’s first major gamble had gone wrong from the start. In reality, it certainly looked that way. As it turned out, Harman was a complex company with over 100 subsidiaries scattered across the globe. Immediately after the acquisition, Samsung had to pore over every single detail, restructure overlapping businesses, and divest non-core areas. The old American company and Korea’s Samsung had different ways of working, cultures, and languages. It took a long time just to get them to work in sync. Amidst those hiccups, performance struggled to recover. When COVID-19 swept the world in 2020, factories halted one by one, and Harman's operating profit slumped back to the 50 billion won range. Far from being a reliable breadwinner, it looked more like a heavy burden for a while. Talk even emerged that the nine-trillion-won gamble was turning into a bubble. Samsung's bet stood at the edge of its first cliff just like that. As time passed, the voices grew colder. At the time of the acquisition, Samsung said it would use Harman as a stepping stone to buy more car companies, but years passed without such news. Doubts grew about where the promised synergy actually was. The industry began calling Harman an unfinished, half-baked business. The applause from the day the acquisition was announced had turned into a chilly assessment of "half-success." But this is where Samsung's choice was different . Usually, when a big company is acquired, the existing management is replaced with one's own people. However , Samsung kept Dinesh Paliwal, the CEO who led Harman, in his position. They did not shake up Harman's technology, people, or long-standing client relationships. Because if the proud talent turned their backs, the valuable relationships bought for nine trillion won could have collapsed together. I mentioned this earlier. Samsung didn't buy Harman's speakers; they bought the master key that would open doors to Mercedes-Benz and BMW. The true value of that key was not in the brand or technology, but in the people and relationships Harman had built over decades. So, there was no reason to shake it up and ruin it. Even during those years when performance hit rock bottom, Samsung gave Harman time to settle in rather than pressuring it impatiently. If they had changed course when the mockery poured in, the Harman of today might not exist. Samsung pushed forward in one direction, quietly but firmly. That was automotive electronics. No matter how dismal the immediate performance was, they kept betting on the future of automotive electronic equipment. Samsung did not miss the fact that the massive waves of electric vehicles and autonomous driving were coming. Those years that people called a failure were actually a silent period of preparing for an explosion. While everyone else was turning their backs, only Samsung believed that the tide would soon turn. And finally, that silence is broken. Automotive electronics was the answer. What Samsung was really after at Harman wasn't audio, but the seat at the table in the automotive industry that Harman had built for decades. They used Harman as a key to open the door at once, a door that couldn't be opened even after knocking for a long time with semiconductors alone. Then, Samsung's technology began to be layered onto Harman's position. Harman's automotive communication equipment, combined with Samsung's 5G technology, has been integrated into BMW's electric vehicles . The dawn of the electric vehicle era was also completely transforming car interiors. As engines disappeared and spaces grew, cars began to turn into small living rooms. Digital cockpits, which fill those spaces with screens, sound, connectivity, and software, became Harman's primary weapon. This market was a golden opportunity poised for rapid growth. Beyond Mercedes and BMW, top-tier brands like Toyota, Lexus , and Cadillac added their names to Harman’s client list. In 2023, they even joined hands with the notoriously exclusive Ferrari. The situation had completely reversed. Automakers that wouldn't open their doors to Harman 20 years ago now seek them out first when designing new cars. It is because an era has arrived where a car cannot be called premium without high-quality sound and a smart display. The guest who was once turned away has become the prized partner everyone wants to have. And this transformation is proven by the numbers. Revenue, which was around 7 trillion won right after the acquisition, surpassed 10 trillion won in 2019. Operating profit, which stood at 57.4 billion won, exceeded 1 trillion won for the first time in 2023 . Then, in 2025, they finally set an all-time record of 15 trillion won in revenue and 1.5 trillion won in operating profit. Compared to the start , operating profit has jumped more than 26 times. What is even more surprising is that most of this money comes from automotive electronics, not just audio. Harman has reached the global top tier in both digital cockpits and car audio, known as the two pillars of a vehicle. Profit margins have also reached nearly 10 percent. Essentially, the company bought for 9 trillion won is now generating 1.5 trillion won in profit every year. If this trend continues, recouping the entire acquisition cost is just a matter of time. The 9 trillion won once labeled an expensive mistake turned out to be an admission ticket to the future. That money, once mocked, has become a weapon that brings in over 1 trillion won in profit annually. The gamble once called reckless is finally being proven right, one number at a time. However, Harman had another side hidden away. The company that once created sound is now beginning to covet the eyes of the car as well. This is none other than autonomous driving. At the end of 2025, Harman announced it would acquire the Advanced Driver Assistance System (ADAS ) division of Germany's ZF. The deal was valued at approximately 2.6 trillion won. The key is that this division held world-class technology in smart cameras, which allow cars to see ahead and detect danger. Once the deal is finalized, about 3,700 personnel specializing in smart camera and radar technology will join Harman. In simple terms, Harman is now moving beyond in-car audio and screens to enter the realm of helping vehicles see ahead and detect danger. Harman has even acquired the audio business division of Masimo. With this acquisition, they have embraced top-tier brands like Bowers& Wilkins, Denon, and Marantz. They have completed their sound portfolio from the mass-market JBL to the most premium high-end brands. When you add Samsung’s semiconductors, automotive chips, and display technology to this, the picture becomes even bigger. We are in an era where cars are transforming from simple modes of transportation into smartphones on wheels. Within that space, Harman is managing sound, screens, connectivity, and sensing technology all at once. As cars that drive themselves while monitoring the road ahead approach, Harman stands one step ahead of everyone else at the door of that massive market. Samsung, which was lingering outside the car industry 20 years ago, now holds the key to opening that door. Of course, they cannot just sit back and relax. The automotive electronics market is currently a fiercely contested battlefield for the entire world. Between the traditional European powerhouses and latecomers armed with low-cost, high-volume products, the price pressure is intense. Furthermore, this business is structured to thrive when the vehicle market is strong, but suffer when cars aren't selling. This means there are clearly still hurdles to overcome. Therefore, it is not a time to jump to the conclusion that the battle is already won. Even so, one thing is certain. The outsider who was pushed out 20 years ago has now become an unrivaled entity that prestigious car brands are eager to partner with. Harman is not just an audio company; it became the definitive gateway for Samsung to enter the world of luxury automobiles, and the 9-trillion-won bet once called reckless has become a weapon generating over 1 trillion won in profit every year. Starting where others said it wouldn't work, and eventually turning the tables. Samsung has once again proven the old formula that our companies have followed throughout their history. A painful failure from the past has returned 20 years later as an invaluable asset. Samsung’s challenge in the automotive industry was never truly over.

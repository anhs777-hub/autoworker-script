Thousands of state-of-the-art combines worth hundreds of millions of won. Next to them, a 50,000-won pedal thresher made with bicycle chains. Common sense dictates that the machine with a computer should win. But in the middle of Kenya, Africa, a sight hard to believe unfolds. South Korea, with world-class agricultural technology, flew in an old thresher that looked like it belonged in a junkyard. Yet, the local farmers who saw it cheered and applauded, calling it advanced technology. Why were they so enthusiastic about our relic, setting aside the expensive machinery from developed nations? I will now reveal the cold calculations and wisdom hidden behind it. It was a field about 25km northwest of Nairobi, the capital of Kenya. It was a place where red dust stirred in the wind. But the landscape on one side of the field was a bit strange. Large, bulky foreign agricultural machines were lined up. These were machines that must have once sparkled. The problem was that they were all at a standstill. The wheels were covered in thick, red rust. Some were abandoned with parts missing. The windows were cracked. Weeds were growing up through the seats. Some say this place was called an agricultural machinery graveyard. These were machines worth hundreds of millions of won each. Why were they lying there like that? The reason was absurdly simple. A small part broke, and there was nowhere nearby to replace it. There were no repair technicians or parts shops. What happens if you order a part ? It has to be shipped from the home country by sea. It takes several months . By then, the farming season is long gone. Eventually, perfectly good machines come to a halt. Hundreds of millions of won effectively became trash because of one button. Right next to them, a Korean research team quietly unpacked their gear. What they brought out was not a new machine. They are said to have quickly built a pedal-operated thresher using a bicycle pedal and a discarded oil drum. At first, the farmers were indifferent. They probably wondered what could be done with that junk. But then, the researcher began to pedal. The drum spun rapidly. He gently held a sheaf of rice against it. >> The grains poured out in a rush. It happened in an instant. Originally, this work had to be done by human hands . They had to strip the grain from the stalks one by one. Bending their backs, all day long. Even with the whole family helping, it was slow work. That was finished in just a few minutes. The eyes of the watching farmers widened. It was understandable. But the true power of this machine was not its speed . It lay elsewhere. It didn't need electricity. It didn't use a single drop of oil. There were no complex computer chips. Then what happens if it breaks? You just take it to a neighborhood bicycle shop. A bit of iron, a few hammer blows, and that was it—that was the key point. To the farmers, this was not just a machine. It was technology they could handle with their own hands. It is said that some were even moved to tears. Expensive machines were just for show, but this rugged machine was theirs. Now , however, a question remains for all of you. Was this just a single, lucky, heartwarming story? That is not the case. Behind this scene lay a meticulous plan designed by the state. Let's start by identifying the name of that plan. KOPIA. It is written as K-O-P-I-A in English. It is an overseas agricultural technology development project run by our Rural Development Administration. Its official launch was in 2009. It took its first steps in six countries across Asia and Africa. The beginning was very modest. The initial budget was about 2.1 million US dollars —a very small scale for a national project. The number of experts dispatched was also just a handful. Yet , this small project steadily took root . The Kenya Center is a prime example. It was established on the outskirts of Nairobi. In July 2013, Director Kim Choong-hoe took office. He was an expert with a doctorate in agriculture. He practically lived on-site to impart his technology. They expanded items one by one, from potatoes and poultry to rice. Let's add some data from international organizations here. These are statistics from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). Rice yield in Africa is about 2.4 tons per hectare. That is less than half the level of Asia. It was not because the land was bad. On the contrary, African soil is quite fertile. The problem was technology and the system. At the time, the Rural Development Administration explained it this way. That they would contribute to solving Africa's food problems by sharing our Green Revolution technology? It means it was not just a simple act of charity. There was a clear goal in mind. Why was this such a desperate issue? The answer also lies in the numbers. Rice demand in Africa was growing by about 6%each year . The population was exploding, and their tastes were shifting toward rice. But production could not keep up with demand. All the rice in short supply was filled by imports. Shall we look at the US Department of Agriculture's estimates? Rice imports by major African consuming nations are about 19 million tons annually. In terms of value, it reached 6 to 9 billion dollars. In our currency, that is around 10 trillion won every year. That much was poured solely into buying rice . It was truly like pouring water into a bottomless jar. The land was fertile, yet money was leaking outward. This very cycle of viciousness. South Korea intended to break this link. But, everyone, this is only the picture revealed on the surface. The real story begins now. There were three reasons why Korea insisted on taking old threshing machines, which developed nations had completely overlooked. The first is the mismatch in speed. The second is the hidden trap of free aid. And the third is the most unexpected secret of alignment. If you don't know these three, you will miss the true essence of today's story. In particular , you must watch until the end for the third point to truly hit home. Now, let’s break them down slowly, one by one. Let’s start with the first one. The first reason is the mismatch in speed. What do I mean by that? In technology, there are actually two kinds of speeds. One is the speed at which machines advance, and the other is the speed of the ground that supports them. Technology thrives only when these two are in sync. If they are out of sync, even the best machine will die. Western cutting-edge machinery was dazzlingly fast. It pinpointed locations via satellite. Sensors read the soil condition, and fields were plowed at the push of a button. They were literally items from the future. But what was the land like where these machines landed? Paved roads were rare. When it rained, the roads turned into mud pits. Gas stations were dozens of kilometers away. Electricity cut out several times a day. There was absolutely no one to fix broken sensors . The machine came from the future, but the land wasn't ready yet. This mismatch was the real culprit that turned millions of dollars of equipment into scrap metal. Does it sound a bit abstract just hearing it? Let me paint a simple picture for you. Here is the world’s most expensive sports car. A flashy car worth hundreds of thousands of dollars. Just hearing the engine roar makes your heart race. Where shall we place this car? On a dusty, sloping dirt road in the countryside. Now, step on the gas. What will happen? The low-slung chassis will scrape against rocks. The undercarriage will bang and clang. It probably won’t even make it a meter before it stops. It’s not because the performance is bad. Because that car was born for smooth roads. It just didn't match the terrain. Now, let’s look to the side. There is an old, simple tractor. It’s slow, clunky, and unimpressive. But it climbs the same slope without a struggle. Even while carrying a heavy load. Rattling along, it eventually reaches the top. Now, let me ask you a question. On this slope, which is the better technology? The answer is determined by the land, not the price tag. On a smooth highway, the sports car is king. But on a bumpy slope, the tractor is king. The superiority of technology was not determined by a high price. It was determined by the ground it stood on. The fields in Africa were not a sports car's highway. They were a tractor's slope. So, that machine worth hundreds of thousands of dollars is not at fault . The Western companies that made them had no ill intentions. They simply designed the machines for their own well-maintained farms. In their lands, that was the clear and correct answer. It’s just that in front of a different land called Africa, that answer became wrong. It wasn’t that the other side was foolish. It was that the land was different. However, Korea didn’t learn this logic through books . We knew it through experience. Why, you ask? Because just a generation ago, we were on that very same slope. It was a land where everything had collapsed due to war. It was a time when we lacked proper machines, roads, and electricity. We stepped up from that bottom, one rung at a time. We planted rice by hand and watered it with our feet. That is how we prepared the ground first. Only then did we place the machines on top. There was an order to it. That is why we knew. That without preparing the ground, even the best machine would come to a halt. This was wisdom not found in books. It was wisdom that could only be learned with dirt-covered hands. Korea transplanted that very wisdom into the fields of Kenya. Technology that skips the order becomes scrap metal. But technology built from the ground up will eventually survive. This single sentence is the key that pierces through today's entire story. Now, let’s move on to the second reason. The hidden trap of free aid. What on earth could this be? Someone surely must have sent the machines out of good will. How did that good will return as a trap? And how did Korea manage to avoid that snare? I will explain that right away. This is the second reason I promised. The hidden trap of free aid. It’s a slightly chilling story. Surely, someone must have sent the machines out of good will. But how did that good will turn into a trap? Here, we must take another look at the word " free." Is there really such a thing as "free" in this world? A shiny machine arrived in a poor country. The recipient was naturally grateful. But that machine came with a tag attached. It was a tag labeled "parts." The machine was given for free. But the parts were not free. Every single screw , every single belt. They all had to be bought from the country that made the machine. Parts from other companies wouldn't even fit. Because the specifications were all different. Do you understand what that means? The moment they received the machine, dependency began. A structure where they had to buy parts from that company forever. We call that " capital-dependent aid." For the company providing it, it was no loss. It was, in fact, a profitable business. They got to dispose of their surplus stock. And on top of that, they secured a market for their parts. It wore the face of good will, but inside, it was a calculated scheme. I will not call them greedy. Companies are designed to seek profit. That is their nature. However, what was it like for the recipient countries? The moment the machine stopped, they could do nothing. They would order parts and wait for months. Meanwhile, their crops would be ruined. A machine received for free had become their most expensive burden. The Korean approach was the exact opposite. We did not just give them finished products. We taught them how to repair and maintain them. Technology that could be patched at a bike shop or forged at a blacksmith’s. If you had some scrap metal, you could replicate it locally. There was no need to order parts from across the ocean. Because everything could be handled in the neighborhood. This is called circular economy-based appropriate technology. Money doesn't leak out but circulates within the village. It was a method of planting self-reliance, not hindering it. This very difference changed everything. You really have to see it visually to understand. Let’s use clothing as an analogy. Imagine a very expensive designer suit here. It’s a famous foreign brand product. But one day, a button falls off. Now, how do you sew this button on? You can’t just use any button. It has to be a button specific to that brand. Eventually, you send the suit back to the headquarters. The repair cost is as much as the fabric itself. And how many weeks does it take to get back? All because of a single button. Western-style aid was just like that designer suit. It’s stylish, but it’s a garment I can’t even touch myself. Now, let’s look at a different garment. It’s a simple piece of clothing bought at a local market. A button fell off here too. What do I do? I open my sewing kit. I thread the needle. A few stitches and it's done. You can use any similar button to replace it. It costs almost no money or time. Korean-style technology was exactly like this garment. It may not be flashy, but it’s something I can maintain myself. This difference becomes clearer when converted into your wallet. Think of a combine harvester worth hundreds of millions of won. It costs money every year to keep it running. Fuel costs, parts, labor for maintenance—annual upkeep is no joke. What if it breaks down? Repair costs skyrocket. But what about a bicycle-pedal thresher? Zero for fuel, zero for electricity, and parts cost as much as local scrap metal . Even after a year of use, maintenance costs are virtually zero. Now, place the two side by side. One stands still and gathers rust. The other keeps running even after years pass. Which one is the real asset to a poor farmer? The answer is very clear. An expensive price tag was not what made it an asset . Something that works by my side until the end—that is an asset. Now, for the third reason. It is the highlight of today. This is the most unexpected and weightiest story of all. Once you hear this, you’ll probably slap your knee in surprise. Another item that Korea brought to Kenya. It wasn't a machine. It was just a handful of string. Have you heard of a planting line? It’s a string used when planting rice seedlings. It’s a string marked at regular intervals. You stretch this line across the rice paddy . Then you plant the seedlings according to those marks. That’s how the rice stays in line. It is said that Korea passed on how to use this planting line. Now, here is a question. With so many modern machines available, why specifically the method of planting in lines? In this lies the deepest secret of agriculture. Let's say you plant seedlings just anywhere. Here and there, wherever your hands go. At first , it doesn't look like much of a difference. But problems start to erupt after a month. First, you cannot weed the field. It's all a jumble, so you can't tell where the weeds are. Second, you cannot create water channels. Without lines, you can't dig furrows. Third, you cannot count the yield. Because you don't know how many clusters were planted. You can't even plan for the next farming season. On the other hand, what happens if you plant in lines? People can walk between the rows. They can easily pull out the weeds. Water flows through those spaces . You can count the clusters and predict the harvest. Order is created. This is it. Aligning the rows wasn't just about tidiness. It was the first step in turning agriculture into a science. But doesn't this scene look somewhat familiar? That's right. It was our own past. This is a rice paddy in 1970s South Korea. We were exactly the same back then. You likely remember the term "barley hump." It was a time when we went hungry every spring due to a shortage of rice. That was when the Tongil rice variety emerged. It was a variety that greatly increased crop yields. But the variety alone wasn't enough. The planting method had to change as well. So, the whole country planted in lines. Rural extension workers went around the paddies with planting lines. They adjusted the spacing for every single cluster. Those layers of sweat helped us overcome the barley hump. We finally became self-sufficient in rice. What Korea passed on to Kenya was this very sequence of hard work. Not machines, but the wisdom we learned from the ground up—that’s what we handed over. Technology that skips the basics becomes scrap metal. But wisdom built from the ground up eventually becomes history. However, everyone, we shouldn't just applaud and end it here. We must look at the reality coldly. This path has by no means been smooth. First, the pursuit is fierce. Regarding the African agricultural machinery market. Chinese companies are launching an aggressive offensive with massive volume. They move in quickly with cheap tractors and farm tools. They have already deeply penetrated the fields in Kenya. Second, the climate is unstable. Droughts and floods strike in turns. They wipe out hard-earned crops overnight. Third, distribution networks are still poor. Even if you manage to harvest, you can't sell it without roads. Fourth, seed sovereignty is under threat. Massive multinational grain corporations are eyeing the seed market. Fifth, it costs a lot of money. To lay down all this infrastructure, it takes a long time and enormous costs. So, this isn't a finished game yet. We are barely at the stage of sowing seeds . Nevertheless, that small seed has finally sprouted. In 2009, there was one thresher, but that wasn't the end. Over the past 17 years, the scale has grown. Now it is time for you to hear the name K-Rice Belt. It is a project where the MAFRA and the RDA joined forces. >> It's a big picture to produce and supply rice seeds to African nations lacking rice. The experience of KOPIA became the foundation for it. Results are proven by numbers. In the first year, 2023, we conducted pilot production in six countries. The amount harvested was 2,321 tons. It was a performance that exceeded the goal by 14%. The following year, it jumped to 6,365 tons. And in May 2026, the MAFRA makes an announcement. That the cumulative production surpassed 10,000 tons in just three years. The number of participating countries has also increased. It expanded through seven countries, reaching eight including Sierra Leone. What is more important is quality. Shall we look at the productivity of the K-Rice Belt? It is 4.6 tons per hectare. This figure is more than double the local traditional farming method. I mentioned earlier that the African average is 2.4 tons. It has surpassed that by a wide margin. The land is the same, but the yield has doubled. Once the order was corrected, the land responded. The government’s goal does not stop here. From 2027, we plan to produce 10,000 tons annually. Through this, we aim to stably supply rice to 30 million African residents. At this point, Africa is reaching out its hand first. It is said that a high-ranking official from Côte d'Ivoire said this. That Korea, having achieved rice self-sufficiency, is their role model. It was a country that once received aid. That country is now called a role model. Could there be a greater turnaround than this? But everyone, what does this story of distant Africa have to do with our lives? It has a very significant connection. It’s connected to our wallets, as the global grain market is interconnected. If there is a poor harvest in one place, grain prices fluctuate around the world. What will happen if Africa becomes self-sufficient in rice? The pressure on the international grain market eases . That stability comes full circle to reach our dining tables. It acts as a seawall protecting our food prices at home. A single stalk of rice on the other side of the globe is connected to my grocery bill. It may seem like a grand topic, but it was ultimately about my own wallet. However, I don't want to end this story with numbers. Because the real heroes of this achievement are someone else. It is not about flashy diplomatic rhetoric. Nor is it about some dazzling technology. It was the knees of those who crawled on the red dirt ground. It was the hands that stepped on pedals under the scorching sun. And if we look further back, we find the cold, snowy fields of the 1970s. People who bent their waists holding rice lines, those who planted rice until their hands and feet were cracked. It was our parents 'generation . The sweat they shed accumulated and became technology. That technology crossed the ocean and saved Africa. The rice growing in Kenyan paddies today. The sweat of our parents' generation is soaked into its roots. Technology was not something that only came from factories. It emerged from the labor that silently trod the earth. Now, I will summarize today's story in three points. First, what matters more than flashy machines is the level of the ground. Second, technology is not about skipping steps, but about building them up. Third, only those who have walked the earth know the way. And please remember just this one sentence from today. Technology that skips steps becomes scrap metal, but wisdom built from the ground up becomes history. While multi-million dollar machines rust away, 50,000-won wisdom fed an entire continent.

The number one city for study abroad chosen by university students worldwide . For the past six years, that spot was held by London, UK. Paris, New York, Tokyo, and Sydney challenged it every year, but they all ended up failing. Now, here's the thing. Last year, a city emerged that snatched that number one spot. It is none other than Seoul, South Korea. And this year, Seoul succeeded in defending its number one position for two consecutive years, being the only one out of 150 cities to receive a perfect score of 100. Now, when you first hear this, your reaction is usually like this. "Seoul, where life is tough, is number one?" But if you look at the numbers, it might start to make sense. There are currently over 300,000 international students enrolled in Korean universities. Why on earth did students from all over the world choose Seoul out of so many cities? And what’s even more curious is London. Why exactly did the city that was number one for six years give up its spot? Surprisingly, it wasn't the schools that held London back. Today, let's explore these two questions one by one. I mentioned a perfect score of 100, right? But if you look at the numbers right below, you can see how fiercely contested that perfect score is. Second place Tokyo had 98 points, and third place London had 97.3 points. It was 97.3 points. The gap between first and third place is a mere 2.7 points, with Tokyo squeezed into that narrow space. It’s a distance where one wrong step could flip the rankings. Shall we look at the rest of the leaderboard? Fourth place is Melbourne, Australia, and joint fifth are Munich, Germany and Sydney, Australia. The city of romance, Paris, is 7th, Berlin is 8th, Vienna is 9th, and Zurich, Switzerland is 10th. New York has dropped to joint 23rd place. New York, the capital of the global economy, is more than 20 spots below Seoul. Tokyo, London, and Paris have all lined up behind Seoul. The organization that issued the report gave its announcement the following subtitle. "Seoul holds onto first place, Asia shines, and Europe shows mixed results." Since the 1st and 2nd place cities are both in Asia, saying they "shined" is no empty praise. But what kind of test is this report card, anyway? It was produced by QS, a British higher education assessment agency. It’s the very same place famous for its world university rankings. But this report card isn't grading a single university. This time, it put an entire city to the test to evaluate how good it is for university students to live and study in. There are a total of six subjects. University rankings, student mix, desirability, employer activity, affordability, and student voice. Like a school report card, there are separate rankings for each subject. So, this is a test that evaluates not just the schools, but also post-graduation jobs and cost of living all at once. Furthermore, even entering the testing site is very strict. A city must have a population over 250,000, at least two top-ranked universities, and a moderate or lower international safety risk rating to qualify. Simply put, it’s like an amusement park ride with a height requirement. If you don’t meet the criteria, you can’t even get in line. There were 150 cities that made it into this strict testing site, and among them, Seoul was the only one to get a perfect score. Oh, there is one more welcome name at the bottom of this table. Daejeon has entered this ranking for the first time at 67th place. I will call this name again at the end of the story. Now, then, whose was this top spot originally? In boxing, the strap a champion wears around their waist is called a championship belt. The number one spot on this ranking is exactly that belt. At first, the 8 th-ranked city held this belt for four consecutive years. It was a start worthy of a city of romance. In the 2017 edition, Montreal, Canada, took the belt. It was the first city to stop the 8 th-ranked city’s four-year winning streak. And in the 2018 edition , London took first place for the first time. Since then, London hasn’t missed a beat with the belt for six years. As a city packed with famous universities, it was truly an ironclad champion. The city that first snatched that belt away last year is Seoul. An Asian city brought down a six-year veteran champion. At this point, London’s pride must be a bit wounded. But in 2018, when London first wore the belt, what rank was Seoul? It was 10th. And that was actually a drop of six spots from its previous position of 4th . In just over seven years, Seoul climbed nine spots to become the champion and successfully defended its title with a perfect score this year. They say it’s harder to defend a belt than it is to win it. Seoul has gone from challenger to champion and successfully defended the title. But it’s not just the ranking numbers that have risen. I mentioned 300,000 people earlier. The exact figures are as follows. As of April 1, 2026, the number of foreign students enrolled in Korean universities reached a total of 307,464. Three years ago, it was 181,842. In just three years, an increase of 125,622, or 69.1%, has occurred. On a daily basis, that means an average of 115 new foreign students have been added every single day. It’s like enough people to fill two or three university lecture halls have arrived every single day without fail. Compared to just one year ago, it has increased by 21.3%. It’s as if the space for five people has expanded to six in just one year. What’s more worth noting is what these students came here to do. There are 223,191 degree-seeking students, meaning three out of four came for a diploma. This means it’s not just a passing trend of brief language studies. The government had set a goal to welcome 300,000 students by 2027. It’s called Study Korea 300K, where 'K' stands for a thousand, so 300K is 300,000. They’ve surpassed that goal a full year ahead of schedule. But here is the thing. Fifteen years ago, the picture was completely the opposite. In 2011, there were 26,465 of our students at overseas universities, the highest number at the time. There were 89,537 foreign students who came to study in Korea. We were a country that only brought one in for every three we sent out. Do you remember the term "goose father"? It was when kids and mothers were sent to schools overseas while fathers stayed behind in Korea to pay for tuition and living expenses. In 2007 alone, 5 billion dollars left the country for study and training abroad. That’s an amount 5.2 times higher than in the year 2000. That same year, the money coming in was only 46.5 million dollars, less than 1%of what went out. For every 100 dollars sent out, we weren't even getting back one dollar. That was what we looked like less than 20 years ago. In 15 years, that arrow has completely reversed. In 2025, our students abroad dropped to 129,726, cut in half. It’s less than half the number from its peak. That same year, foreign students coming in reached 253,434, nearly double those going out. Compared to the 89,537 from 15 years ago, the number has nearly tripled. A country that sent three and received one now receives two for every one it sends. Isn't that a bit goosebump-inducing? We once stood in line to send our kids across the sea, but now students worldwide line up before our universities. So then, what exactly drew all these students to Seoul? Everyone says it’s thanks to K-pop. But is that really the whole story? You have to understand the logic behind why students chose this city to truly see the picture. Saying it’s because of K-pop isn’t wrong. There are indeed many students who became curious about this country after watching K-dramas and listening to Korean songs. Some might guess it’s because there are many students from nearby countries. This is also true. Among international students in Korea this year, 96,834 are from Vietnam. At 31.5%, they have surpassed China for the first time. Chinese students are at 78,890, or 25.7%, placing them second. China, which held the top spot for a long time, has given up its place. That means about one out of every three international students is from Vietnam. In 2009, 78%of degree-seeking international students were from China, so the map of their countries of origin has changed significantly. This also means that studying in Korea has become a common option for students in neighboring countries. But when we asked the international students themselves why they chose Korea, the answers came from a completely different direction. There is a government survey called the Immigration Status and Employment Survey, conducted jointly by the Ministry of Justice and the National Statistical Office. It is a survey that directly asks foreigners aged 15 and older who have lived in Korea for more than 91 days. The survey was based on data from May 2024, and the results were announced last December. Rankings are scores given by evaluation agencies , but this carries more weight because the students answered in their own words. So, what did the students say? The number one reason for studying in Korea was the excellent curriculum, at 34%. Second place was that their major matched their interests at 20.5%, and third was that a Korean degree helps with employment at 10.1%. If you gather 100 people and ask them, it’s like 34 , 21, and 10 people raising their hands in turn. Adding up just the top three reasons accounts for 64.6%, which is more than six out of ten people. The term "curriculum" might sound a bit rigid to you. Simply put, it’s the structure of the classes. It’s about what is taught, in what order, and how deeply it is covered. Think of the scene where you choose a restaurant for dinner. If the sign is pretty and there’s a rumor that it was on TV, you at least go up to the door. But what actually makes you sit down is the menu. You go in after checking if they have the food you want and if they serve it well. If K-pop is the sign and the rumors, then the curriculum is exactly that menu. No matter how flashy the sign is, if the menu is poor, customers will turn away at the door. In fact, the top three reasons were all about study and degrees. Neither K-pop nor K-dramas were anywhere in those top three slots. Everyone firmly believed it was because of K-pop, but the students themselves said they came to study. Song Jun-haeng, head of the employment statistics division at the National Statistical Office, also cited policy efforts by the government, local municipalities, and universities as the reason for the increase in international students. He also added that interest in Korean culture played a role as well. It’s true that K-pop drew attention to the country of Korea, but that wasn't the only reason they packed their bags. Then what did the evaluation agency’s report card say? In this year's report card, Seoul ranked 1st in the university category and 2nd in the job market category. Tokyo was ranked 1st for jobs. Then, what does each of these two categories look at? The university category looks at how many universities there are in the city and how high their standards are. The job category looks at how much companies value graduates from that city and whether it's a good place for young people to find work. QS also pointed to these two categories as the reason Seoul maintained its number one spot for two consecutive years. The students 'answers and the evaluation agency's report card are pointing in exactly the same direction. Education, and the opportunities after graduation. It is as if the students gave the same answer with their own voices, and the agency gave it with its scores. Oh, and in that same report card, the preference category was ranked 10th. Please keep this number 10 in mind for a moment. We will bring it up again a little later. Shall we talk about diplomas as well? In this year's QS World University Rankings, Seoul National University was 38th, Yonsei University 42nd, and Korea University 52nd. All three schools made it into the top 100 globally, and for Yonsei and Korea University, it's their highest ranking ever. The number of Korean universities on the list reached 43, surpassing Japan for the first time . It means that diplomas recognized anywhere in the world are concentrated in one city. Simply put, it's like having three famous restaurants side by side in the same alley. Whether they return to their hometowns or go to another country, just the name of these schools fills the first line of their resumes. That is why things like this happen. An international student I met in a broadcast interview said that while struggling to decide between the so-called SKY universities, they chose Korea University simply because they liked the color red. It sounds like a joke, but looking at it differently, it means they had enough options to choose a world-class school based on color. By now, it feels like we have the answer, right? It's because the universities are good. But if it's just because the universities are good, there is something strange. In 2018, Seoul was already ranked 3rd in the world in the university category. Then why was it 10th overall? No matter how good the universities are, for a student to pack their bags, two doors must be open. The door for coming in, and the door for going out. If the entrance is narrow or the exit is blocked, students won't buy a plane ticket. In Seoul, both of these doors were open. The key point is that it's useless if only one door is open. Let's look at the entrance door first. Students dreaming of studying abroad first run the numbers on two things. The price of the flight and their monthly living expenses. If these two issues aren't resolved, they can't go no matter how much they want to. The Korean government's Global Korea Scholarship, or GKS for short, makes them put those calculators down. It covers full tuition, round-trip airfare , monthly living costs, and even a settlement allowance. Simply put, it means all they have to do is show up. This scholarship program started in 1967 and has selected 21,406 students from 161 countries to date. This year alone, about 1,900 new students are being selected. This scholarship program, which has continued for nearly 60 years, has left graduates who passed through Korea all around the world. When the government first launched " Study Korea" in 2004, there were only about 16,000 foreign students in Korea. That number is now 307,464. It has increased nearly 20-fold in just over 20 years. It’s jaw-dropping, isn’t it? Then what about the exit door? The answer lies in the report card we saw earlier. It ranked 2nd in the world for jobs, and 10.1%said a Korean degree helps with employment. The fact that companies recognize graduates from that city means that when you walk into an interview with your diploma, that school's name carries weight. It’s a structure that takes care of both the day they arrive at the airport and the day they receive their diploma. So, the Korean Wave made them knock on the door , but it was the scholarships and diplomas that actually opened it. This is exactly half of the story. The road ahead contains stories of life beyond studies, and the reason why London, the six-year champion, surrendered its belt . Another international student I met in a broadcast interview said that when she first came to Korea, she packed pepper spray with her mother. It must have been the heart of a parent sending their child to a strange country. But after living here, she said she never once had to take that spray out. The spray, tucked deep inside her bag, just ended up sleeping there. Even after late-night studies, there were 24-hour restaurants with the lights on, and her palate, which started with kimchi stew, fell in love with jajangmyeon. She also didn't forget to mention that the convenience stores are really great. A neighborhood where you don't have to worry about going out to buy a cup of ramen in the middle of the night. For us, it's just daily life. She also mentioned that because there are so many mountains in Seoul, she ended up going hiking more often than she thought. It means a classroom by day, lit alleys by night, and mountains on weekends are all within one city. A city where you don't have to worry about walking alone at night or dawn. It's a night walk so familiar to us that we didn't even know it was a charm . This is also confirmed by the numbers . In the 2026 Safety Index by Numbeo, which aggregates citizens' perceptions of safety, Seoul scored 74.6 points, ranking it among the top 12 major global cities. Tokyo scored 75.9, and there were no European cities in the top ten. When it comes to night safety, Seoul has effectively surpassed London, which ranked 8th. Now, it's time to bring out the number you were asked to remember earlier. 10th place in preference. What on earth is this category looking at? This category looks at safety, corruption, healthcare , climate, and pollution. Try to picture the scene of choosing a neighborhood to move to. You check things like whether the streets are safe at night, if the hospital is close , and if the air is okay. You also subtly care about whether public offices work transparently. Even with a great school nearby, if the walk home at night feels unsafe, you'll hesitate to move. That preference is exactly the scorecard for a neighborhood you'd move to. It’s not school grades, but neighborhood grades. But was this neighborhood score always this good for Seoul? That wasn't the case. Back in 2018, looking at Seoul’s subject scores, it ranked 3rd in universities and 4th in jobs, keeping it in the top tier even then. It was held back by just one subject. It ranked 38th in preference. The score for "a city you want to live in" was stuck at 38th place. In the 2026 rankings, that score jumped 31 spots to 7th place with 90.2 points. And it maintained its top-10 spot again this year. Since other subjects were already top-tier, that one subject jumping 31 places naturally boosted the overall score significantly . In other words, the final engine that brought the belt home wasn't the "city for study" score, but the "city you want to live in" score. A city ranked first for universities is now also among the world's top ten places to live. Isn't that truly amazing? Schools to study in, gateways for entry and exit, and a city to live in. These are Seoul’s three pillars. If the story ended here, it would have just been a pleasant piece of ranking news. But the real story starts right now. Just how expensive is it, anyway? The average rent for student-only housing in London for the 2024-2025 academic year is 13,595 pounds per year, or 295 pounds per week . However, the maximum living cost loan a British student can receive is 13,348 pounds. The rent is more expensive than the loan limit. It means that even if you pour in all the money the government lends you, it’s not enough to cover the rent. You're already in the red on rent before you even buy a single meal.

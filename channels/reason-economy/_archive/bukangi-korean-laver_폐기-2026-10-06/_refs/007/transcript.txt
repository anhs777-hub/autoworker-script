Until a few months ago, I was afraid to pay for this. Back in January, the price of ten sheets of dried laver hit an all-time high of 1,512 won. But in just eight months, the situation has completely flipped. At the end of last month, the wholesale price dropped 24% in just a week. In just one week. Prices don't usually fluctuate like this. But laver has been like this for several years now. It rises for years, then suddenly drops, only to rise again . You’re probably all thinking the same thing. "I guess production just went up." I thought so at first, too. But when I traced the reason, it led to a completely unexpected country. The price of the common laver we eat every day is being swayed by what's happening in Japan. What on earth happened in Japan to make the price of Korean laver fluctuate this much? Before we begin, subscribing and liking really helps us make this kind of content. Let’s jump right in. Those of you who have visited a supermarket lately know what I mean. Seasoned laver used to be affordable with just 10,000 won. Now, your hands shake a little at the price. Back in 2021, it was 899 won for ten sheets; it’s up nearly 70%since then. Gimbap shop owners are in the same boat. I’m sure you’ve heard about the rising burden of material costs. This raises one question. Why laver, of all things? Rice and beef go up too, but not as dramatically as laver. Raw laver. If you look at the raw harvest price, it averages 1,157 won per kg, but it has peaked at 4,591 won. It’s swinging by more than four times the amount. Laver rises for years, then suddenly drops at some point, and starts rising again. Almost as if someone is manipulating it . It’s hard to just dismiss it as " that’s how inflation is"—the pattern is too strange. Let’s start with the reason everyone is already guessing. Laver is a huge hit overseas these days. Last year’s laver exports reached $ 1.13 billion. That’s over 1.5 trillion won in our currency. It’s an all-time high. It’s the number one exported seafood product in Korea. Not tuna, not, but laver . Frozen gimbap is being exported and displayed in major US supermarkets; its popularity overseas is incredible. At this point, anyone would think the same thing. "Since so much of ours is going overseas, domestic supply is down, causing the price to rise." That sounds like common sense. In fact, a few years ago, rumors spread that Japan had bought up all our laver, causing domestic prices to skyrocket. The problem starts right here. Recently, a study was released that directly refutes this common sense. There is a government-funded research institute called the Korea Maritime Institute, or KMI for short. They analyzed 20 years of data on dried seaweed (gim) prices, production volume, and export volume from 2005 to 2025. What was the conclusion? There is limited statistical evidence that rising exports directly lead to a continuous increase in domestic consumer prices. To put it simply, here is how it works. Raw seaweed. At the raw stage, right after being harvested from the sea, export demand can indeed affect local prices. However, as it goes through various stages—processed into dried seaweed, then seasoned, and finally placed on supermarket shelves—that impact is significantly buffered. It is similar to water being purified as it passes through several filters. So, the core point is this. A clear, direct correlation between export volume and consumer price, as we often assume, was not confirmed. Then what actually drives the prices? The industry believes that the actual production volume for that year is a much bigger factor. In fact, the recent drop in prices was not because exports decreased. It happened because production increased significantly due to more farms and favorable weather. There is evidence for this, too. Last year, exports hit an all-time high, yet domestic prices stabilized this year. At the end of last month, prices dropped by 24%in just one week. Exports keep rising, yet prices are falling. If this is the case, our initial hypothesis—the formula that exports make it expensive—falls apart. So, where did the real force that drove up seaweed prices over the past few years come from? The answer was not within us , but outside. That’s right. It is the country I mentioned at the beginning that no one expected: Japan. Japan is a country that has been eating seaweed for much longer and in much greater quantities than we have. Annual per capita consumption is 82 sheets. Along with Korea and China, it is considered one of the world's top three seaweed-consuming nations. Seaweed is an essential ingredient in rice balls, sushi, and even on top of ramen. And they were originally a global powerhouse in seaweed production as well. Regardless of our belief that we are the originators of seaweed, in terms of industry scale, Japan was a formidable player. A few years ago, something unusual started happening in Japan. In 2022, a disaster-like event occurred in the Japanese seaweed industry. Their production volume fell to just 43%of its peak in 2002. That’s less than half. It was the lowest level in 51 years. The cause was the ocean. In the Ariake Sea, Japan’s largest seaweed production area, rising water temperatures and low rainfall led to red tides. Simply put, a red tide is a phenomenon where specific plankton multiply explosively, and these plankton steal the nutrients in the seawater that the seaweed needs to grow . It's as if the weeds in the field are sucking up the fertilizer meant for the crops. The raw materials for high-quality nori are simply disappearing. On top of this, there's a unique problem in Japan. An aging fishing population and a lack of successors. A fisherman with 43 years of experience in nori farming even called it the worst year ever in a broadcast interview. To make matters worse, an oil spill in the Miyagi Prefecture in the Tohoku region caused a loss of about 100 million sheets. It was a case of natural and man-made disasters striking at the same time. Japan, one of the world's top three nori-consuming nations and a global powerhouse in the industry, was driven to a point where it couldn't even produce enough for its own needs. There is a bit of irony here. Despite the domestic shortage, Japan's nori exports have actually nearly doubled over the last five years. This is because there has been steady demand for premium Japanese nori in health-conscious markets like the U.S., New Zealand, and Hong Kong. They were selling their own nori at high prices abroad while struggling with a domestic shortage at home. Eventually, a structure emerged where domestic demand had to be met with imports, and Korean nori filled that gap. This is where Korea comes into the picture. In 2015, Japan reached an agreement with our government. They agreed to gradually increase the import quota for Korean nori, more than doubling it from 1.2 billion sheets in 2015 to 2.7 billion by 2025. When this agreement was first made, it was likely just part of standard trade negotiations. But when the poor harvests hit, it finally became clear just how vital this quota was to the Japanese market. Japan's annual demand for nori is about 8 billion sheets, and nearly 3 billion of that is now being met by imports. And a significant portion of that was Korean. A war to secure raw material even broke out among Japanese manufacturers. According to domestic industry insiders , some large overseas distributors have reportedly bought up nearly 10%of Korea's total nori production. High-quality raw nori has now become a product that domestic companies and foreign buyers compete to purchase first. However, we need to clarify one thing here. If you rank Japan's nori imports by value, the top supplier is actually China, not Korea. Korea is in second place. Chinese nori fills the volume for low-cost, mass-market use, while Korean nori leverages its quality and taste to increasingly penetrate the gaps in a market shaken by poor harvests. So, saying that Korea has completely taken over the Japanese nori market would be an exaggeration. This is also accurately reflected in the numbers. Our nori export values keep hitting record highs, and the government has set a goal of reaching $ 1.8 billion in nori exports. However, the government isn't just pushing for exports blindly. The government is also aware of the concern that rising exports could destabilize domestic prices. So, they introduced a contract production system between raw seaweed farmers and processors to reduce price volatility in advance. The government is also pushing for a system to stockpile a certain amount directly, along with plans for a seaweed exchange . The idea is to grow exports while managing domestic prices through separate mechanisms. On top of that, they developed 2,700 hectares of new seaweed farms and added another 626 hectares this year. In terms of soccer field area, that's a scale reaching into the thousands. A simple side dish has started to be treated as a strategic export product directly managed by the state. However, there is a point here that needs to be addressed in a balanced way. In 2025, Japanese seaweed recovered, ending the three-year price surge, and began trading stably at lower price levels. Actually, the fishing industry is one that fluctuates based on the weather and water temperatures. In other words, a bad harvest this year doesn't guarantee a bad harvest next year. And South Korea isn't in a position to be completely at ease either. Over the past 55 years, our sea temperatures have risen by 1.36 degrees, which is two and a half times faster than the global average. The National Institute of Fisheries Science is still developing seaweed varieties that can withstand high water temperatures. In other words, what happened to Japan today is already approaching our waters with a time lag. If we only see others 'crises as opportunities and ignore our own, we could find ourselves in the same position someday. Nevertheless, the big picture remains unchanged. The government expects global seaweed demand to grow to 210 million bundles by 2030, much higher than it is now. The unit "bundle" might sound a bit unfamiliar. It’s a unit used to count a pack of 100 sheets of seaweed. That means demand is expected to increase significantly in the future. As the climate becomes more unstable, the value of countries that can stably mass-produce seaweed rises even further . While the government and industry continue these efforts, companies are also making moves. Companies like CJ CheilJedang are implementing strategies to expand exports to over 60 countries with high-value products like seasoned seaweed. Of course, the burden of production costs hasn't completely disappeared. But what is clear is the fact that seaweed is no longer just a simple side dish. Let's return to the initial question. Why did seaweed prices drop by 24%in just one week? The superficial reason is that domestic production has increased significantly. But the real story behind it is a bit different. It’s not that our dinner tables suffered because we exported too much, but that while one of the world's best seaweed-producing nations faltered , Korea was gradually filling that void . Ultimately, the price of laver was never just about a side dish. Changes in climate, industry, and the global market are all reflected in a single sheet of laver. The next time you pick up a pack of seasoned laver at the store, I hope you’ll remember that it also contains the currents of the global market.

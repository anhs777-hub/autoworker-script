In 1951, on the island of Geoje in Gyeongsangnam-do, there were no front lines and no bombings. Yet, it became one of the places where the most brutal violence was repeated throughout the entire Korean War. This place was the Geoje Prisoner of War Camp. It was less than a year after the Korean War broke out. The UN forces transported a large number of captured North Korean and Chinese soldiers south from the front lines. The number reached approximately 170,000. It was the largest prisoner of war camp in the world at the time. The official administrative authority of the camp was the UN forces. The actual operation was handled by the U.S. Eighth Army. However, the order within the barbed wire soon drifted in a completely different direction. The prisoners quickly split into two groups . Hardliners who wanted to remain loyal to communism, and prisoners who chose to defect or remain neutral. The problem was that this separation was not the result of voluntary choices. Key figures with strong organizational skills among the North Korean and Chinese prisoners seized control inside the camp. Despite being prisoners themselves, they formed secret organizations, established their own rules, and began ideological control. At night, group meetings called " criticism sessions" were held. Prisoners who expressed a desire to defect or were suspected of cooperating with the U.S. military were dragged out at these sessions. Mass beatings took place, and intimidation and public violence were repeated. Some prisoners died on the spot. This is not a rumor. The U.S. Eighth Army reports explicitly state that hardline communist prisoners enforced a reign of terror against other prisoners. In reality, inside the camp, prisoners watched other prisoners , interrogated them, and punished them. Between late 1951 and early 1952, several incidents occurred where anti-communist prisoners died after being subjected to mass assaults. Bodies were found in drainage ditches and behind latrines or tents. The perpetrators of all this violence were not the armed U.S. soldiers, but the hardline North Korean and Chinese prisoner factions. The camp was no longer a space protecting prisoners of war. It was, in effect, a North Korean-style controlled society within the barbed wire. And on May 7, 1952, this organization led to an unprecedented event in military history . Brigadier General Francis Dodd of the U.S. Army, the commander of the Geoje POW camp, was kidnapped by the prisoners. It was an incident where disarmed prisoners captured a serving U.S. general. This kidnapping was not an accidental occurrence. It was systematically planned, collectively executed, and had clear demands. The prisoners presented a statement demanding an end to the abuse of communist prisoners and a halt to forced defection. General Dodd was only released after signing this document. This incident was immediately reported to the U.S. Department of Defense and the White House. The U.S. military is shocked. The problem was clear. It was revealed that the POW camp had already been taken over by North Korean and Chinese prisoner organizations. Following this, the U.S. military deems the Geoje POW camp to be out of control . The existing management system is dismantled, and orders for a hardline crackdown are issued. In June 1952, the U.S. military begins forcibly separating and housing the prisoners. During this process, hardline communist prisoners engage in collective resistance. They shake the barbed wire, throw stones and blunt weapons, and attack the guards. The U.S. military uses live ammunition. While the number of casualties at the time is officially recorded in the dozens, reports and prisoner testimonies suggest the figure may have been much higher. However, one thing is clear. Even before the shots were fired, rampant violence was already being committed inside the camp by North Korean and Chinese prisoners. This crackdown was not a massacre without cause, but a response to the murders, kidnappings, and organized violence that had already occurred within. On July 27, 1953, the armistice agreement is signed. The prisoners of the Geoje POW camp are repatriated to North Korea and China via Panmunjom. However, an individual investigation into who killed whom and which organizations led the violence in the camp is never carried out. The Geoje POW camp is not an event that can be explained solely by the mistakes of the U.S. military. It is a stark example of how North Korean and Chinese organizations maintained violence and ideological control even while disarmed . Even without guns, power still functioned. And the victims were their fellow prisoners on the same side. That is all.

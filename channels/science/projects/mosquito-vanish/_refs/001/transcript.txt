When summer arrives, mosquitoes follow along with the deohee. Mosquitoes torment us throughout the summer, and since they can squeeze through any tiny gap, it is difficult to block their mouths. The reason we dislike these weapons is that a bite causes itching and a buzzing, annoying sensation. Therefore, it is common practice to catch and eliminate mosquitoes immediately upon sight. However, catching mosquitoes is not an easy task. While they are easy to catch when clinging to walls or ceilings, they are truly difficult to catch when you don't know where they are. Even if you manage to locate them and attempt to catch them, quick-witted mosquitoes will darting away; you have likely experienced a mosquito suddenly vanishing from sight while you are trying to catch one in flight. A mosquito's flight speed is not that fast, ranging from 2.4 to 4.8 km/h. So why do we miss them even after looking at them so closely? When focusing on the movement of large objects, the eye's speed can reach up to 700 degrees per second. In the case of small objects, while it may not be as fast as focusing on large objects, it is still not a speed at which the eye cannot focus. To put it simply, this is because various factors aligned well. First, mosquitoes have excellent flight capabilities. Because their instantaneous and evasive speeds are fast, the eye can lose sight of them as they fly while moving here and there. To make this easier to understand, imagine a fly entering your home; you can observe it flying around at a very high speed. A fly's flight speed is 8 to 15 km/h, which is about three to four times faster than a mosquito. Even if you lose sight of it, its relatively large size allows it to be quickly caught again. However, because mosquitoes are small, it is difficult to catch them once they are out of sight. Of course, this argument alone lacks persuasiveness, so let me present a scientific argument. Adults blink their eyes approximately 15 to 20 times per minute. At this time, the eyes blink at a speed of 100 to 150 meters; although this is a very fast speed, one inevitably cannot perceive objects the moment the eyes are closed. Furthermore , since the eyeball does not remain stationary but moves slightly during a blink, there is a subtle difference between the focus established before the blink and the focus established afterward. In this case, if the mosquito flies only in a straight line, the eyes predict the path and adjust the focus at the moment of blinking. The problem is that the mosquito does not fly only in a straight line but also moves further away from the point of focus, so there may be moments when it is lost from the field of vision. The background also has an influence. If you think about the interior of most homes, it is not monotonous. It is not easy for the eyes to stably focus on a moving object amidst such a complex background, yet when focusing on a flying mosquito, the eyes make an effort to eliminate the background. However, if the background acts as camouflage for the moving object or is too complex, it causes confusion in perception and interferes. In this situation, visual information about the object enters segmentally, causing you to view the object discontinuously and lose sight of the object you intend to observe. As these various factors overlap, the phenomenon of a flying mosquito suddenly disappearing from your view occurs. So, is there no solution? When focusing on an object with your eyes, your sense of balance in your head works in conjunction with visual information; therefore, it is best to move your head as well, rather than relying solely on your eyes.

What would happen if we drilled a huge hole in the bottom of the Han River and drained all the water? As soon as the water drains, a massive valley of sand, 1km wide, would be revealed in the middle of Seoul. The bridges would expose their bare feet. But the bridge pillars we see every day are only a small part of them. The pillars actually pierce through the riverbed and stand firmly anchored into the solid rock below. The Han River is shallower than you might think. Did you know that there is a subway line running 28 meters beneath that riverbed ? Last time, we looked at the Pacific Ocean, the deepest place on Earth. Today, we’re looking at our own local river, the Han River. Since the Han River is shallow, we’ll dive into its history along with its depth. We’ll cover bombs that slept on the riverbed for 60 years after the war, an island that was blown up and then grew six times larger, an island that vanished completely after being dredged for sand , and the true secrets hidden beneath the Han River. If you like stories like this, please subscribe and like the video. Shall we head down to the riverbed? First, let’s look at what kind of river the Han River is. The Han River begins in the mountains of Gangwon-do, meets the Namhan and Bukhan rivers at Yangsuri to become one, and flows about 494km to the West Sea. As it passes through Seoul, the river is about 1km wide. That’s a width you could only cross by laying four 63 Building skyscrapers end to end. The section passing through Seoul alone, from the edge of Gangdong to the edge of Gangseo, spans about 41.5 km. But this wide river is shallower than you’d expect. When the riverbed was dredged in the 1980s, the depth was set to at least 2.5 meters. That’s just a bit deeper than an adult’s height. Of course, there are deeper spots. Near Yanghwa Bridge, there are places that are over 10 meters deep. 10 meters is the height of six adults standing on each other’s shoulders. In ancient times, people called this river "Arisu. " It’s written that way on the Gwanggaeto Stele of Goguryeo. The Baekje kingdom called it "Ungiha." There’s a reason why such a shallow river always looks like a calm lake. Now, let’s really drain the water. Even draining just a little reveals a strange wall blocking the river. It’s a concrete weir stretching across the entire river just below Jamsil Bridge. It is about 6 meters high and 873 meters long. Its name is the Jamsil Underwater Weir. Though it’s hard to see because it’s submerged, this weir holds the water upstream. That’s why the Han River upstream feels more like a giant lake than a flowing river. There is another one downstream, below Gimpo Bridge. It’s the Singok Underwater Weir, built in 1988. It prevents the tide from the West Sea from pushing up toward Seoul. It’s not just there to block the water. There are fish ladders that look like stairs on the Jamsil Underwater Weir so that fish can move up and down. Both weirs were created during the Han River Comprehensive Development project in the 1980s. It's to keep the river's water level consistent. So, if you drain the water, the Han River reveals itself as two massive steps. We have been looking at the water pooled on those steps all this time. This time, let’s drain 5 meters more of the water. Beneath the murky water, the riverbed starts to appear. The first thing you see is trash. The Seoul Metropolitan Government once surveyed the riverbed with ultrasound and found about 2,300 tons sunk just in the Seoul section. Items retrieved by divers included motorcycles, bicycles, and even waste tires. Visibility is almost zero underwater, so they have to search by feeling around with their hands. There is construction waste, too. In 2015, during the Yanghwa Bridge construction, about 34 tons of concrete debris were caught after being secretly buried in the riverbed. And in December 2015, a terrifying object was found 7 meters deep under the Hangang Railroad Bridge. It was a 130cm long, 450kg aerial bomb dropped by the U.S. military during the Korean War. A company cleaning the riverbed found it, and eventually, they halted KTX and subway operations overnight to hoist it up. For over 60 years since the war ended, the bomb had been lying right there on the riverbed beneath our feet. When the water is drained, the first things revealed are the things we discarded and forgot. Now, let’s drain 10 meters of water. The bottom is fully exposed even in the deepest spots . So, can we see the bridge piers all the way to the end? No, we can't. The piers don't end at the riverbed; they go through the sand and gravel down to the solid rock far below. Sand and gravel on the riverbed shift with the currents. If you set a pier there, the base could erode during a flood, causing the pier to tilt. That’s why bridge foundations are required to be set on solid rock, deeper than the currents can erode. Then, how do they drive a pier down to the rock in the middle of a river? The secret is a caisson. They set a huge, bottomless concrete cylinder on the riverbed and keep digging out the soil inside. Then, the cylinder gradually sinks under its own weight. They repeat the digging and sinking process until it hits solid rock, then fill the inside with concrete. This becomes the bridge's foundation. Let’s look at an actual photo. This is the Cheonho Bridge construction site in 1975. There are round, cylinder-shaped frames lined up on the river. They build the bridge foundations inside these round frames. Most of the Han River bridges stand on these caissons. From Hangang Bridge and Hannam Bridge to Jamsil Bridge and Mapo Bridge. The largest one is the World Cup Bridge, which opened in 2021. The diameter of its main caisson is a staggering 32 meters. It’s a container as large as a 10-story building laid on its side. That is why the bridge foundations must always be monitored. The Seongsu Bridge, which collapsed in 1994, was rebuilt by encasing two 6 m-diameter cylindrical pillars in concrete to create a massive 26m by 9m oval pillar. How deep the foundation goes depends on the bridge and its location. That’s because it goes down until it reaches bedrock. So, the answer isn’t a number, but the rock itself. This is why you can’t say a bridge is secure just by looking at the pillars above water. The real strength comes from the feet anchored in the rock. But there’s more than just bridge foundations beneath the riverbed. Between Yeouido and Mapo. Subway Line 5 runs about 28 meters below the riverbed on average. That’s a depth equal to the height of a nine-story apartment building. The tunnel is about 1.5 km long and consists of a 7.6 m-diameter tube. That’s why the platforms at Yeouinaru and Mapo stations are deep underground. There was even a shocking incident. In August 1995, river water burst into the tunnel under construction, flooding it entirely. About 25,000 tons of water flooded in. That’s how daunting a task it is to drill under a river. You are essentially carrying an entire river over your head. Currently, the route between Gwangnaru and Gangbyeon on Line 5, and the Line 8 Byeollae Line that opened in 2024, also pass under the Han River. The deepest point of the Byeollae Line tunnel reaches 52 meters below the surface. So, even if you drained all the water, that’s not the end of it. There is another Seoul passing beneath the riverbed. Now, let’s look at a bridge that sinks when the water rises. That would be the Jamsugyo Bridge. When the Han River water level reaches 6.5 meters. At 5.5 meters, the bridge gets submerged. It’s a bridge that literally dives, as its name suggests. Jamsugyo Bridge was completed on July 15, 1976. It was submerged for the first time on August 13, just 28 days later. Why was it built so low? It’s said it was to allow military vehicles like tanks to cross quickly in wartime and to be easily repaired if bombed. That’s why the distance between the pillars is also short, at about 15 meters. So it could be quickly reconnected if destroyed. In 1982, the Banpo Bridge was built above it, making it Korea's first double-decker bridge. Even today, over 20,000 cars cross this low bridge every day on average. It’s a road that has connected the north and south of Seoul for 50 years. And a big change is coming to this bridge. Starting October 26, 2026, Jamsugyo will be closed to cars and transformed into a pedestrian bridge. A bridge that spent half a century repeatedly sinking and rising will now become a place for people to walk. That concludes the depth. Since the Han River is shallow, to go any deeper, we must travel back in time. As we descend through the 1980s, 1970s, 1960s, and all the way back to the Joseon Dynasty, the Han River becomes an entirely different river. First, let's head back to the 1980s. In September 1982, Seoul began a massive project to completely overhaul the Han River. It was called the Han River Comprehensive Development Project . They dredged the winding riverbed to make it straight and lined the riverbanks with concrete. It was essentially a total redesign of the river. The amount of sand and gravel dredged from the riverbed reached a staggering 54 million tons. That’s enough to fill 3.6 million 15-ton dump trucks. It was three times the amount that experts deemed appropriate. The dredged sand and gravel were sold to cover about 190 billion won of the construction costs. In a sense, they renovated the river by digging up its own floor. That is how today’s Han River Park waterfronts and the Olympic Expressway were created. The underwater weirs in Jamsil and Singok were also built at this time. When the project ended in September 1986, the Han River took on the straight appearance we know today. However, there were things lost in exchange. Specifically, the natural riverbed where layers of gravel, sand, and mud had accumulated. Critics pointed out that too much dredging had destroyed the natural layers. But before the river became so straight, there were islands in the Han River that have now vanished. Let's go back even further to the 1970s. The present-day Jamsil was originally an island floating in the middle of the river. In 1971, the Songpa River, which flowed toward the island, was dammed and filled with soil. That is how the island became land. A smaller island right next to it also became land at this time. However, there is a remnant of the old waterway that they left behind. That is the current Seokchon Lake. Seokchon Lake is essentially a trace of the old Han River. Jamsil was not the only island to disappear in the 1970s. In front of Apgujeong, there was a large sand island called Jeojado. It covered an area of about 1.18 million square meters. It was an island about the size of 100 soccer fields. But starting in 1970, sand was taken from this island to reclaim land for building apartments in Apgujeong. And in 1972, Jeojado completely vanished from the map. The Apgujeong apartment complex was built on the land reclaimed with that sand. An entire island essentially became the foundation for apartments. Mudongdo, a small island near Cheongdam-dong, also disappeared without a trace during the same period due to sand mining and reclamation. From 1962 to 1971, Seoul filled in 19 locations along the river, including Banpo, Apgujeong, Guui, and Jamsil. The land created this way spans about 3.96 million square meters. A significant number of the apartments lining the river today stand on this reclaimed land. At the western end of the river was another island called Nanjido. From 1978 to 1993. 92 million tons of Seoul's waste were buried on this island. This created two trash mountains, each 95 meters high. Haneul Park, where silver grass now waves, sits right on top of those trash mountains. Seoul expanded its land using sand from the Han River. But dredging sand wasn't the only way they got rid of islands. On February 10, 1968, at 3 p.m., a massive explosion rocked a small island in the middle of the Han River. It was the moment Bamseom Island was blown up. Bamseom was originally home to 62 families and 443 residents. It was a small village settled right in the middle of the river. They blew up the island to improve the river flow for Yeouido's development and to secure stone. The residents moved to the hills of Mapo, and the island was shattered into nine small pieces. That same year, they built the 7.6 km-long Yunjung Embankment around Yeouido in just 110 days. Yeouido had originally been an airfield since 1916. It was a sandy islet where a runway had been laid. That sandy island has now become Yeouido, home to the National Assembly, broadcasters, and financial firms. People often say Yeouido was built with rocks from the Bamseom explosion, but that's not true. The rocks and soil from Bamseom made up only 3.3%of the fill used for Yeouido. Bamseom, the island that was blown up and abandoned. But the story of this island doesn't end here. I'll show you the real twist in just a moment. If we go back further to the 1960s, the Han River had a completely different look. Long stretches of white sandy beaches lined the riverbanks. In the summer, people used to swim in the Han River. There was even a Han River swimming pool at Ttukseom. There were even showers and water taps on the beaches for vacationers. It was a landscape entirely different from today's Han River parks. In the winter, they cut ice from the frozen river, stored it in warehouses, and sold it in the summer. There was also a laundry area along the Mapo riverbank. The Han River was a living space that Seoul residents visited every day. But swimming was banned in some sections in 1963 as Seoul grew and the river water started to get dirty. However, sand-dredging boats were already traveling on the river by then. Back in 1958, dredgers were already scooping up sand from the riverbed. And the Han River flooded quite often. Because the embankments were low, riverside villages were frequently submerged during heavy rain. Even in 1972, the water rose high enough to reach just below the Han River railway bridge. Let's go further back to July 1925. It was the Great Flood of 1925, the largest in Han River history. The water level reached 12.74 meters based on the old Yongsan record, which remains the highest ever. In the Han River basin alone, 404 people were swept away and died. This flood completely washed away riverside villages in Ttukseom, Ichon-dong, Jamsil, and Songpa. Part of the sandy island of Jamsil was also swept away during this flood. The river constantly erases islands and creates new ones like this. There is a place where they quarried stone to fight the floods. It was Seonyubong, a peak that stood about 40 meters high. It was eroded and eroded until it finally became an island. That is today's Seonyudo Park. Finally, let's go back to the Joseon Dynasty. People in the Joseon Dynasty called the Han River passing through Seoul the "Gyeonggang." Boats carrying tax rice collected from across the country came up to the Mapo, Yongsan, and Seogang ferry ports. It is said that over 10,000 boats carrying fish and salt came and went through Mapo every year. If you unfold a map from that time, the river is full of islands everywhere. Yeouido, Bamseom, Jamsil, Jeojado, and even Mudongdo. These are the islands we saw today. Most of today's Han River bridges were placed where the old ferry ports used to be. The bridges now replace the routes that boats once crossed. A river full of islands and sandbars. That was the original appearance of the Han River. Let's come back to the present again. The truly amazing part starts now. Bamseom, which was blown up in 1968. What happened to it now? Before it was blown up, Bamseom was about 45,000 square meters in 1966, but it grew to about 280,000 square meters in 2013. It has become more than six times larger. It’s not like anyone brought soil to fill it in. Once people stopped interfering, the sand and soil brought by the river accumulated every year, and the island grew on its own. The principle is simple. The river erodes the outer curves and deposits sand on the inside where the current is slow. When sand keeps piling up where the current slows, an island forms in the middle of the river. This is called an alluvial island. Yeouido and Bamseom are also islands formed this way. Now, Bamseom is filled with willow forests, and migratory birds rest there. Public access has been restricted since 1999, and it was registered as a Ramsar Wetland in 2012. The number of species living on Bamseom increased from 250 in 2013 to 329 in 2025. The same goes for Jeojado, which had disappeared. After some 50 years, sand is accumulating again, and the island is gradually emerging. An island that vanishes when people remove it, and piles up again when left alone. The Han River is still creating islands on its own. The Han River's water is also becoming clear again. The level of pollution in the lower reaches dropped from 11.1 mg per liter in the 1980s to 1.8 mg in July 2026. And we drink this water. About 26 million people in Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi-do are drinking Han River water. Seoul's tap water is drawn from five intake stations located in a 25km section between Paldang Dam and the Jamsil Underwater Weir. It is the very same Han River we see every day. While dams and levees now prevent floods like the one in 1925, the Han River is still a river that can swell frighteningly whenever there is heavy rain. Today, by draining the Han River, we discovered stairs under the water, a bomb dormant for over 60 years, bridge piers embedded in rock, subways under the riverbed, and islands that disappeared and returned. And in the end, all that remained was sand. Seoul dredged that sand to expand its land, and the Han River is still building islands with what’s left. What we need to do is simpler than you’d think. Dredge less , dump less, and sometimes just leave it alone. Because it’s the river that 26 million people drink from.

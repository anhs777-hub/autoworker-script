Here is a dam built in the exact opposite way of Soyanggang Dam. It is Chungju Dam. While Soyanggang Dam was made by piling up earth and rock, this is the nation’s largest concrete dam, built by pouring 900,000 cubic meters of concrete. Both dams block the same Han River water, supply water to the same Seoul, and are similar in size. Yet one was built with earth, and the other with concrete. And that was within just five years. Why did the same country give polar opposite answers for the same river? We must start with the story of Soyanggang Dam. The original design for this dam was also concrete. It was a design drawn up by a Japanese firm that had built dams all over the world. The government was just about to start the implementation design according to those plans. But Hyundai Engineering&Construction, which took on the project, came forward with a different proposal. They suggested building it with earth and rock. The reason was simple. Concrete dams consume a massive amount of cement and rebar, and transporting them to the mountains of Gangwon-do cost a fortune. On the other hand, soil, gravel, and rock were scattered all around the construction site. The calculation was that switching to an embankment dam would reduce both costs and construction time. The designers and the government were dead set against it . After all, there was no domestic experience in building such a massive dam made of earth at the time. So, why not just go with the proven concrete method? This is where national security logic flipped the table. A concrete dam would shatter under bombing, causing water to pour out at once, but an earthen dam would only suffer a crater at the impact site without collapsing entirely. It was a dam located upstream of Seoul, not far from the armistice line. The President accepted this logic , and the design was changed in 1968. However, who led this process is still a matter of debate. The Hyundai side claims they pushed for it, while the Ministry of Construction at the time claims the government decided it first. Either way, the conclusion was the same . In 1973, an embankment dam 123 meters high, capable of holding 2.9 billion tons of water, was completed. At the time of completion, it was one of the largest embankment dams in Asia, and the volume of piled earth and rock reached 9.6 million cubic meters. Instead of cement, they put the local soil to work. But earth and concrete handle the pressure of water in completely different ways. An earthen dam holds up through its sheer thickness. It absorbs the water’s pushing force with its wide and heavy body. That is why the 123-meter-tall Soyanggang Dam has an incredibly wide base. However, it has one weakness. If water ever flows over the top of the dam, the soil washes away, and it collapses in an instant. That is why, for earthen dams, the spillway for releasing floodwater is built by cutting into the adjacent mountain, not the dam body itself. A concrete dam is different. It holds up through its weight. Viewed from the side, it's a triangle; this mass, wide at the bottom and pointed at the top, sits firmly on the bed with its own weight to push back the water. That is why it's called a gravity dam. The only enemy isn't just the force of water. Water seeping under the dam pushes it upward from the bottom. This is called uplift pressure. To prevent this, holes are drilled closely into the foundation rock to inject cement grout to block the water, and any remaining leakage is drained through drainage galleries inside the dam. Another enemy is heat. Cement generates heat as it sets, so if you pour 900,000 cubic meters at once, the inside gets hot and then cracks as it cools. That is why a concrete dam is not built as a single mass. It is divided into blocks, stacked in thin layers, with pipes buried inside to circulate cold water as they are built up. This is why construction at Chungju Dam began in the spring of 1982 and required several more years of pouring. On the other hand, a concrete dam can withstand water overflowing the top and allows floodgates to be placed directly on its body. So, the reason why concrete was chosen for the Namhan River five years later lies not in the material, but in the conditions. Although the reason isn't written in a single line in official records, it becomes clear when you compare the conditions of the two dams. First, the situation regarding cement had changed. Around 1968, when the Soyang River Dam design was changed, cement production was just beginning to grow, but by the late 1970s, production had increased to rank among the top ten in the world. Moreover, where were those cement factories located? They were right upstream of the Namhan River in Jecheon and Danyang. It was just above the river that the Chungju Dam would block. At the very least, they were not in a position where they had to haul cement from far away. Second, the flood factor . A major mission of the Chungju Dam was to store over 600 million tons of water during floods to lower the Han River's water level. To do that, six massive floodgates capable of discharging over 10,000 tons per second were needed, and in a narrow gorge, the only place to put them was on the dam's body. If it had been an earth dam, they would have had to carve out an entire mountain separately. The reasons that led to choosing soil for the Soyang River Dam had disappeared one by one in Chungju. Thus, in June 1978, construction began on a concrete dam 97.5 meters high and 447 meters long. It holds 2.75 billion tons of water. It used 902,000 cubic meters of concrete, and with four 100,000 kW generators added, it created the largest power plant among multipurpose dams in the country. However, this power plant created a new problem. The Chungju Dam was designed for peak power generation. It is a method of generating power by releasing a large volume of water only during the hours when the entire nation consumes the most electricity. Thermal and nuclear power plants struggle to quickly adjust output, but hydropower generates electricity within minutes just by opening the floodgates. It's essentially holding back 2.7 billion tons for those few hours. The problem was what happened downstream. Water gushes out only when power is generated , and for the rest of the time, the flow stops. The river rises and falls several times a day. Yet, people downstream rely on the river for water. They use it for farming, running factories, and as tap water. So, why not just release the water at a constant rate? Because then, peak power generation would be impossible. The whole purpose was to concentrate power production during peak demand hours. If you generate power, the river surges; if you keep the river steady, you can't generate power. It's a maddening dilemma. So, they flipped the approach. Instead of trying to do both with one dam, they decided to build another dam further down. 19.6 km downstream from the main dam, they built a lower dam, 21 meters high and 480.7 meters long, with 20 floodgates. This is a regulating dam. It collects the water released from above and releases it steadily at about 100 tons per second, 24 hours a day. The top generates the electricity, and the bottom stabilizes the flow. If Soyang Dam had earth do the work instead of cement, Chungju Dam had a smaller dam do the work instead of the main dam. This regulating dam also has two 6,000 kW generators to produce electricity. While smoothing out the flow, it generates extra power as a bonus. Of course, there were downsides. Valleys were submerged, villages vanished, and over 40,000 residents had to leave their homes. 43 cultural heritage sites that would have been submerged were dismantled over three years and relocated to the Cheongpung Cultural Heritage Complex. Their original locations are now at the bottom of Chungju Lake. After construction began in June 1978, it took over seven years to complete, with the ceremony held in the fall of 1985. It lowered the flood level of the Han River bridges by over a meter and provides 3.38 billion tons of water annually to the capital region and North Chungcheong Province. Even now, this dam releases water when electricity is needed most, and the small dam below balances it out. Soyang Dam and Chungju Dam were not opposites. They simply provided different solutions based on the available materials and the roles they were assigned. A dam with another dam beneath it.

A massive gamble, risking an astronomical 40 trillion won, is now unfolding. At the very moment others laughed, calling it impossible, Korean engineers completed a technology beyond imagination. The miraculous hydrogen steelworks that emits nothing but pure water instead of black smoke from its chimneys. We now reveal the scene of that bold history, which has completely changed the landscape of global steel. Let’s turn back time half a century to June 9, 1973. At 7:30 AM, molten iron finally poured out of the No. 1 blast furnace at the Pohang steel mill. It was a deeply moving moment as iron erupted for the first time in South Korean history. Honorary Chairman Park Tae-joon and his engineers embraced one another and wept uncontrollably. When a poor nation with no capital or technology proposed building a steel mill, the World Bank refused loans, calling it impossible. But with a desperate resolve to drown themselves in Yeongil Bay if they failed, they finally achieved a miracle. To commemorate this first casting, South Korea designated June 9 as Steel Day. Since that day, the Pohang No. 1 furnace burned its red flames without pause for 48 years and six months. The molten iron produced by this giant furnace reached a staggering 55.2 million tons. It was an immense amount, enough to build 55 million mid-sized cars. It was the heart that supported the Miracle on the Han River from the bottom up, building Hyundai car bodies and launching massive oil tankers. But on December 29, 2021, after half a century, the fire was finally extinguished as it retired into history . It was the official end of the blast furnace that seemed like it would last forever. And from this moment on, a massive dark cloud began to loom, shaking the entire South Korean steel industry. The 1,500-degree flames that melted iron were now branded as the primary culprit of global warming. In its natural state, iron ore is tightly bonded with oxygen. To strip away this oxygen and obtain pure molten iron, one must burn a massive amount of coal, or coke. This had been the only way humanity produced iron for thousands of years. But the price was severe. For every ton of molten iron produced, about 2 tons of massive carbon dioxide are released into the air. As of 2024, POSCO’s annual greenhouse gas emissions reached a staggering 72.06 million tons. It was an astronomical figure that has exceeded 70 million tons for three consecutive years. This accounted for a massive 11%of South Korea’s total national greenhouse gas emissions. The proud chimneys that once drove the national economy overnight became a massive fuse for national carbon emissions. To make matters worse , the European Union announced that it would fully implement the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism from 2026. It was a cold notice that astronomical carbon taxes would be levied on steel products made with high carbon emissions. If huge carbon taxes are added to steel prices, Korea's auto and shipbuilding industries will lose price competitiveness as well. It was a moment when the miracle of building the world's best manufacturing industry in a country with no oil or iron ore was shaken to its core. A sense of dread crept in that the industrial glory built with blood and sweat for half a century could disappear like a mirage. Yet, it was impossible to immediately stop the operation of blast furnaces pouring out tens of millions of tons of molten iron. If steel production stops, a desperate crisis would strike, as the very heart of Korea's manufacturing industry would cease to beat. Then, the answer seems quite simple. Couldn't we just pay to buy Europe's latest eco-friendly steelmaking technology? However, a deadly trap that is hard to even imagine was hidden behind that seemingly easy solution. If we need to reduce carbon, introducing the hydrogen reduction steelmaking technology already developed by Europe seems the fastest and safest way. In fact, steel powerhouses like Sweden and Germany had already launched large-scale projects long ago. The method they chose was a shaft furnace in the shape of a massive cylindrical tower. It was a process of dropping iron ore from the top of the tower and blowing hot hydrogen from the bottom to remove oxygen. On the surface , it looked like a perfectly verified technology. However, POSCO engineers could not hide their deep despair and shock. Because it was a massive trap that was flashy on the outside but impossible to handle. There was a fatal structural weakness hidden in the shaft furnace method. For gas to pass smoothly from bottom to top, the raw material must be in a uniform, solid bead shape. This processed, bead-shaped iron ore is called a pellet. It is a processed product made by mixing iron ore powder with a special binder and hardening it into balls. Since natural ore must be processed once more, the cost inevitably skyrockets. If powdered iron ore is put in as is, the gas holes get completely blocked, preventing proper reduction. The problem was precisely this pellet supply chain. Pellets account for a mere 25%to 30%of all iron ore mined worldwide. What was even more desperate was the extremely scarce supply of ultra-high-grade pellets with over 67%iron content. These were precious commodities effectively monopolized by a few large mining conglomerates in Sweden and Brazil. These high-grade pellets are 20 %to 30%more expensive than unprocessed, ordinary iron ore. We were in a situation where we could barely acquire them by paying a huge premium of tens of dollars per ton. What would happen if steelmakers around the world all started building European-style shaft reduction furnaces? The price of high-grade pellets would skyrocket, leaving resource-poor Korea scrambling to secure raw materials. It would be a nightmare come true: state-of-the-art factories built with trillions of won would grind to a halt for lack of materials. Following the European approach was essentially an own goal, surrendering raw material sovereignty and being held on a leash by others. In the end, POSCO rejected the comfortable path paved by others and chose the rugged, thorny road. It was a technology to use the common and cheap powdered iron ore found all over the world as it is. They decided to create a completely new, proprietary process that had never existed in the world. However, if it were as easy as it sounds, advanced steel-producing nations around the world would have created it long ago. Using powdered iron ore directly to make steel was the most reckless challenge in the history of steelmaking. The powdered iron ore mined in its natural state is called iron ore fines. They are grains like fine sand or black dust, with a diameter of only 8mm or less. Surprisingly, a whopping 70%to 75%of the iron ore produced globally is exactly this type of fine ore. They are everywhere on Earth and require no processing, making them much cheaper and more stable than pellets. Then why hasn't humanity been able to directly use this common fine ore for the past hundreds of years? The answer lay in the immutable laws of physics. If you put this powder into a giant furnace and blow strong wind from below, the powder rides the wind and flies out the chimney. Conversely, if you blow the wind weakly, the dust sticks together and completely blocks the gas passages. Ultimately, operating a reduction furnace was impossible without spending trillions of won to clump the powder into pellets. The same was true in the early 1990s when POSCO announced it would use powdered iron ore directly. Steel giants in Japan and the U.S. sent cold sneers, calling it pouring water into a bottomless pit. But right before this barrier of impossibility, POSCO unveiled a secret weapon, the only one of its kind in the world. It was FINEX, a proprietary technology completed after pouring trillions of won into R&D over the past 30 years. POSCO’s engineers turned the fatal weakness of the powder flying into the air into a reverse-thinking breakthrough. It was a brilliant idea: if the powder blows away, why not carry out the chemical reaction while it's suspended in mid-air? This is the fluidized bed reactor technology, the core heart of POSCO's hydrogen reduction steelmaking. Hydrogen gas is blasted upward from the bottom of a cylindrical reactor at precisely calculated pressure and flow rates. Then, hundreds of millions of iron ore powder particles are lifted into the air, riding the wind. Like water boiling in a pot, the fine powder dances within the gas, swirling ferociously. In Europe's shaft furnaces , it took hours for gas to permeate into thick pellets. But in the fluidized bed reactor, every single dispersed powder particle comes into direct contact with the hydrogen. The surface area expands thousands of times , and a miraculous chemical reaction occurs, stripping away oxygen in just a few seconds. While the past FINEX process used coal gas, HyREX has replaced it with 100%pure green hydrogen. Since not a single bit of coal is used, the only thing coming out of the chimney is clean water vapor. It was a magnificent achievement that instantly collapsed the expensive pellet cartel Europe had built over decades with mere sand-like ore fines. However, having a perfect theory did not mean it would lead directly to commercialization. The moment we replaced coal with hydrogen, another massive, unimaginable physical barrier stood in our way. In a traditional blast furnace, coal burns and emits tremendous heat on its own, but hydrogen is the exact opposite. When hydrogen strips away oxygen, it causes an endothermic reaction that ruthlessly absorbs heat from its surroundings. As the reaction progressed, the temperature inside the furnace plummeted, causing the powder particles to cool down rapidly. The astonishing paradox was that the furnace we had worked so hard to heat up would freeze itself and come to a halt. Maintaining high temperatures of hundreds of degrees while preventing hydrogen—the smallest and lightest gas—from leaking was an extreme challenge. How exactly did Korean engineers solve this hellish thermodynamic dilemma? The answer lay in an innovative structure: a massive four-stage series fluidized bed reactor. Instead of trying to finish all reactions in one vessel, they broke the role down into four stages. If you attempt reduction in just one reactor, the cold ore and hot gas collide, causing the powder to clump together and harden. This was the nozzle-clogging phenomenon known as " sticking." POSCO connected four cylindrical reactors in a cascade, passing the iron ore powder and hydrogen through them in opposite directions. Cold iron ore powder is fed into the highest, first-stage reactor. And high-purity, 800-degree hot hydrogen is blown into the fourth-stage reactor at the very bottom. It was a structure where falling black dust and rising hydrogen collided head-on. In the first tower, gas rising from below gradually preheats the cold iron ore to 450 degrees. This step removes moisture from the powder and brings it to the optimal reaction temperature. As it descends to the second and third towers , the real chemical battle with hydrogen begins. The oxygen molecules tightly bonded to the iron ore are stripped away step by step. And finally , when it reaches the fourth tower at the bottom, the final magic is complete . Facing the hottest and purest hydrogen, over 95%of the oxygen completely evaporates. As a result, the red dust-like iron ore transforms into direct reduced iron, a pure silver-colored metal powder. The hydrogen that has lost its oxygen turns only into clean water and steam, then flies off into the air. Not a single drop of the remaining hydrogen gas is wasted; it is filtered, reheated, and 100%recycled. What’s even more amazing is that the high-temperature metal powder leaving the fourth tower retains a heat of 700 degrees. It was a sophisticated energy design that drastically reduced power consumption by passing the heat directly to the next process. It was an unprecedented complex process that no steelmaker in the world had even dared to attempt. They perfectly overcame the thermodynamic limits of nature through sophisticated multi-stage engineering. So, has every problem been solved now? Unfortunately, a more difficult final hurdle stood before the engineers. That is because the steel plates used for cars and ships must be made of solid molten steel, not powder. How could they possibly melt cold iron powder without coal flames into molten steel boiling at 1500 degrees? The answer lay in a special electric furnace technology called an electric melting furnace. It was a facility that continuously fed the hot iron powder from the hydrogen reduction process and melted it instantly. Without using any coal, it produces high-purity molten steel at 1500 degrees using only clean electricity. However, more time was needed until 2030, when HyREX technology would be fully commercialized. This is because Europe's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism was fast approaching starting in 2026. Instead of waiting idly, POSCO built a massive stepping-stone fortress in Gwangyang, Jeonnam. The fruit of this labor was the large electric furnace at Gwangyang Steelworks, which held its completion ceremony on June 17 , 2026. It was a massive facility with an annual capacity of 2.5 million tons, the largest single unit in the country. POSCO invested approximately 600 billion won and mobilized a total of 270,000 workers to complete it in 28 months. Compared to traditional blast furnaces, this massive electric furnace has dramatically reduced carbon dioxide emissions by up to 75%. The original electric furnaces used to melt scrap metal had a fatal weakness: impurities made it difficult to produce high-grade automotive outer panels. Here, POSCO unveiled a unique alloying technology that shocked the global steel industry. It was an innovative method of blending low-carbon molten steel from electric furnaces with clean molten steel from traditional blast furnaces in a golden ratio. While cutting carbon emissions by a minute amount, they perfectly maintained the top-tier quality required for premium automotive steel sheets. Gwangyang's electric furnace became the most reliable shield to immediately fend off carbon border taxes. However, this was merely a temporary stepping stone to buy time. There are limits to scrap metal supply, and achieving net zero by 2050 requires the complete replacement of the blast furnaces themselves. Building hydrogen reduction steelmaking facilities and massive electric smelting furnaces required an unimaginably large amount of space. Yet, there wasn't a single inch of empty land left within the Pohang Steelworks, which had been expanding non-stop for 50 years. Demolishing existing plants created a dilemma, as it would cut off the supply of molten steel urgently needed by automakers and shipyards. In the end, if there was no land, the only option was to reclaim the sea and create new territory. It was like returning to the starting point of history, which their predecessors built with blood and tears 50 years ago. The engineers 'eyes turned beyond the factory walls toward the sea, where rough waves crashed. The true stage for the decisive battle that would determine the fate of Korean steel was quietly rising, not in Gwangyang, but in the waters off Yeongil Bay. It was the largest and most audacious maritime pioneering operation in the history of South Korean industry. POSCO made the bold decision to directly reclaim the public waters of Yeongil Bay, right in front of the Pohang Steelworks. The reclamation area alone is a staggering 1.35 million square meters. In more familiar terms, it was a massive sea spanning about 410,000 pyeong. It was a monumental project to create a new territory in the middle of the ocean that is larger than 190 soccer fields combined. It was a difficult construction project requiring the stacking of massive caisson sea walls on the seabed to block the rough waves and typhoons of the East Sea. Although it faced difficulties for years due to environmental and permitting issues, a miraculous breakthrough finally opened in March 2026. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport finalized the approval for the change in the Pohang industrial complex plan, unlocking all legal barriers. With this , the civil engineering project of the century to reclaim the sea in phases until 2041 begins in earnest. What will rise above these 410,000 pyeong of blue waves? At its center, the world's first outpost for the commercialization of hydrogen reduction steelmaking will reveal its magnificent form. First, in 2026, the HyREX demonstration plant with a capacity of 300,000 tons per year broke ground for its historic start. It was the moment when the four-stage fluidized-bed reduction furnace, developed at the cost of trillions of won, and the electric furnace were combined into one massive plant. This demonstration facility will be completed in 2028 and will begin full-scale molten iron production. Following this, POSCO plans to complete the securing of HyREX commercialization technology by 2030. Afterward, three super-sized HyREX facilities and electric furnaces will be installed sequentially on the reclaimed land. A perfect self-sufficient ecosystem will be completed, equipped with large-scale green hydrogen storage facilities and eco-friendly water treatment plants. The landscape of old steel mills, with their red dust and acrid smoke, will disappear completely. A dream steel mill will be born, with the sound of magnificent electric arcs and only pure white steam rising against the backdrop of the blue East Sea. The scale of the funding for this massive project is beyond imagination. An astronomical sum of about 20 trillion won is being poured into the construction of the hydrogen steel mill at Pohang Yeongil Bay alone. On top of that, the total investment to completely replace all blast furnaces in Pohang and Gwangyang with hydrogen by 2050 reaches a staggering 40 trillion won. It was an unprecedented gamble where a single company bet a significant portion of the national annual budget for a single technological transition. What would have happened if POSCO had avoided this risky challenge and settled for existing blast furnaces? The only future would have been a miserable one, slowly dying while paying hundreds of billions of won in carbon taxes every year. Faced with the cold market law that a giant that does not change will perish, they chose an unavoidable head-on breakthrough. If they succeed, they will become the one and only hegemon, churning out carbon-free steel with the cheapest raw materials in the world. However, if they fail, it would be a perilous tightrope walk where a 40 -trillion-won debt mountain could swallow the entire group. Fifty years ago, Honorary Chairman Park Tae-joon shouted to his predecessors on a desolate sandy field. He told them that if this steel mill, built with the blood of our ancestors, were to fail, they should all drown themselves in the Yeongil Bay. But today, half a century later, POSCO’s successors no longer need the resolve to drown in the sea. It was the most desperate line of defense to protect the heart of South Korea's export economy and our families' livelihoods. However, to turn this bold dream into reality, there is one last massive mountain left to climb. It is the problem of the supply chain for electricity and hydrogen, which are needed in unimaginable amounts to produce true green steel. Where on earth can we procure this immense amount of energy? However, HyREX is not a magic key that promises only a bright future. There are still 25 long years left until the complete transition in 2050. In that arduous journey, the walls of reality we must overcome are much higher than we think. For one, there are still too many unknown territories that remain unexplored technologically. When powdered iron ore meets high-temperature hydrogen gas, it is prone to a fatal sticking phenomenon where the particles fuse together. Finely controlling gas flow and temperature to prevent the inside of the reactor from hardening is an extremely difficult engineering challenge. Beyond the laboratory scale, not a single error can be tolerated in the commercialization stage where millions of tons are produced continuously. The second challenge is the supply network for clean hydrogen and electricity. For HyREX to fully replace fossil fuels, millions of tons of green hydrogen are needed every year . However, Korea lacks sufficient renewable energy, so most of the required hydrogen must be imported at a high cost. On top of that, there is an absolute shortage of carbon-free power grids to operate large electric furnaces and reduction facilities 24/7. If we melt ore with electricity from coal-fired plants, the very claim of " green steel" loses its meaning. The third challenge is the brutal global price competition. Overseas competitors not yet subject to carbon regulations are flooding the market with low-priced steel made using cheap coal. There is a "valley of death" where companies must endure massive losses before expensive eco-friendly steel gets its fair market value. The astronomical investment cost , reaching 40 trillion won, is also a heavy burden for a single company to bear alone. Ultimately, this bold experiment goes far beyond a mere technological gamble by POSCO. It is a massive survival game that can only be completed when national energy policies and stable power grid infrastructure align. Can we truly solve these daunting tasks and complete this miracle?

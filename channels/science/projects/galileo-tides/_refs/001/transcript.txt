The person reading a letter beyond the iron grate can never step outside that door for the rest of her life. She entered this convent at thirteen and has never stepped outside the lattice since. The letters only pass through the grate that no person can cross. The recipient of the letters is currently in Rome. He is the father who became the most famous name in Europe by writing a book about what he saw through a telescope. He was Galileo. He is now standing in court for the reason that he said the Earth rotates. Of the letters the two exchanged, only those written by the daughter remain today. There is not a single reply from the father. The Roman court where the father was summoned had one old rule. They do not ask whether it is wrong. They only ask if you have signed your name here. Whether or not the Earth rotates was outside their concern. It only took a moment to write a name on the blank line at the bottom, but it took Rome 359 years to open its mouth again. Today, I will tell the story of a trial that took place over a signature on a piece of paper, not the universe, and the last line that remained empty until the end. Before we reach that blank line, we must look at a sentence we believe we know about this trial. It is said that as he left the courtroom after the trial ended, he stomped his foot and muttered under his breath. "And yet it moves." This phrase is nowhere in the trial records. It is not in the interrogation records, the verdict, or the abjuration he read while kneeling. Even in the biography compiled by his disciple who stayed by his side until his final years, this sentence never appears. It first appeared in print in 1757 in an Italian guidebook published in London. It was 124 years after the trial ended. The book states that Galileo was imprisoned for six years and tortured, neither of which is true. Since then, this one sentence has passed from hand to hand, completing a picture. A dungeon, chains , torture devices, and an old man who would not bend the truth to the end. This image is so vivid that no one became curious about the actual words exchanged in that room. What that court held onto for over two months was neither the sun nor the Earth. It was a single piece of paper. A piece of paper written 17 years earlier, upon which no one had signed their name. We must go to the day that paper was created. It is Rome in February 1616. That winter, a theological advisory group began reviewing the claim that the sun stands still in the center of the universe, and a report was submitted on February 24th. "Philosophically foolish and contrary to dogma." That was the conclusion in those two lines. The next day, Pope Paul V held a meeting and issued instructions to Cardinal Bellarmine. He was told to summon Galileo and urge him to abandon his theory. The instructions came with specific conditions. If he refused, a strict prohibition was to be issued, and if he still persisted, he was to be imprisoned; three steps were pre-determined. First, persuasion; if refused, a formal order; if that failed , imprisonment. The following day, February 26, Galileo visited Bellarmine’s residence. At that time, Bellarmine was 73, and Galileo had just turned 51. The only surviving record of what happened in that room is a single document in the Roman archives, which states the following. Galileo obediently accepted the admonition. However, the very next line states that Segizzi, the Commissary of the Inquisition, read out a strict prohibition. That he must not advocate, defend, or teach it in any way. The order of events had been reversed. The prohibition was issued even though he had not refused. Furthermore, that document had no signatures. Neither the Commissary who read it, the witnesses who stood by, nor Galileo himself signed the paper. On March 3, Bellarmine reported the day’s events at the Inquisition meeting. He mentioned that Galileo had accepted the admonition, but said nothing about a prohibition. The person present at the reading of the order did not mention that such an order had been given. That document was then filed away in the archives and forgotten. No one looked at it again, and few even remembered that such an order existed. Then, 17 years later, this unsigned paper would bring a man before the court. Galileo also had a document of his own. When rumors of his reprimand began circulating in Rome that spring, he visited Bellarmine on May 26 to obtain a document. It was a certificate he kept to silence the rumors. It bore Bellarmine’s own handwritten signature. And the text written there differed from the official record. Do not advocate or defend that theory. That was all it said. It did not include the phrase "do not teach" or " in any way." Two documents created within three months were telling different stories. One, unsigned, sat in the Roman archives, while the other, signed, sat in an individual’s drawer . It was in the gap between these two documents that a trial would later take place. That autumn, Galileo’s eldest daughter took her vows at the cloistered convent in Arcetri and received a new name. Maria Celeste, a name combining the Holy Mother’s name with the heavens her father gazed upon every night. She prepared medicine at the convent for her father and mended the shirts he sent, returning them through the grate. Within a few years, the atmosphere in Rome changed drastically. In 1623, Cardinal Maffeo Barberini became Pope Urban VIII. He had been Galileo’s patron for over a decade and had even written a poem about his books. Once he became Pope, he invited Galileo for long, private conversations six times. In doing so, he set one condition. Treat that theory as a hypothesis, not as fact. Present it only as one of many ways to calculate the heavens. In 1632, believing he had met those conditions, Galileo published a book. The title was "Dialogue." It was structured as a discussion between three people over four days regarding the two world systems. The book had been fully approved by censors in both Rome and Florence. However, just months after its release, the Pope exploded with rage. It is said he kept repeating that he had been deceived. When the ambassador asked what the problem was, Galileo said he would know and offered no further explanation. What exactly provoked him so much is still not entirely certain. However, there is one strong suspicion. The most foolish character in the book was named Simplicio, and the argument he made at the end happened to be what the Pope had told Galileo himself. His own argument had become the dialogue of a fool. Before the summons was issued, Rome was already digging through their archives. What could they use to charge this man? And so, the document that had been dormant since February 1616 was brought back out into the world. The summons was written only after that paper was unearthed. In October 1632, an inquisitor from Florence arrived at Galileo’s home with it. Galileo was sixty-eight at the time and in poor health. He requested the trial be held in Florence, but he was denied. He set out in mid-winter, a journey that took twenty-four days, and was held up at the border for a plague quarantine. He was carried in a litter provided by the Grand Duke. He arrived in Rome on February 13, 1633. According to regulations, he should have been jailed upon the start of the trial, but he stayed at the Tuscan embassy. The ambassador and his wife treated him like a guest. The first interrogation began on April 12. The inquisitor in charge, Macolani, was more of a practical official who designed fortresses and managed defense budgets than a theologian. However, the questions did not start with the universe. What were you told in 1616? The interrogation began right there. Galileo replied that he had been reprimanded, and then pulled a piece of paper from his pocket. It was the certificate with Bellarmine's signed confirmation, which he had kept for 17 years. In that moment, the prosecution faltered. Because the paper presented by the individual had a name, while the paper provided by the court did not. If they pushed forward with a document lacking a signature, the procedural foundations of the trial would crumble before the trial itself. So, what happened next did not take place inside the courtroom. It was two weeks after the first interrogation had begun. Maculani, with the consent of his colleagues, went directly to the room where Galileo was staying. And he quietly made an offer. If you admit that your arguments in the book went too far, I will ensure you avoid a heavy sentence. I will not drag out the trial, either. Instead of arguing in court, they chose to settle the price privately in his room. This visit is recorded in a letter Maculani sent to Cardinal Barberini a few days later. On April 30th, in the second interrogation , Galileo said he had read his book again after a long time. He admitted that it looked like someone else's writing and that he seemed to have stated the opposing view more strongly than intended. He added that his desire to leave a mark had blinded him. On May 10th, in the third interrogation, he submitted a defense statement. That he was old and his illness was deep. And that, apart from Bellarmine's certificate, he had no way of knowing what he had promised. The only weapon he could pull out in court was that single sheet of paper to the end. On June 16th, the Pope decided on the final policy. Inform him of torture before interrogation, force a retraction, ban his recent book; the results of the final interrogation were already documented five days before it was held. June 21st, the fourth interrogation. The proceeding was called a rigorous examination, and it was here that the threat of torture was delivered. It was the stage where the possibility of torture was verbally announced. The torture was not carried out. But the announcement was made. On the spot, Galileo replied that he did not believe in that theory. They didn't ask further. The necessary answer was recorded in the transcript, and that was the end of it. Without laying a hand on him, the document was completed . On the morning of June 22, 1633, at the Dominican monastery of Minerva in Rome, Galileo knelt before the cardinals in the white robes of a penitent. He was sixty-nine years old. Two months and ten days after the first interrogation, the verdict was read aloud before him. The charge read aloud was not heresy. It was a strong suspicion of heresy. Under Roman law, the two were in different categories. If confirmed as a heretic, one couldn't know how severe the punishment would be , but remaining at the level of suspicion allowed for a public recantation. That one margin determined the sentence and, at the same time, opened an escape route. There was no excommunication, and the sentence given was not death, but house arrest. That space left open, without escalating the charges, was actually a place kept empty to secure his signature. As the reading concluded, the recantation text was placed before him. It is said that he requested two sentences be removed from that draft. The sentence stating that he was not a good believer. And the sentence claiming he intended to deceive others when publishing his book . Those two lines were actually removed . He read the rest. I abandon, curse, and detest the idea that the Earth moves. I will never speak of such things again. And on the empty line at the bottom, he pressed his name in ink. I, Galileo Galilei, have recanted as written above with my own hand. The inquisitor wasn't after that sentence. It was the one line for his name below it. Throughout those two months, they never once argued in that room whether the Earth actually moves or not. The statement that his theory was wrong was already printed in the recantation, and all that remained was to get his signature below it. The judgment read that day contained the names of ten cardinals. However, there were only seven handwritten signatures below. Three spaces were left empty. One of them was the place for Francesco Barberini, the Pope's nephew. The records do not state why it was left empty. On June 24th, he was moved to the Medici Villa, and a trip to Siena was permitted. He spent five months at the residence of the Archbishop of Siena. Though he was released from Rome , he was not yet allowed to return home . The verdict came with one quiet clause. He was to recite seven penitential psalms once a week for three years; it was a private task done alone, not in front of others, and there was no way to verify if he kept it. While staying in Siena, a letter arrived. It was a letter from his daughter saying she would recite those penitential psalms on his behalf. It wasn't something she was asked to do. The words were tucked in briefly between talk of the grape harvest and household chores. In December of that year, he returned to his home in Arcetri and entered house arrest. It was right next to the wall of the convent where his daughter stayed. Then , on April 2nd, Maria Celeste passed away from dysentery. She was 33 years old. It was only four months after his return, a time when the two had been seeing each other daily across the convent grate. That verdict had already been circulated to inquisitors in every region. It was meant to be used as an example. However, what held him back longer was not what was written in that document. After that, any new work he wrote could not receive permission to be printed in Italy. So, his final book in 1638 had its manuscript smuggled across the border to be published in the Netherlands. It was a book that dealt with how objects fall and how much they can withstand, later called the starting point of modern physics. By the time he handed over the manuscript, he could barely see, so his disciples took dictation. And in January 1642, he closed his eyes in that house in Arcetri. It was a spot just a few steps away from the wall where his daughter was buried. Later, 124 letters from his daughter were found among his belongings. They covered ten years, from 1623 to 1633. However, not a single sheet of paper going in the opposite direction was ever found. Records do not say who destroyed them or when, once again. Even after he closed his eyes, that paper remained alive in the Roman archives. The first thing to move was not the document, but his grave. He could not be buried in the main hall of the cathedral and was placed in a small room under the bell tower, until 1737. His remains were moved inside the Basilica of Santa Croce. It was ninety-five years later. In 1741, his complete works were granted permission for publication on the condition that annotations were added. In 1758, the clause that collectively banned heliocentrism disappeared. However, his "Dialogue" remained on the list as an individually designated forbidden book. The book bearing his name was held captive by the sin of his name. In 1822 , the publication of books describing heliocentrism as a fact was permitted in Rome. And when you open the index of forbidden books in 1835, his book is gone. There was no announcement, no explanation, and no correction. The line was simply missing when the next edition was printed. The book was quietly cleared that way, but the verdict itself remained untouched. The verdict, the recantation, and the single line of his signature below remained exactly as they were. It took more time for Rome to even bring up that verdict. In 1979, the Pope proposed that this case be re-examined. A committee was actually formed two years later, and it took another eleven years for the results to come out. It was October 31, 1992, 359 years after the day Galileo knelt and signed his name. However, the verdict was not overturned on that spot. It has not been overturned even to this day. There was no word of apology, and no one was identified as having done anything wrong. All that emerged was one phrase. It meant a "tragic mutual misunderstanding," implying that both sides failed to properly understand each other. Furthermore, it was also claimed that this incident was a myth created after the Enlightenment and was far from what actually happened. The issue was not who delivered the verdict , but the way it was read. There was a set order for obtaining signatures. When to call, what to ask, what to announce, and even where to have them sign were all mapped out. However, the order for retracting those names was not mapped out anywhere. That is why one side ended in two months, while the other took over 300 years. All a man needed to sign his name was a pen and a place to kneel. That day in Minerva's room, both were ready. The single line of his signature did not stop the Earth . What stopped was one man's voice, but what had been silent for far longer was the mouth of those who made him kneel. If a court prioritizes the lawbreaker over the one who said it was wrong, then who was it so meticulously trying to protect? 124 letters sent from inside those bars remain, but not a single one sent in has survived. It is said that his daughter even transcribed her father's manuscripts for him. So, some of the father's sentences we read today exist only in his daughter's handwriting. The paper he knelt to read is also still in Rome. At the very bottom, a single name, pressed in by hand. And below that, for 359 years, until Rome finally spoke, no one added another name. It remained an empty line .

Have you ever waited for takeoff in a window seat and noticed large numbers painted on the runway surface? Incheon International Airport has four runways. On their surfaces, numbers like 15, 16, 33, and 34 are painted in large white letters. However, if you look closely, there is something strange. At one end of a single runway, 15 is written, while 33 is written at the opposite end . One runway has two different numbers. And since the four runways lie side by side, looking at the same end reveals that some are marked 15 and others 16. There is also a letter attached next to each number. What could these numbers be telling us? And who decided these numbers, and based on what? Following these numbers to the end leads us all the way to how a multi-hundred-ton airplane takes flight. When looking at these numbers, it’s easy to think of length first. Like assuming 33 means the runway is about 3.3 km long. Or perhaps it could be a serial number indicating which order the runway was built. Neither is correct. These numbers represent direction. It is the direction the airplane faces when it runs along that runway. On a compass, north is 0 degrees, and the circle is divided into 360 degrees clockwise. East is 90 degrees, south is 180 degrees, and west is 270 degrees. The number on the ground is the direction the airplane is facing, measured in these degrees, divided by 10 and rounded. 33 represents roughly 330 degrees, which is northwest, tilted slightly west from north. Then, at which end of the runway would 33 be written? To run while facing northwest, you must start from the opposite end, which is the southeast. Therefore, 33 is painted on the ground at the southeast end, right in front of the airplanes starting from that point. If you run the same runway in the opposite direction, you start from the northwest end and face southeast. That is about 150 degrees, the exact opposite of 330 degrees. That is why 15 is painted on the ground at the northwest end. This is the reason why one runway has two names. If you start from one end it's 33, and if you start from the opposite end it's 15. That is why the two numbers on one runway always differ by 18. Because the exact opposite direction is 180 degrees, and dividing that by 10 gives 18. However, the four runways at Incheon Airport are all oriented in the same direction. When viewed from the sky, the four lines look as straight as if drawn with a ruler. If you measure the direction from the northwest end with a compass, all four are around 153 degrees, which becomes 15 when divided by 10 and rounded. Going by direction alone, all four runways should have 15 written on the ground at the northwest end. When two runways with the same number lie side by side, you can distinguish them like twins by calling them left and right. The standard is the view of the pilot starting from that end. If you stand at the northwest end and look southeast, the left is northeast and the right is southwest. Therefore, the left strip is 15 Left, and the right strip is 15 Right. The floor is also painted with the number along with the English letter L for left or R for right. When an air traffic controller radios to go to 15 Left, the pilot won't get confused. However, with four strips, "left" and "right" are not enough to identify them. So, according to international rules, the four strips are divided into two pairs side by side . One pair keeps the number 15, while the other pair borrows the adjacent number 16. This is because 153 degrees is closer to 16 than to 14. At Incheon, the first two strips built are 15, and the later two are 16. 15 Left, 15 Right , 16 Left, 16 Right. This is how the four strips came to have different names. The southeast end is also divided into 33 and 34 in the same way. At the opposite end, since the viewing direction is reversed, left and right are also swapped. 16 is not a number assigned because of a different direction. It is a number borrowed to avoid confusion. Then why were these four strips laid out in the direction connecting northwest and southeast? To find the answer, you have to look at a plane actually running on this runway. It is because the orientation of the runway depends on what a plane needs when taking off and landing. Think about a plane taking off. At the end of the runway, the plane revs up its engines loudly and rushes forward. It accelerates so fast that you're pushed back into your seat, and at some point, the nose lifts and the wheels leave the ground. How much runway is needed for this takeoff? The Boeing 747-400, a large airliner, requires a 3,270-meter runway to take off. Large airliners usually need to reach around 300 km/h to take off. Including the distance to reach that speed and the margin to stop in an emergency, more than 3 km is required. Why does it have to be so fast? It is because of the wings. Wings only lift the plane when air hits them very quickly. The runway is 3 to 4 km long because it needs that distance to reach the speed where the wings can lift the plane. The third runway built at Incheon Airport in 2008 is 4 km long . According to Incheon International Airport Corporation, they extended the length starting from the 747-400's 3,270 m. This is because when it is hot , the air becomes thinner, requiring the same plane to run longer to take off. They factored in future global warming in advance. Then how do wings lift hundreds of tons using air? There is a common explanation you may have heard. Wings are convex on top and flat on the bottom. So, the air on top has a longer path to travel. For the air separated above and below to meet at the wing's trailing edge at the same time, the air on top must move faster. As air flows faster, its pressure decreases. This explains why there is less downward pressure on top and a stronger upward push from below. You may have heard this in school or on TV at least once. It is true that airplane wings are convex and the air on top moves faster. What is incorrect is the reason that the air must speed up to meet at the same time. In 2012, an engineering professor at the University of Cambridge released an experimental video. The video shows smoke being released in short pulses past a wing in a wind tunnel. When pausing the video, the top stream of smoke had already cleared the trailing edge while the bottom stream was still under the wing. The top air didn't meet the bottom air at the same time; it arrived much earlier. The slightly longer path on top cannot account for such a high speed difference. Also, planes can fly even if their wings aren't convex. The wings of paper airplanes are equally flat on both sides. Some stunt planes can even fly upside down. This means the convex shape is not the sole reason for lift. Then, what makes the wing lift? You might have tried sticking your palm out of a moving car window. When you hold your palm flat to the ground, nothing much happens. But if you tilt your fingertips up slightly, your hand is pushed upward. Wings do the same thing. A wing travels through the air with its front tilted slightly upward. Air coming from the front splits above and below the leading edge of the wing. The air going below hits the slanted underside and is deflected downward. The air going above flows along the top surface and curves downward at the trailing edge. Both the air that went above and below is ultimately directed downward. As the wing pushes the air downward, the wing itself is pushed in the opposite direction: upward. At this point, if you measure the pressure, it is lower on top and higher on the bottom. Since the upward push from below is stronger, the wing moves upward. The faster speed of the top air and the lower pressure on top are both results of the wing forcing the air downward. Therefore, what you learned in school is not entirely wrong. The only incorrect part is the reason about meeting at the same time. Pushing air downward and the difference in pressure are just different ways of viewing the same phenomenon. The convex wing shape simply makes this process more efficient. It is because the air flowing over the upper surface bends downward more sharply at the trailing edge. This is also the reason why planes lift their nose right before taking off. Tilting the nose up increases the angle of the wings, pushing more air downward. However, if it tilts too much, the air cannot flow along the upper surface and detaches. Then, the lifting force suddenly disappears. If you hold your palm out the window and tilt it straight up, it only gets pushed back, not lifted up. The force gained by pushing air downward increases as the air strikes the wing faster. Interestingly, this force grows much faster than the increase in speed. As speed increases, the wings encounter more air in the same amount of time. The strength used to push that air downward also increases. Since both increase together , when the speed doubles, the lifting force becomes four times greater. If the speed triples, the force increases ninefold. Conversely, if the speed is halved, the lifting force drops to one-quarter. That is why heavier planes need to run faster. The 747-8, the largest of the 747 family, weighs 448 tons when fully loaded with cargo and passengers. This aircraft must reach about 320 km/h to take off safely. That is about three times faster than a car traveling on a highway. This required takeoff speed is also the key to determining the direction of Incheon's runways. However, runway length is limited, so they cannot just run indefinitely. So, when taking off and landing, the wing shape is changed to stay aloft at slower speeds. If you look from a window seat, you'll see panels sliding out from the back of the wing and bending downward. Panels also emerge from the front of the wing. The wings become wider and more curved. This allows them to deflect more air downward even at slower speeds. However , the more these panels are extended, the more they catch the air and drag the plane. That is why they are only partially extended during takeoff, when speed is needed, and fully extended during landing, when speed must be reduced. Now, let's return to the four runways at Incheon Airport. The key is that speed of 300 km/h needed for the plane to lift off, as we saw earlier. The 300 km/h mentioned so far is the speed of the plane on the ground on a windless day. But what the wings actually feel is not the ground speed, but the speed of the air hitting them. Without wind, the two are the same, but they differ when the wind blows. Think about when you ride a bicycle. When riding against the wind, the wind hitting your face is much stronger, even at the same speed. When riding with the wind, you barely feel any wind on your face. It is the same for wings. With a headwind, the wings hit sufficiently fast air even without moving as quickly over the ground. Even for a plane that needs to reach 300 km/ h to take off, if a 30 km/h headwind is blowing, it only needs to reach 270 km/ h on the ground. This difference is larger than you might think. This time, it's not about the wings, but about the distance covered on the ground. Calculated simply, a car also needs to cover about four times the distance to reach 100 km/h compared to reaching 50 km/h. Even if the required speed decreases just a little, the distance needed to run decreases much more significantly. Like the example just now, if a headwind of 10%of the takeoff speed blows, the speed needed on the ground decreases by 10%. Then, the distance required to run shortens by about 19%. The speed decreased by 10%, but the distance decreased by about twice that amount. Conversely, if you run with the same intensity of wind at your back, the distance increases by about 21%. It is the same when landing. Landing while facing the wind slows your ground speed, allowing you to stop in a shorter distance. That is why airports watch the wind every day. Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport regulations require aircraft to take off and land facing as close to the wind as possible when it exceeds 9 km/h. This practice of changing direction is clearly visible in the records of nearby Gimpo Airport. Gimpo Airport also has runways running northwest and southeast. When a northwesterly wind blows, planes take off facing northwest toward Gimpo, and when a southeasterly wind blows, they face southeast toward Seoul. Throughout 2022, two-thirds of the flights at Gimpo Airport took off and landed facing the Gimpo side. This is because strong northwesterly winds often blow in Gimpo. Incheon Airport is the same. The same runway is called "33" on some days and "15" on others. The air traffic controller looks at the day's wind and chooses which direction the plane should face for takeoff and landing. If they pick the end based on the wind every day, did they also check the wind when they first laid the runway? Once a runway is laid, its direction cannot be changed. That is why, when building an airport, they first collect data on what winds have blown in that area. International standards require looking at wind records measured at least eight times a day for at least five years. What they are looking for is the direction that receives the least amount of crosswind. Crosswinds do not help like headwinds do. They push the airplane sideways, causing it to drift from the center of the runway. If you align the runway with the direction the wind blows most frequently, taking off and landing toward whichever end the wind comes from becomes a headwind. What kind of wind blows at the Incheon Airport site? If you count by frequency alone, you also frequently see winds coming from the northeast. For the Incheon runway connecting the northwest and southeast, this is a crosswind. However, this wind is generally weak. A weak wind, even when blowing from the side, cannot push a large airplane very far. According to international standards, a runway used by large passenger aircraft is only considered unusable if crosswinds exceed 37 km/h. The northeasterly winds at Incheon average just over 10 km/h, far below this threshold. There is a different wind that blows frequently and with greater intensity. It is the northwesterly wind that blows in winter . The stronger the wind, the more it tends to blow parallel to the runway. If you look only at winds exceeding 30 km/h, 65%blow from the northwest or southeast, which is the direction the runways are laid out. Based on 10 years of wind data at Incheon Airport, the difference between the ideal direction to minimize crosswinds and the actual runway direction is only 5 degrees. Gimpo Airport, which we looked at earlier, also has a 5-degree difference . Incheon International Airport Corporation officials also explain that they analyze 10 years of wind data to build runways in the direction where the wind blows most frequently. Therefore, the four runways at Incheon Airport are positioned so that aircraft can take off and land while facing the winter northwesterly winds head-on. Not all airports are aligned with the wind in this way. The main runway at Jeju Airport is oriented toward the northeast and southwest. However, the most frequent wind at Jeju Airport comes from the northwest. This is a crosswind that hits the runway at an almost right angle. Why was it placed out of alignment with the wind? Airport officials explain that it was unavoidable due to the island's terrain . As a volcanic island, Jeju's land slopes upward from the coast toward Hallasan Mountain. The direction connecting the northwest and southeast follows this upward slope. Jeju Airport does have one runway laid out in this direction. However, it is rarely used because it is on an incline, is short in length, and its southeastern end is near a residential area. Most aircraft use the main runway, which is oriented northeast to southwest, crossing this slope. There is one more wind-related problem in the skies over Jeju. It is wind that suddenly changes in both direction and intensity. This wind cannot be avoided, no matter which direction the runway is built. As of 2026, such wind warnings have been issued 1,515 times over the past five years. This is the highest number among all airports in the country. On July 12 , 2026, due to a typhoon, both sudden wind-shift warnings and strong wind warnings were issued, leading to the cancellation of 105 flights by that afternoon. Three flights arriving from overseas were unable to land and had to turn back. Returning to Incheon, the numbers on the runway surface indicate the direction determined by analyzing these wind patterns. However, these numbers can sometimes change even without repaving the runway. The numbers on the surface represent the direction based on a compass. But the north indicated by a compass needle is not the same as true north on a map. This is because the Earth is a giant magnet, and magnetic north is in a different location than map north. In Korea, magnetic north is about 9 degrees west of map north, and this gap widens slightly every year. So, if you measure with a compass, the reading is about 9 degrees larger than on a map. The reason I mentioned earlier that Incheon's runway is about 153 degrees is because that's the compass measurement; on a map, it's about 144 degrees. Even if the runway stays in place, the angle measured by a compass changes slightly each year. Eventually, when the value crosses the rounding threshold to a different number, the floor markings must be repainted. Incheon still has about one degree left before reaching that boundary. In January 2011, Tampa Airport in the U.S. closed its main runway until January 13th to erase the number 18 and paint 19 in its place. They also replaced over 140 airport signs. Airports in the UK and Ireland have also repainted their runway numbers for the same reason. The runway itself did not move even a single centimeter. What moved was the compass needle. Now, let's wrap things up. The question I asked at the beginning was what the numbers 15, 16, 33, and 34 on the Incheon Airport runway mean. These numbers represent direction. They indicate the direction the plane is facing while running down the runway, expressed as a compass angle. That is why 15 and 33 are both written at either end of a single runway. The numbers 16 and 34 aren't there because the direction is different. Since there are parallel runways, they borrowed the adjacent numbers to avoid confusion. This orientation was determined by the airport builders based on local wind data. The reason they checked the wind is because of the wings. Wings push the oncoming air downward, and the faster that air is, the more lift they generate. It makes the air hitting the wings move faster. That’s why planes take off and land facing the wind, and runways are positioned to face the strongest local winds. Today, Incheon Airport controllers are still checking the wind to choose which direction planes should face for takeoff and landing. Next time you sit by a window on a plane, try to look for the numbers on the runway floor. Also, check which way the plane is facing, and notice where the wind is coming from that day.

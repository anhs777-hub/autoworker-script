Here in Jamsil, Seoul, there is a lake that used to be the Han River. It’s Seokchon Lake. But the water in this lake isn't just sitting still. It leaks out through the bottom every day, and we replenish it with as much water pumped from the Han River. An average of 2,000 tons drains out through the bottom daily. This site was originally the main stream of the Han River. It was called the Songpa River, and the Songpa Ferry from the Joseon Dynasty was right here. Jamsil was an island on the other side of that river. But today , that river water is pretending to be a calm lake beneath the Lotte World amusement rides. The problem with this lake can be summarized in a single line . It's a container with a leaking bottom. Everything that follows starts from this point. Jamsil was originally an island. It was a sandy island wedged between two branches of the Han River. The southern waterway was the main branch, the Songpa River, and the northern one was the Saetgang. The island was full of mulberry trees, so it was named "Jamsil," meaning a place for sericulture. If you took a boat from the Songpa Ferry in the south, you would pass this island to cross to the north bank. Then, during the Great Flood of 1925, the northern Saetgang widened significantly, and it is said that water began to flow more toward the north from then on. The southern Songpa River became that much weaker. So, the place where Lotte World stands now was once the river and ferry landing where people in the Joseon Dynasty traveled by boat. In April 1971 , the Seoul Metropolitan Government began construction to turn Jamsil from an island into land. It was the reclamation of public Han River land. They widened the northern waterway and blocked the southern waterway, the Songpa River. They essentially wiped out a whole river and filled the space with soil. However, they didn't fill in the entire southern waterway. They left a portion behind, which is what we now call Seokchon Lake. A segment of the cut-off river became the lake. Later, as Songpa-daero Road passed through the middle, it was split into an East Lake and a West Lake, which are connected by a small waterway under a bridge. But this lake had problems from the very moment it was born. Since it was originally a riverbed, the bottom isn't made of clay, but sand. It's silty sand that drains water easily. Moreover, the surface of the lake is up to 5.1 meters higher than the surface of the Han River. Water at a higher level naturally seeks to flow to a lower level. So, the lake water slowly seeps through the sandy bottom and leaks underground toward the Han River. An average of 2,000 tons per day. It's an ironic situation. They blocked the river to make a lake, but the lake is trying to return to the river. You might ask, why not just waterproof the bottom? That isn't possible either. The area of this lake exceeds 210,000 square meters. That's the size of 30 soccer fields. Draining all the water and digging up the entire bottom to lay a waterproof membrane would essentially be destroying the lake and rebuilding it from scratch. And the bottom of this lake is connected to the surrounding underground groundwater. If you block it off completely, the flow of the surrounding groundwater changes entirely. So, the Seoul Metropolitan Government reversed its thinking. Let's not stop the leaking. Instead, let's add as much as leaks out. This is how the method of drawing water from the Han River to maintain the lake's water level began. The first is pipes. We buried pipes to draw water from the Han River to the lake, and when the water level drops, we pump up Han River water to refill it. The full water level is around 5 meters. This water fills the lake, leaks through the bottom, passes through the ground, and returns to the Han River. It comes out of the Han River and returns to the Han River. They didn't eliminate the leakage; they gave it a path. In the 1980s, the lake became a park with walking paths and green spaces, and in 1989, Lotte World Magic Island was built on the western lake. The lake became the front yard of the amusement park, but the bottom remained sand. However, this balance was once severely broken. Between October 2011 and October 2013, the lake's water level dropped noticeably. The water level, which had been 4.68 meters on average annually, fell to 4.17 meters. Even though they kept pouring water in. The rate at which it was leaking became faster than the rate at which they were pouring it in. With the lake drying up in the middle of Jamsil, the neighborhood was in an uproar. Combined with road cave-ins nearby, rumors spread that the ground was sinking because the lake water was draining out. The Seoul Metropolitan Government commissioned the Korea Rural Community Corporation to investigate the cause and announced the results in August 2015. At that time, subway line 9 construction, Lotte World Tower construction, and eight large building projects were all underway around the lake. Construction that digs deep into the ground requires pumping out groundwater. As the surrounding groundwater level dropped, the lake water was sucked out toward those sites more quickly. The lake's natural drainage overlapped with the drainage from the construction sites. Analyzing the isotopes of the water showed that the lake water and the water from the construction sites were similar, and the evidence was that during the construction period, the underground water flow had shifted toward the construction sites. The second is measurement. At this time, the biggest concern was not the lake water, but the surrounding ground. The investigation concluded that the impact of the water level drop on the surrounding ground was minimal and had no direct connection to the nearby road sinkholes . Instead, the Seoul Metropolitan Government continues to measure the groundwater level around the lake. They also predicted that once the large-scale construction was finished, the outflow of water would decrease by 20 to 30 percent. In fact, it was confirmed through measurement that the outflow volume did decrease. It’s not about stopping the water from leaking, but continuously measuring it and adjusting the amount being added. The third is the amount of water. Still, they have to pour in Han River water every year. In 2017 alone, 790,000 tons of Han River water flowed into Seokchon Lake. The lake where hundreds of thousands walk under cherry blossoms swallows this much Han River water every year. Beneath the seemingly calm surface, the water is still flowing today. Lake water leaks into the Han River, and Han River water flows into the lake; only the direction differs from when it was a river, but the water still moves. Shall we return to our first question? What did they do about a lake with a leaking bottom? Seoul did not block this lake. They added as much Han River water as was lost, measured the surrounding ground, and searched for the cause whenever the flow fluctuated. They didn't turn the river into a lake, but left it as a very slowly flowing river. This is how Seokchon Lake in Seoul was born.
